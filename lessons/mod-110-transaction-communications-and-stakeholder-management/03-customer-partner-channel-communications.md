# Customer, Partner, and Channel Communications

## Why this matters

The single largest source of realised deal-value erosion post-close is **customer churn attributable to the transaction** — accounts that would have renewed under the standalone company that do not renew under the acquired company. The pattern is well-documented in the post-merger integration literature: absent a specific customer-retention workstream, mid-market deals commonly lose 3–8% of ARR in the twelve months post-close beyond the standalone-attrition baseline, and specific concentrated-account deals (where the top 10 customers represent >40% of ARR) can lose double that. The revenue that leaves through this channel is uninsurable — no R&W policy covers customer churn — and it lands as a specific miss against the acquirer's synergy model, often in the first earnings-cycle post-close where it is highly visible to the acquirer's own investors.

The workstream that prevents this is not "the CS team sends an email." It is a *specific pre-close-designed, sequenced, and sponsor-driven customer-communications programme* that covers change-of-control-clause diligence (which contracts require the customer's consent to close, which merely require notice, which have specific termination-for-convenience rights), top-ARR retention calls (personally led by the CEO or the CRO to the top-20% ARR accounts within hours of announcement), partner tier-communications (sequenced by strategic importance), channel-reseller consent workflows (which channel-partner agreements have specific ownership-change consent requirements), integration-communication cadence (weekly / monthly rhythm during integration), and the specific reference-request timing (the customer references sitting in the buy-side data room and being called during exclusivity — which the customer must be pre-warned about).

This chapter installs that workstream. It is written for a CRO / VP-Customer-Success / SVP-Partnerships / VP-Channel who inherits the customer-communications workload at the moment the deal team briefs them (typically a specific 24–48 hours pre-announcement), but is designed by the CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev during the diligence period so that the workload arrives with a specific plan rather than a specific scramble.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal advice. Every commercial contract has specific language, specific governing-law overlays, and specific interpretation history. The change-of-control diligence workstream described below identifies patterns; the specific legal analysis of a specific contract is done by outside counsel.

## Change-of-control clause diligence — the pre-close workstream

The **change-of-control (COC) clause diligence** is the specific workstream that reviews every material commercial contract for language that triggers on the transaction. This is a specific pre-signing (and continuing pre-close) legal workstream, typically led by sell-side counsel with the sell-side commercial-contracts owner (typically a specific member of the GC's team, sometimes the head of legal operations or a specific senior counsel).

**The three categories of COC language.**

**Category 1 — consent required.** The specific contract requires the counterparty's *written consent* to a change-of-control transaction. Consent-required contracts are the most consequential — a specific contract on the counterparty's paper may include an unqualified consent right, which effectively gives the counterparty a specific negotiating leverage over the deal (or, in the extreme, the ability to terminate). The specific practitioner-defensive language on the sell-side is that consent-required clauses include a *"consent not to be unreasonably withheld, conditioned, or delayed"* qualifier — the specific qualifier that limits the counterparty's ability to weaponise the consent right.

**Category 2 — notice required.** The specific contract requires the target to *notify* the counterparty of the change of control, typically within a specific window (e.g., 30 days post-signing). Notice-required contracts are lower risk but must be tracked so that the specific notice is delivered on the specific timeline.

**Category 3 — termination on change of control.** The specific contract gives the counterparty the *right to terminate* on change of control, typically within a specific window post-notice (e.g., 90 days). Termination-right contracts are the highest-risk category for revenue continuity — even if the counterparty does not exercise, the specific commercial conversation is now open. Termination-right clauses often show up in *customer* contracts where the customer negotiated a specific "we want the option to leave if you get acquired by a competitor" provision, in *reseller* contracts, in *supplier* contracts, and in *lease / real-estate* contracts.

**The specific COC diligence deliverable.** The diligence workstream produces a specific spreadsheet or database with one row per material contract, covering: contract name, counterparty, contract-value (annual and total), COC-category (consent / notice / termination / none), specific clause text, specific timing requirement, specific defence-language (unreasonably-withheld qualifier or equivalent), and specific action-plan (who calls the counterparty, when, with what specific script). The deliverable is delivered to the SVP-Corp-Dev / GC pre-signing so that any specific "material" consent requirement can be either obtained pre-signing (typically preferred), addressed with a specific waiver-condition in the definitive agreement, or flagged as a specific closing-condition risk.

**Which contracts count as "material."** The specific threshold varies but a common practitioner pattern:
- All customer contracts above a specific ARR threshold (e.g., top 20 by ARR, or all contracts above $250K ARR).
- All supplier contracts above a specific spend threshold (e.g., top 10 by spend).
- All real-estate leases.
- All channel-partner / reseller agreements.
- All licensing / IP-in / open-source-inbound contracts of specific significance.
- All specific regulated contracts (government contracts, healthcare BAAs, specific data-processing agreements).

**The specific pattern for change-of-control in a stock deal vs. asset deal.** In a stock deal (the buyer acquires the target's stock), the target entity survives and continues to be a party to its contracts — a specific change-of-control clause triggers based on whether the specific stock-transfer meets the specific triggering condition (varies by contract, but typically any change in the majority beneficial ownership). In an asset deal (the buyer acquires specific assets), each contract must be *assigned* from the target to the buyer, and any specific "no assignment without consent" clause becomes a specific consent workflow — this is why asset deals are often specifically more consent-heavy than stock deals, and why sell-side sellers with heavily consent-restricted contract portfolios often push for a stock-deal structure. Mod-103 covers the deal-structure decision; this module owns the *communications-workflow consequence* of the specific structure.

## Top-ARR customer retention calls — the specific sequencing

The **top-ARR retention calls** are the specific personal outreach to the highest-value customers, typically led by the CEO or the CRO. The sequencing is:

**Pre-announcement — the target list and script preparation.** The CRO (with the CFO) identifies the top-20% ARR customers (typically 10–50 accounts in a mid-size company; the top 5 in a highly-concentrated company). For each account, the specific pre-call preparation includes: current ARR, current health (green / yellow / red), renewal date, expansion opportunity, specific relationship contact (the person to call), any specific known concerns, any specific in-flight negotiation, any specific COC-clause implication.

**Announcement-day timing.** Top-ARR calls happen within 2–4 hours of the announcement — typically after the internal all-hands (so the sell-side is not making external commitments before the workforce is briefed) and before the end of business day in the customer's time zone. For a US-headquartered company with international customers, the specific sequencing is: Asia-Pacific customers called first thing on announcement morning (which is their afternoon), Europe customers called mid-morning US time (their afternoon), US customers called mid-day and afternoon.

**The specific call script.** A 15-minute call following a specific structure:

- Opening: "I wanted to reach out personally to let you know that today we announced [specific transaction]. Before you see it in the press, I wanted you to hear from me directly."
- Framing: "The transaction is [specific framing]. Here is what specifically will change for you as a customer: [specific commitments — product continuity, roadmap continuity, support continuity, account-team continuity, pricing continuity]. Here is what specifically will not change: [specific stability commitments]."
- Specific-benefit framing: "Here is what specifically may improve for you: [specific benefits — expanded capability, expanded footprint, expanded roadmap, specific integration with acquirer product]."
- Specific relationship commitment: "Your account team [name] will continue to be your primary contact. I am also available directly at [specific commitment on personal availability]."
- Specific request: "I want to understand any specific concerns you have. What questions do you have? What would you need from us to feel confident?"
- Follow-up: "Your account team will follow up with the specific customer-facing FAQ later today. There will be [specific commitment on ongoing communication cadence — weekly / monthly]. Thank you for your partnership."

**The specific decision — who makes the call.** The CEO calls the top-5 (or the specific hand-picked list where the CEO's personal relationship matters). The CRO or specific relationship-owning executive calls the next tier. The specific customer-success manager calls the next tier with a specific written communication going to everyone else. The specific pattern is that *every top-20% ARR customer receives a personal outreach the same day*, whether the outreach is a CEO call or a CS-manager call.

**The joint-call pattern (acquirer participation).** For strategic-buyer transactions where the acquirer's participation adds credibility (a specific commitment from the acquirer CEO on customer-continuity, a specific commitment on product-roadmap integration), the specific top-5 customer calls may be joint calls with the acquirer's CEO or the acquirer's specific relationship-owning executive. Joint calls are choreographed in advance with the acquirer's comms team.

**The specific escalation path for customer concerns.** A specific concern surfaced in a customer call — a specific renewal-at-risk question, a specific competitive-concern about the acquirer, a specific pricing-question, a specific integration-timing question — is escalated to a specific named executive with a specific response commitment on a specific timeline. The specific escalation-and-response discipline is what turns a specific concern into a specific retention.

## Partner communications sequenced by tier

**Strategic partners** — the specific relationships that materially affect the target's business (major technology integrations, major channel-partner relationships, major co-marketing agreements, major joint-venture partners) — are treated with a specific tier-1 treatment analogous to the top-ARR customer treatment. Personal outreach by the CEO or specific relationship-owning executive within hours of announcement, specific commitments on relationship-continuity, specific commitments on ongoing engagement, specific commitments on any specific in-flight joint work.

**Technology integration partners** — the specific SaaS or platform vendors whose product the target integrates with, or who integrate with the target's product — are treated with a specific tier-2 treatment: written outreach the same day or the next business day, specific commitments on integration-continuity, specific coordination on any specific joint customer-facing implications. Where the acquirer is a competitor (or a partner) of the technology partner, the specific outreach includes a specific commitment on ongoing partnership (or a specific acknowledgement that the relationship will be re-evaluated, with a specific timeline).

**Channel-reseller partners** — the specific partners who resell the target's product to their own customers — are treated with the specific tier-3 treatment: written outreach, specific commitments on channel-programme continuity, specific commitments on commission-treatment for in-flight deals, specific commitments on partner-portal / partner-training / partner-enablement continuity. Channel-reseller agreements often contain specific COC-consent clauses and specific ownership-change termination rights, so channel-reseller outreach coordinates with the specific COC diligence workstream.

**Referral / affiliate partners** — lower-touch treatment: an email from the head of partnerships with the specific FAQ, referring specific concerns to a specific contact. The specific volume of referral-partner outreach is often high (hundreds or thousands of specific partners), so the outreach is templated and pushed via the specific partner-portal / partner-comm channel.

**Government / regulatory / association partners** — specific outreach for specific relationships where the sell-side has an ongoing regulatory or advocacy relationship. Typically handled by the GC or by a specific government-affairs lead.

**The partner-communications cadence during integration.** From announcement through close, partners receive a specific bi-weekly or monthly update on integration progress, specific in-flight-workstream-status updates, and specific point-of-contact updates. Post-close, the integration-communications workstream continues under mod-111's cadence.

## Channel-reseller consent workflows — the specific mechanic

Channel-reseller agreements typically contain the specific COC-clause pattern that requires the most specific pre-close attention:

**Consent-required clauses.** A specific channel-reseller agreement may require the reseller's specific written consent to a change of control, particularly where the acquirer is a competitor of the reseller. The specific consent-workflow: outside counsel drafts a specific consent request, the sell-side partnerships lead delivers it to the specific reseller counterpart, the reseller reviews and (typically) grants consent — sometimes with specific negotiated conditions (e.g., a specific extension of the reseller agreement, a specific price protection, a specific exclusivity commitment).

**Termination-right clauses.** A specific channel-reseller agreement may give the reseller the specific right to terminate on change of control, typically within a specific window post-notice. Where the reseller has a specific strategic reason to terminate (e.g., the acquirer is a competitor), the sell-side may need to negotiate a specific renegotiation of the reseller agreement or accept the specific loss of the reseller.

**Consent aggregation and closing conditions.** For a target with a large channel-reseller footprint, the specific consent workflow may need to aggregate consents across many resellers. The definitive agreement typically includes a specific closing condition that a specific percentage of consent-required contracts (by revenue) have been obtained — typically 80–90% — with a specific mechanism for the remaining consents (post-close cure, specific indemnification, specific price adjustment).

**The reseller-side communications discipline.** The reseller-facing outreach is typically drafted jointly by the target's partnerships team and the target's outside counsel (because it is both a commercial conversation and a legal instrument). The specific outreach documents the specific transaction facts, the specific consent request, the specific ongoing-commitment language, and the specific timeline.

## Integration-communications cadence

Post-signing, customers and partners receive a specific cadence of integration communications that runs through close and into the first 100 days:

**Weekly customer updates (for the top-ARR customers).** A specific weekly written update from the CRO or from the CEO covering: integration-status highlights, product-roadmap updates, customer-facing team updates, any specific relevant news. The specific weekly cadence is a specific touchpoint that keeps the account team's relationship active during the ambiguity window.

**Monthly all-customer updates.** A specific monthly written update sent to all customers (typically via email newsletter or the specific customer portal) covering: integration milestones, product updates, upcoming customer events, ongoing account-team commitments.

**Quarterly customer summits (post-close, for the top-ARR customers).** A specific in-person or virtual event for the top-ARR customers, typically 60–120 days post-close, that reinforces the specific commitments made at announcement and previews the specific ongoing roadmap. The specific event is often joint with the acquirer's leadership.

**Partner ecosystem updates.** Analogous cadence for partners — weekly for tier-1, monthly for tier-2 and below.

**The integration-communications-cadence handoff.** The sell-side communications workstream continues through the first 100 days under this module's discipline. From day 100 onwards, the workstream is subsumed into the ongoing acquirer-managed customer-communications programme (mod-111's territory).

## Reference-request timing — the pre-warned reference-call pattern

A specific customer-communications workstream that happens **before signing** and that the sell-side commonly under-manages is the **reference-call workstream**. The pattern:

**The buy-side data room contains customer references.** During diligence, the buyer typically requests customer references — specific named customers whom the buyer's team can call directly to validate specific claims (product satisfaction, competitive positioning, willingness to expand, specific case-study material). The sell-side provides a specific reference list — typically 5–15 named customers, drawn from the top-tier accounts, with specific contact information.

**Reference calls happen during exclusivity.** The specific reference calls are typically made during the exclusivity period (post-LOI, pre-signing), often in the final 2–4 weeks before signing. The buyer's diligence team (or their specific external commercial-diligence firm — e.g., L.E.K., McKinsey, Bain, or a specific tech-diligence firm) calls the specific reference contacts.

**The customer must be pre-warned.** The specific practitioner pattern: the CRO or the specific relationship-owning executive calls each specific reference customer *before* the specific buy-side reference call, explains that the target is in a specific confidential process, asks the customer to accept a specific inbound call from "a firm doing due diligence on us in connection with a strategic transaction we are exploring," and specifically requests that the customer maintain confidentiality until the transaction is publicly announced. The pre-warning is critical because:
- The customer being called out of the blue by an unknown firm asking specific questions about the target is a specific alarming signal that erodes the relationship.
- The customer being pre-warned and asked for their participation feels included in the relationship rather than blindsided.
- The customer's specific answers to the specific reference-call questions are typically more considered when the customer knows the context.
- The customer's specific promise of confidentiality is a specific protective mechanism against the deal leaking through the customer channel.

**The reference-call script for the sell-side pre-warning.** A specific 5-minute call: "[Name], I wanted to reach out about something specifically confidential. We are exploring a specific strategic transaction — I cannot share details yet, but I wanted to give you a heads-up because someone from [buy-side diligence firm, if allowed] may reach out to you in the next 1–2 weeks with specific questions about your experience as a customer. I would ask two specific things: first, if you are open to taking the call, we would appreciate it — your voice matters. Second, I would ask that you hold this in confidence until we are able to announce publicly. Are you comfortable with that? Thank you for your partnership."

**Reference outcomes.** The buyer's reference calls occasionally surface specific concerns that shape the specific deal terms (a specific customer concern about pricing, a specific customer concern about product direction). The sell-side is typically not on the specific reference calls, but hears the buyer's synthesis of the specific reference-call findings and has the opportunity to respond to specific concerns in the specific documentation.

## The specific pattern of holding customer and press communications separate

A specific coordination discipline: the customer-communications workstream is deliberately held separate from the press-communications workstream, even though both go out on the same day. The specific reason: **customers should hear about the deal directly from the target before they read about it in the press**.

The specific operational mechanism: the press embargo (see chapter 4) is set for a specific time — say 6:30am Eastern — that is *after* the customer-call schedule begins on the announcement day. The top-5 customers are called before the press embargo lifts. The next tier is called in the specific window between the embargo lift and the press's article publication — typically a 30–90-minute window. The rest of the customer-communications programme is executed in parallel with the press cycle, but the specific top-tier accounts hear from the target first.

The specific consequence of failing to sequence: a specific top-customer reads the transaction in the WSJ before receiving a call from the target CEO, and the specific-customer relationship carries a specific dent for the specific reason that the customer feels informed by a journalist rather than a partner. The specific pattern that recurs across deals is that the specific customers who felt blindsided by press-first learn are more likely to be the specific customers who do not renew.

## Summary

The customer, partner, and channel-communications workstream is a *specific pre-designed, sequenced, and sponsor-driven programme* that begins with change-of-control clause diligence during the pre-signing period, transitions to top-ARR-customer personal outreach within hours of announcement, layers on partner tier-communications sequenced by strategic importance, coordinates specific channel-reseller consent workflows for specific COC-consent-required agreements, runs a specific weekly / monthly / quarterly cadence of integration communications through close and into the first 100 days, and includes the specific pre-warned reference-call pattern where the customer references sitting in the buy-side data room are personally briefed by the target's CRO before the specific reference call. The workstream is held deliberately separate from the press-communications workstream so that specific top-tier customers hear from the target directly before they read about the deal in the press. The specific consequence of executing the workstream well is a retained customer base and a preserved partner ecosystem; the specific consequence of executing it poorly is a specific ARR-churn premium and a specific partner-defection wave that eats a specific fraction of the deal's realised synergy.
