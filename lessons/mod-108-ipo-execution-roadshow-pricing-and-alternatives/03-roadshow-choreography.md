# Roadshow Choreography — Pilot Fishing to Pricing Week

## Why this matters

The roadshow is the two-week marketing sprint in which the CEO, CFO, and (sometimes) the head of product or COO meet institutional-investor accounts one-on-one, in small groups, and at analyst-hosted group lunches to build the demand book that determines the pricing and allocation of the offering. Every meeting matters — a single Fidelity portfolio-manager conversation that goes badly can knock $100M of anticipated demand out of the book overnight; a single Baillie Gifford long-view conversation that lands well can bring in a $150M anchor order that reprices the range upward. The CEO / CFO who arrives at the roadshow without a specific rehearsed narrative, without the specific target-investor map, without the specific analyst-facing walk-through of the operating model, and without the specific practised answer to the specific hard question is a CEO / CFO who prices the deal on the wrong side of the range they could have achieved.

The roadshow is also the moment when the underwriter's *institutional-sales-force* franchise translates into a specific book. The lead-left's salespeople are on the phone every day of the marketing period with their institutional-account contacts — the Fidelity growth-fund PMs, the T. Rowe Price large-cap-growth analysts, the Wellington sector-team leads, the Capital Group Global Growth PMs — building indications of interest, escalating specific concerns to management for follow-up, and reading the book back to the lead-left syndicate desk. The daily-book-building read is what tells the syndicate whether the current range is under-covered, well-covered, or oversubscribed at what multiple; the read reprices the range in real time and shapes the specific pricing decision at the pricing meeting on the T-1 evening.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline of choreographing the roadshow — the T-14 to T-1 calendar, pilot fishing, cornerstone conversations, the mega-fund one-on-one map, the group-lunch pattern, the analyst-day, the roadshow-deck construction, and the daily book-building read.

> Reminder: the roadshow choreography sits on top of the gun-jumping regime installed in chapter 2. Every roadshow communication is post-filing (the S-1 is public by roadshow launch under the JOBS Act 15-day rule) but is still governed by the Section 5(b) prohibitions and the Rule 433 FWP regime. Every specific communication in the roadshow deck and every specific talking point in the CEO / CFO scripts is cleared by counsel with the S-1-consistency discipline.

## The T-14 to T-1 marketing calendar

The typical US IPO roadshow runs for approximately two weeks — the "T-14 to T-1" convention, where T is the day of pricing (an evening decision) and T+1 is the first day of trading on the exchange (the "break of issue" morning). The specific day-by-day calendar varies with the deal but converges on a repeatable pattern.

**T-30 to T-15 — pilot fishing.** The lead-left institutional sales team, working from the target-investor list, has 15–30 pilot-fishing conversations with the largest anticipated institutional accounts before the S-1 flip. Pilot fishing is TTW under Section 5(d) (chapter 2) — the specific rules apply. The purpose is to test the specific institutional-investor thesis on the issuer, to surface the specific concerns that the roadshow will need to address, and to build the initial anchor of the demand book. Pilot fishing is conducted by senior sales-and-trading personnel (typically the syndicate-desk head or a senior institutional-sales MD), not by the CEO / CFO; the CEO / CFO participates in a subset of pilot-fishing conversations for the specific largest anchor accounts.

**T-15 — public flip.** The confidential DRS is publicly filed as the S-1, starting the JOBS Act 15-day clock. Public filing triggers the transition from TTW to post-filing marketing. The specific communications regime shifts — the roadshow deck is authored as an FWP, the preliminary prospectus (red herring) is available on EDGAR, and the analyst-facing marketing content is prepared.

**T-14 to T-10 — analyst day and pre-launch preparation.** The specific practice varies — some deals hold an analyst day (a formal meeting for the sell-side research analysts who will initiate coverage at the end of the post-pricing quiet period), some deals hold an equivalent underwriter-branded institutional-marketing conference, some deals compress the pre-launch preparation into the T-14 to T-8 window. The CEO / CFO rehearse the roadshow presentation, sit for the underwriter's mock-Q&A ("murder board") sessions, and finalise the specific pricing-range and offering-size proposal to be filed in the pre-effective amendment.

**T-10 to T-9 — range announcement and roadshow launch.** The pre-effective amendment to the S-1 is filed with the SEC containing the anticipated pricing range and offering size (the specific practice: the range is announced as of the roadshow launch date, and is typically 15–20% below the anticipated pricing to give the deal room to price at or above the range). The roadshow launches — the electronic roadshow video is posted (typically on NetRoadshow or RetailRoadshow), and the CEO / CFO begin their in-person and virtual meeting sequence.

**T-9 to T-2 — the roadshow proper.** The CEO / CFO travel through the specific list of institutional-investor cities — typically New York (multiple days, given the concentration of institutional accounts), Boston (Fidelity, Wellington, State Street, MFS, Putnam), Baltimore (T. Rowe Price), San Francisco (Capital Group, Wellington SF office, Franklin Templeton, various hedge funds and crossovers), Los Angeles (Capital Group HQ, Franklin Templeton, PIMCO for cross-asset investors), Chicago (Nuveen, Northern Trust, William Blair, Ariel), Denver (Janus Henderson, various growth-focused funds), Kansas City (American Century), Edinburgh (Baillie Gifford — for global-growth deals, a specific mandatory stop), London (BlackRock EMEA, Vanguard EMEA, various UK-based long-only and hedge accounts), Frankfurt / Amsterdam / Zurich (specific European long-only accounts), Singapore / Hong Kong (GIC, Temasek, Fidelity APAC, various Asia-based accounts). The specific city list is tailored per deal — a US-institutional-only book skips Europe and Asia; a large-cap deal with global appetite hits all major hubs. Post-COVID, a meaningful fraction of the roadshow is virtual (Zoom meetings replacing physical travel), which has compressed the calendar and expanded the number of accounts that can be reached in a given day.

**T-2 to T-1 — book closes, pricing meeting, underwriting agreement execution.** The book closes on T-1 late afternoon (typically 4 PM ET, sometimes later for a hot deal). The syndicate desk reads the book — total indications of interest, the price at which the book is covered, the composition of the book by account type — and presents to the pricing committee. The pricing meeting (T-1 evening, typically 5–7 PM) is where the final price and size are decided. The underwriting agreement is signed after the pricing meeting. The company is officially "priced."

**T-0 — first day of trading.** The stock opens on the exchange the following morning. The lead-left syndicate desk manages the opening — the first trade prints at whatever the market clears (typically above the offering price in a hot deal, at or slightly above in a well-priced deal, at or below in a soft deal), and the syndicate begins the aftermarket stabilisation and greenshoe management under Reg M (chapter 5).

**T+1 to T+30 — the aftermarket window.** The syndicate manages stabilisation and covers the syndicate short with the greenshoe under Reg M. The FINRA Rule 2242 research quiet period constrains underwriter research initiation; at the quiet-period expiry (typically 10 days for lead managers under current FINRA rules, longer for other participants historically), the sell-side research initiations arrive. The lock-up begins its 180-day clock (chapter 6).

## Pilot fishing — what it accomplishes

Pilot fishing serves three specific purposes.

**Pricing signal.** The specific institutional-investor feedback on pricing during pilot fishing — "we'd be interested at $18, we would not be interested at $22" from a Fidelity growth-fund PM whose specific mandate makes them a natural anchor — is the earliest signal on where the book will clear. Multiple pilot-fishing conversations converge to a specific reading of the demand curve at various price points. That reading is what the lead-left uses to propose the initial-range filing (the 15–20% below the anticipated pricing convention).

**Thesis-and-concern surfacing.** Pilot fishing surfaces the specific concerns that will need to be addressed in the roadshow — the growth-deceleration concern, the customer-concentration concern, the competitive-positioning concern, the profitability-timeline concern, the specific-metric-comparison-to-comparable-issuers concern. The concerns become the roadshow-Q&A preparation topics — the specific answers the CEO / CFO practise until each answer is fluent and consistent with the S-1.

**Anchor-account development.** Pilot fishing is where the specific anchor accounts — the largest anticipated institutional orders — are cultivated. A T. Rowe Price large-cap-growth PM who commits (informally, subject to standard institutional-investor caveats) to a $75M anchor order is a specific book-building outcome that reprices the range and reprices the deal's confidence. The CEO / CFO participation in specific pilot-fishing meetings with anchor accounts is what makes the anchor conversation stick — a pilot-fishing meeting run only by sales-and-trading typically does not produce anchor commitments; a CEO / CFO meeting typically does.

## Cornerstone conversations

A **cornerstone investor** is a specific pre-pricing investor commitment to purchase a specified allocation at the IPO price. Cornerstones are standard in Asia-Pacific IPO practice (HKEX and SGX listings routinely feature multiple cornerstone investors) and are increasingly common in large US IPOs. In the US, the cornerstone commitment is typically documented in a subscription letter delivered to the underwriters, is subject to specific disclosure treatment in the prospectus, and is subject to a specific lock-up (often 90–180 days) that mirrors the standard insider lock-up.

The cornerstone conversation runs during the TTW / pilot-fishing period. The CEO / CFO meets the specific cornerstone candidate (typically a sovereign-wealth fund — GIC, Temasek, Mubadala, ADIA, Norges; a strategic corporate investor with an existing commercial or strategic relationship; a large family office; or a specific institutional-investor account with a mandate that fits the anchor role). The specific ask is a commitment to purchase $X million of the offering at the pricing determined by the deal, subject to standard institutional-investor caveats and subject to the specific lock-up terms.

Cornerstone commitments serve three specific purposes. They **de-risk the offering** — a $150M cornerstone anchor in a $300M deal means the book only needs to build $150M of independent institutional demand. They **signal quality** — a Baillie Gifford or GIC cornerstone commitment signals to the buy-side that a specific sophisticated long-view investor has done the diligence and taken the position. They **reprice the range** — a cornerstone commitment at the top of the initial range strengthens the pricing conversation and often reprices the range upward.

The trade-off is that a cornerstone commitment ties up a specific fraction of the allocation with a single account. The allocation choreography (chapter 4) has to fit around the cornerstone commitment — the remaining allocation is what is available for the roadshow book. A deal with a $200M cornerstone in a $300M base offering has only $100M plus the greenshoe available for the broader institutional book; the demand-and-oversubscription math has to work at that reduced-availability base.

## The mega-fund one-on-one map

The specific institutional-investor accounts most valuable in the US IPO book are the large long-only mutual-fund complexes, the large sovereign-wealth funds, the largest crossover hedge funds, and the largest index-fund complexes.

**Long-only mutual funds.** T. Rowe Price (Baltimore — large-cap growth, mid-cap growth, and specific sector strategies), Fidelity (Boston — Contrafund, Blue Chip Growth, OTC, Growth Company, and dozens of specific sector funds), Wellington (Boston — the sub-advisor to Vanguard PRIMECAP and other large mandates, plus proprietary funds), Capital Group (Los Angeles — American Funds Growth Fund of America, New Perspective Fund, EuroPacific Growth Fund, and dozens of others), Franklin Templeton (San Mateo — proprietary long-only mandates and the Legg Mason franchises acquired in 2020), MFS (Boston), Putnam (Boston), Janus Henderson (Denver / London), Nuveen (Chicago), American Century (Kansas City), Vanguard (Malvern — index funds and specific actively-managed funds through subadvisors), BlackRock (New York — index funds, iShares, and actively-managed mandates through Aladdin infrastructure), State Street (Boston — SPDR index funds and specific active mandates), Legg Mason via Franklin, and Baillie Gifford (Edinburgh — global-growth long-view specialist with a specific 5–10-year holding-period discipline that makes Baillie a specific fit for long-view issuers).

**Crossover hedge funds.** Coatue (Philippe Laffont, Chase Coleman lineage, tech-crossover from Tiger Cub tradition), Tiger Global (Chase Coleman, Julian Robertson lineage), D1 Capital (Dan Sundheim, formerly Viking, tech and consumer crossover), Whale Rock (Alex Sacerdote, tech-crossover), Lone Pine (Steve Mandel, Julian Robertson lineage, broader mandate), Viking (Andreas Halvorsen, Julian Robertson lineage), Maverick (Lee Ainslie, Julian Robertson lineage), Discovery Capital (Rob Citrone, Julian Robertson lineage). The Tiger-cub-tradition funds and the specific specialist funds (Whale Rock, D1) are the specific accounts that provide the crossover demand that lifts a hot deal.

**Sovereign-wealth funds.** GIC (Singapore — massive AUM, deep US institutional allocation), Temasek (Singapore — specific tech-crossover mandate), Mubadala (Abu Dhabi), ADIA (Abu Dhabi), Norges (Norway — the largest single-country sovereign fund, largely US-institutional-allocation), CIC (China), KIC (Korea), CPP Investments (Canada), OTPP (Ontario Teachers). Cornerstone-conversation candidates.

**Sector-specialist and thematic funds.** The specific sector-focused funds — cloud specialists, cybersecurity specialists, biotech specialists, fintech specialists, EV specialists, AI-infrastructure specialists — depend on the issuer's sub-sector. For an AI-infrastructure IPO, the specific accounts include ARK Invest (Cathie Wood), Rowan / Coatue AI-focus vehicles, and the specific AI-thesis funds in the Fidelity / Wellington / T. Rowe Price lineups.

The specific one-on-one map is authored by the lead-left syndicate desk with CFO input, is validated against the pilot-fishing feedback, and is executed across the T-9 to T-2 window. The CEO / CFO calendar is booked at typically 8–12 meetings per day (in a heavy roadshow) with specific accounts pre-scheduled and specific alternates in case a meeting cancels.

## Group lunches and the analyst day

Beyond the one-on-one meetings, two specific group-format events shape the demand book.

**Group lunches.** Each roadshow city typically features one or two group lunches — a 60–90-minute event where 15–40 institutional-investor representatives attend to see the CEO / CFO present the deck and take Q&A. Group lunches are efficiency devices — they let the CEO / CFO reach 30 accounts in an hour instead of one at a time — and they serve as a specific signal-generation event; the accounts who ask specific engaged questions and follow up post-lunch for one-on-ones are the specific accounts most likely to convert to demand. Group lunches are typically hosted by a specific syndicate bank in a specific city (Goldman NY, Morgan Stanley Boston, JPMorgan Chicago, BofA SF, etc.), with the host bank's institutional-sales team populating the guest list.

**The analyst day / underwriter-branded institutional-marketing conference.** For larger deals, the syndicate may host an analyst day (a formal meeting for the sell-side research analysts covering the sector, held before the roadshow launch — typically T-14 or T-12) or an underwriter-branded institutional-marketing conference. The analyst day is not a marketing event per se — the analysts are not permitted to publish research on the specific issuer during the offering window under the Rule 137/138/139 and FINRA Rule 2242 framework — but the analyst day gives the analysts the specific management-time exposure they need to build the initiation-of-coverage model they will publish at the end of the quiet period post-pricing. The specific analyst-day discipline is (a) the analysts get the same information the buy-side sees in the roadshow deck, (b) the analysts submit questions in a specific structured format, and (c) the specific answers are captured for the roadshow-Q&A discipline that applies through the offering window.

**Institutional marketing collateral.** The underwriter salespeople carry a specific institutional-marketing packet — the preliminary prospectus, the roadshow-deck FWP, the underwriter-branded sales sheet (if any — the specific format subject to Rule 433 filing), the specific issuer-produced FAQ / Q&A extract, and specific comparable-issuer analysis authored by the underwriter's institutional-sales-desk research. The collateral is deployed through the specific institutional-sales-force calls that build the book each day.

## The roadshow deck — construction principles

The roadshow deck is the single most important marketing artefact of the offering. It is an FWP under Rule 433, is filed with the SEC (or hosted with equivalent access controls), and is consistent with the S-1 in every material respect. The construction discipline is specific.

**Length.** Typical roadshow deck length is 30–45 slides. Too short and the account-level Q&A drifts into topics not anchored in the deck; too long and the CEO / CFO cannot execute the deck within a 45-minute meeting slot.

**Structure.** The typical structure: (1) title-and-transaction slide (offering size, ticker, exchange, use of proceeds), (2) executive-summary slide (the specific one-sentence pitch), (3) market opportunity, (4) product / platform, (5) customer / GTM traction with specific metrics (ARR, growth, NRR, gross margin, unit economics, Rule of 40, cohort analysis), (6) competitive positioning with specific comparable-vendor mapping, (7) financial model (historical, forward-guidance-consistent projections aligned to the S-1 MD&A), (8) capital structure and use-of-proceeds detail, (9) leadership team and board, (10) transaction summary, (11) appendix (specific supporting data — market-sizing detail, cohort-analysis detail, product-detail slides, backup analysis).

**Reproducibility with the S-1.** Every specific claim in the deck — every metric, every projection, every market-size figure, every competitive-positioning statement — is reproducible in the S-1 or in the SEC-filed supplementary prospectus material. The specific practitioner discipline: any deck slide that cannot be tied back to a specific S-1 page is either revised or removed.

**FWP legend.** The deck carries the specific Rule 433 legend on every slide (or on the specific cover slide) directing recipients to the preliminary prospectus and the SEC registration.

**Rehearsal.** The CEO / CFO rehearse the deck delivery to fluency — the specific timing of each slide, the specific transitions, the specific talking-point for each slide. Underwriter counsel and the lead-left banker sit in on rehearsals ("murder boards") and stress-test the delivery with specific hard-Q&A. The rehearsal cycle typically runs 2–4 sessions across the T-14 to T-10 window.

## The daily book-building read

Every day of the marketing period, the lead-left syndicate desk assembles the book — the running list of indications of interest by account, by size, by price, by conditions. The book is read in a specific daily cadence.

**Daily coverage reading.** The syndicate desk reads the book against the initial range: is the book "covered" (indications sum to the offering size at the range midpoint), "well-covered" (2× the offering size), "oversubscribed" (5×+ the offering size), or "under-covered" (less than 1× the offering size at the range midpoint)? The reading is presented to the CEO / CFO in a daily update.

**Composition reading.** Beyond aggregate coverage, the book is read for composition. What percentage of the book is long-only mutual funds vs. crossover hedge funds vs. hedge funds vs. cornerstone? What percentage is US vs. Europe vs. Asia? What percentage is the top-10 accounts vs. the tail? What percentage is priced-at-range vs. priced-above-range vs. priced-below-range? The composition reading shapes the specific allocation decisions at the pricing meeting.

**Range revision.** If the book is well-covered above the range within the first 2–3 days, the lead-left may propose a **range up-revision** — filing an amendment to the S-1 with an increased pricing range (e.g., moving from $18–$22 to $22–$26). Range up-revisions require a specific supplementary prospectus filing and can require the SEC to reprocess certain aspects of the effectiveness readiness. Under Rule 430A and specific SEC guidance, the pricing can be set within a limited spread from the last-filed range without requiring a new preliminary prospectus circulation; a range revision outside that spread triggers specific re-circulation requirements. The lead-left / issuer counsel navigates the specifics.

If the book is under-covered, the lead-left may propose a **range down-revision** (an amendment to a lower range) or, in more severe cases, a **postponement** of the pricing. Postponing a deal is a specific negative signal — it makes the deal harder to price when it reopens — and is done only when the alternative is worse.

**Quality signal reading.** The specific accounts committing to the book are read for aftermarket signal. A book heavily weighted to fundamental long-only investors with 3–10-year holding periods is a specific aftermarket-quality signal; a book heavily weighted to hedge-fund flippers who intend to sell at the open is a specific aftermarket-risk signal. The composition informs the allocation decision (chapter 4) — the allocation methodology weights the fundamental-quality accounts and dilutes the flip-oriented accounts.

## The specific hard questions and how to answer them

Every roadshow encounters specific hard questions. The CEO / CFO who has rehearsed the specific answers wins; the one who improvises loses.

**"Why now?"** Why is this the right IPO window for this company? Answer: the specific readiness signals (ARR, growth, Rule of 40, market position) plus the specific capital-markets window plus the specific strategic use of the proceeds and public-company currency.

**"What's your growth-durability story?"** Investors want to underwrite growth that continues, not growth that peaked. Answer: the specific cohort-based analysis showing durable retention and expansion, the specific new-product and new-market-vector story, the specific competitive-position dynamics that support continued growth.

**"Walk us through the unit economics."** Payback period, magic number, LTV / CAC, CAC-adjusted-for-mix, gross-margin trajectory. Answer: the specific numbers, drawn from the S-1 and the roadshow deck, with the specific reconciliation between the reported metrics and the underlying cohort math.

**"How do you compete with [specific competitor]?"** The specific competitive-positioning story, drawn from the S-1 competitive-landscape disclosure, with the specific product / GTM / customer-outcome differentiation.

**"What's the profitability path?"** The specific timeline to positive operating cash flow, positive net income, and free-cash-flow-conversion. Answer: the specific S-1-consistent projections with the specific milestones and the specific investment-lever adjustments the finance function is committed to executing.

**"What's the M&A appetite?"** The specific inorganic-growth thesis, drawn from the use-of-proceeds disclosure, with the specific criteria the company will apply to acquisition opportunities.

**"How do you think about capital allocation post-IPO?"** The specific framework — organic reinvestment, opportunistic M&A, working-capital needs, potential buybacks — with the specific board oversight discipline.

The specific answers are S-1-consistent and rehearsed. The CEO / CFO who is asked a question they have not rehearsed answers with a specific deferral ("we'll follow up on that specifically after the meeting; the general approach in our S-1 is [X]") rather than improvising.

## Post-pricing hand-off

At the pricing meeting on T-1, the CEO / CFO transition from marketing to execution. The specific hand-off:

- The pricing committee approves the final price and size.
- The underwriting agreement is executed.
- The syndicate desk moves into stabilisation-and-greenshoe mode for the aftermarket (chapter 5).
- The IR team activates for the first-day-of-trading press coverage.
- The 180-day lock-up begins (chapter 6).
- The FINRA Rule 2242 research quiet period begins for the syndicate research analysts, running through the specific expiry period (typically 10 days for lead managers).

The CEO / CFO are done marketing. The next public-facing conversation is the first quarterly earnings call as a public company, roughly 6–10 weeks post-pricing depending on the fiscal calendar. The IR function (stood up 6–12 months before pricing under the mod-107 chapter 1 readiness discipline) runs the aftermarket engagement — the sell-side coverage relationships, the buy-side account-relationship management, the industry-conference calendar, and the specific Reg FD discipline for public-company communications.

## Summary

The roadshow is the two-week marketing sprint that builds the demand book determining pricing and allocation. The T-14 to T-1 calendar covers pilot fishing (T-30 to T-15), the public S-1 flip (T-15), the roadshow-launch and range-announcement (T-10 to T-9), the roadshow proper (T-9 to T-2), and the pricing meeting (T-1). Pilot fishing surfaces the pricing signal, the thesis-and-concern set, and the anchor accounts. Cornerstone conversations produce pre-pricing commitments from sovereign-wealth-fund, strategic-corporate-investor, and specific institutional-account anchors with specific lock-up discipline. The mega-fund one-on-one map covers the specific long-only accounts (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Wellington, Capital Group, BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, Franklin Templeton, Baillie Gifford), the crossover hedge funds (Coatue, Tiger, D1, Whale Rock, Lone Pine), the sovereign-wealth funds (GIC, Temasek, Norges, Mubadala, ADIA), and the sector-specialist funds relevant to the sub-sector. Group lunches and the analyst-day supplement the one-on-one meetings. The roadshow deck is a 30–45-slide FWP consistent with the S-1, rehearsed to fluency. The daily book-building read tracks coverage, composition, and quality signal; range up-revisions and down-revisions are made in response to the read within the Rule 430A framework. The specific hard-question rehearsal (why-now, growth-durability, unit economics, competition, profitability path, M&A appetite, capital allocation) is what separates a well-priced deal from a soft one. Post-pricing, the CEO / CFO hand off to the IR function and the syndicate stabilisation desk.

Chapter 4 turns to the pricing and allocation decision — the initial-range convention, the range-revision decision, the pricing-meeting choreography, and the allocation methodology across long-only / crossover / cornerstone / EDSP / friends-and-family buckets.
