# IPO Alternatives — Direct Listing, SPAC / de-SPAC, DPO, and Reg A+

## Why this matters

The traditional bookbuilt IPO — the six-to-nine-month S-1 workstream, the underwriter bake-off, the roadshow, the priced-at-offering, the lock-up — is the default path but is not the only path. Four specific alternatives operate under specific SEC and exchange rules, each with a specific issuer-type-and-transaction-goal fit:

- **Direct listing.** A company lists its existing shares on an exchange without a bookbuilt underwritten offering. Since 2020, direct listings may include a primary-capital-raise component.
- **SPAC / de-SPAC.** A special-purpose acquisition company that raised cash from public investors merges with a private target, taking the target public through the merger rather than through an S-1 offering.
- **Direct Public Offering (DPO).** A small-issuer direct-to-investor offering, typically under a specific exemption or a small SEC registration, without an underwriter syndicate.
- **Reg A+ Tier 2.** A specific SEC-registered offering up to $75M in a 12-month period, with scaled disclosure and often direct-to-retail marketing.

Each alternative has a specific transaction cost, a specific execution timeline, a specific investor-base outcome, and a specific ongoing-regime post-listing implication. The CFO / CEO / GC's decision is not "IPO good, alternatives bad" — the decision is a specific issuer-fit match. Spotify and Slack chose direct listing because they had strong brand recognition, a large enough existing shareholder base to create liquidity without a bookbuilt offering, and no need for primary capital. A specific consumer-brand company with a passionate retail audience may fit Reg A+ Tier 2 better than a traditional IPO. A specific pre-revenue technology company with a specific SPAC-sponsor-relationship may fit a de-SPAC transaction. The specific decision requires understanding each alternative on its specific terms.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline of the four alternatives — the specific mechanic, the specific rule framework, the specific issuer fit, and the specific decision criteria for choosing among them and the traditional IPO.

## The direct listing

A **direct listing** (also called a "direct floor listing") is a mechanism by which a company lists its existing outstanding shares on a national securities exchange (NYSE or Nasdaq) without a bookbuilt underwritten offering. Existing shareholders can sell into the aftermarket immediately at market-determined prices; the company itself does not sell primary shares (in the pre-2020 form) or may sell a specific primary component (in the post-2020 form).

**Mechanic — original form (pre-2020).** The company files an S-1 (or a Form 10 for a spin-off) with the SEC, clears the SEC review, and lists on the exchange. On the specific first day of trading, the exchange's opening auction (NYSE Designated Market Maker or Nasdaq Opening Cross) determines the specific opening price based on buy and sell orders from existing shareholders and new investors. There is no bookbuilt offering, no underwriter syndicate, no lock-up (except any pre-existing contractual lock-ups from pre-IPO financing rounds), and no primary-capital raise.

**Mechanic — post-2020 primary-offering component.** In December 2020, the SEC approved amendments to NYSE Rule 102.01B (and separately Nasdaq Rule 5315) that authorised direct listings to include a **primary-offering component** — the company sells newly-issued shares into the opening auction, raising primary capital. The specific mechanic: the company registers the specific primary shares on the S-1 alongside the existing-shareholder shares, and the opening auction absorbs both the primary and secondary shares at the market-cleared price. The 2020 amendments removed the specific pre-2020 constraint that direct listings were secondary-only, materially expanding the addressable-issuer set.

**SEC and exchange rules.** NYSE Rule 102.01B (with the specific 2020 amendments) and Nasdaq Rule 5315 (with the specific Nasdaq-equivalent framework) govern the direct-listing eligibility, the specific opening-auction mechanics, and the specific advisor role.

**Financial advisor role.** Direct listings do not have a traditional underwriter with firm-commitment purchase and gross-spread economics. Instead, the company engages one or more **financial advisors** (typically the same tier-1 investment banks that would underwrite a traditional IPO — Goldman, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, etc.) who advise on the specific S-1 drafting, coordinate the specific investor-education activities, and (for post-2020 primary-component direct listings) advise on the specific opening-auction pricing. The financial advisors are paid a specific advisory fee (typically materially lower than the traditional 6-7% gross spread — often 1-2% of the transaction), do not take firm-commitment underwriting risk, do not have a syndicate short, do not exercise a greenshoe, and do not run a traditional bookbuilt allocation. The specific financial-advisor role is a specific mid-2010s-and-later innovation in direct-listing execution.

**Investor-education pre-listing.** Direct listings involve a specific "investor day" or "investor education" event — a public presentation by management to institutional investors, in some cases open to retail, that provides the specific business overview, financial results, growth trajectory, and forward-looking guidance. The investor day is *not* a bookbuilt marketing exercise (there are no allocation decisions, no cornerstone commitments, no traditional roadshow structure) but serves the same investor-education function — building the specific institutional-investor understanding of the company that will inform the specific opening-auction pricing.

**Aftermarket dynamics.** Direct listings have specific aftermarket dynamics that differ from bookbuilt IPOs. There is no specific 15-25% first-day pop (the opening auction clears at the market price, not at an underwriter-set offering price); there is no specific syndicate stabilisation regime (the specific market-maker opening-auction mechanics substitute); there is no specific 180-day lock-up as a standard (some direct listings involve specific contractual lock-ups on specific pre-IPO shareholders, others do not — the specific practice varies). The specific first-week / first-month aftermarket price behaviour tends to be more volatile than bookbuilt IPOs because the specific supply and demand curves are less controlled by underwriter mechanics.

**Case examples.** Spotify (April 2018, NYSE, secondary-only), Slack (June 2019, NYSE, secondary-only), Palantir (September 2020, NYSE, secondary-only), Asana (September 2020, NYSE, secondary-only), Coinbase (April 2021, Nasdaq, secondary-only), Roblox (March 2021, NYSE, secondary-only), Warby Parker (September 2021, NYSE, secondary-only), Amplitude (September 2021, Nasdaq, secondary-only), Squarespace (May 2021, NYSE, secondary-only). The pre-2021 cohort was almost entirely secondary-only; the 2020 amendments authorising primary-component direct listings have seen slower-than-anticipated adoption, with only a small number of primary-component direct listings executed in the years since.

**Fit criteria for direct listing.** Direct listings tend to fit companies with:

- **Strong brand recognition and existing shareholder base.** The opening auction needs a specific base of existing sellers and new buyers to clear meaningfully; a company with a well-known brand and a large existing shareholder base (from meaningful venture-backing across multiple rounds, employees, and secondary-market transactions) has the natural liquidity to support the opening.
- **No urgent need for primary capital.** Companies with strong balance sheets that do not need the specific fundraising output of a bookbuilt IPO can accept the specific secondary-only form (in pre-2020 form) or the specific smaller primary component (in post-2020 form). Companies with specific primary-capital needs may find the bookbuilt IPO's specific fundraising mechanic more efficient.
- **Willingness to accept first-day price volatility.** Direct listings do not have the underwriter-set offering price and the specific first-day pop; the specific opening price is what the market clears. Companies that specifically want the underwriter-mediated pricing predictability may find the bookbuilt IPO the better fit.
- **Strong investor-education infrastructure.** The direct-listing investor-education process requires a specific pre-listing management effort that substitutes for the bookbuilt roadshow. Companies with strong existing investor-communication infrastructure fit this better than companies without.

## SPAC / de-SPAC

A **SPAC** (special-purpose acquisition company) is a shell company that raises cash from public investors in an IPO of specific units (typically each unit consisting of one share and a fraction of a warrant), places the specific cash in trust, and has a specific fixed time period (typically 18-24 months) to identify and merge with a private-company target (the "de-SPAC" transaction). **The two-step process.**

1. **SPAC IPO.** The SPAC raises cash from public investors (typically institutional investors and specific retail participants) in a specific SEC-registered IPO. - **Co-registrant liability.** Under the specific 2024 rules, both the SPAC and the target private company are named as co-registrants on the de-SPAC registration statement (Form S-4 or Form F-4). ## Direct Public Offering (DPO)

A **Direct Public Offering** is a small-issuer securities offering made directly to investors (typically retail, sometimes institutional) without an underwriter syndicate. DPOs typically use specific SEC exemptions (Rule 504 up to $10M in 12 months, Rule 147 for intrastate offerings, or specific small SEC-registered offerings under Regulation A / Reg A+ discussed below) rather than the full S-1 process.

**Mechanic.** The issuer files the specific offering document with the SEC (or with specific state regulators for state-level exemptions), markets the offering directly to investors through specific channels (typically online platforms, community-and-affinity marketing, and specific direct-investor-outreach), and takes subscriptions and payments directly.

**Historical use.** DPOs were more common in the pre-online-securities-marketing era (specific consumer-brand DPOs by companies like Ben & Jerry's in 1984 raised capital directly from customers and community members). The modern equivalent is largely subsumed by Regulation A+ (discussed below) and by specific crowdfunding platforms operating under Regulation Crowdfunding.

**Fit criteria.** DPOs remain a specific option for small-issuer specific-community-anchor situations. A specific consumer-brand or specific mission-driven company with a passionate customer or community base may find a DPO a specific attractive path — the specific investor-base is aligned with the company's mission, the specific costs are lower than a traditional IPO, and the specific ongoing-regime is lighter. The specific trade-off is that DPOs do not produce the specific institutional-investor base or the specific liquid public-market listing that a traditional IPO or Reg A+ produces.

Post-offering, the company is subject to specific ongoing reporting under Form 1-K (annual), Form 1-SA (semi-annual), and Form 1-U (current-report equivalent) — a specific reduced-frequency reporting regime compared to full Exchange Act reporting.

**Exchange listing.** Reg A+ Tier 2 securities can be listed on national securities exchanges (NYSE, Nasdaq, or the specific NYSE American or Nasdaq Capital Market tier). A Reg A+ issuer that lists on a national exchange typically becomes subject to specific Exchange Act reporting (Form 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K) alongside or in place of the Reg A+ reporting regime — the specific dual-regime navigation is a specific practitioner topic. Many Reg A+ Tier 2 offerings historically have been unlisted or listed only on the OTC Markets rather than on a national exchange.

## The decision framework — choosing among the alternatives

The specific decision among traditional IPO, direct listing, de-SPAC, DPO, and Reg A+ Tier 2 is a specific issuer-fit match across several dimensions.

**Dimension 2 — brand and shareholder-base strength.** Direct listing works when the company has strong brand recognition and a large existing shareholder base that can support the opening-auction liquidity. **Dimension 3 — sector fit.** Certain sectors have historically shown specific alternative-fit patterns — consumer brands for Reg A+, mission-driven consumer for DPO, mega-brand-tech for direct listing, specific early-stage-technology for de-SPAC (though the 2024 rules have reduced de-SPAC fit). The specific sector-fit is one of several inputs.

**Dimension 4 — timeline.** Traditional IPO takes 6-9 months from org meeting to pricing. Direct listing is roughly equivalent in duration. De-SPAC (post-target-identification) takes 4-6 months typically. Reg A+ Tier 2 takes 4-8 months typically. The specific timeline urgency varies by issuer and by market-window read.

**Dimension 5 — cost.** Traditional IPO gross-spread is 6-7% (chapter 1). Direct-listing financial-advisor fees are typically 1-2%. **Dimension 6 — ongoing regime.** Traditional IPO, direct listing, and de-SPAC all produce a specific full Exchange Act reporting regime (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, proxy, Section 16). **Dimension 7 — liability posture.** All four alternatives (with de-SPAC particularly relevant given the 2024 rules) engage specific SEC Section 11 / Section 12 / Rule 10b-5 liability. The specific liability posture is a specific counsel-review topic.

**Dimension 8 — investor-base composition.** Traditional IPO produces the specific institutional-investor-anchored base (long-only mutual funds, crossover funds, sovereign wealth, plus retail).
