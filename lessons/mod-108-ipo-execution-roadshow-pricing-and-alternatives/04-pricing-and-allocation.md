# Pricing the Offering and Allocating the Book

## Why this matters

The pricing decision is the single evening decision in the entire IPO process at which every prior workstream — the readiness assessment, the S-1 drafting, the underwriter selection, the roadshow — converges into a specific number. The number determines how much cash the company raises (deal size × price), how much dilution the existing cap table takes, what the first-day-of-trading market capitalisation is (which is the number the press reports, the number the employees see on the office ticker, the number the buy-side accounts mark their positions against), and — critically — where the aftermarket clears in the first days and weeks. A deal priced too high breaks issue and produces the "broken IPO" scenario covered in mod-107 chapter 1; a deal priced too low leaves money on the table for the underwriters' preferred accounts to make on day one at the company's expense. The pricing decision is bounded on both sides.

The allocation decision is the parallel decision at the pricing meeting that determines *who* owns the offered securities the next morning. Allocation is not democratic — the syndicate desk allocates the book across long-only, crossover, hedge-fund, cornerstone, employee-directed-share-programme, friends-and-family, and retail buckets according to a specific methodology that trades off aftermarket-quality (fundamental long-only holders don't flip; hedge funds might) against demand-clearing (a book that excludes hedge-fund demand may not clear at the target price) against strategic-relationship-building (a cornerstone account gets what was promised; a large future-strategic-account gets a specific-quality allocation) against retail-and-employee obligations (the friends-and-family programme, the employee-directed-share-programme). The allocation shape is largely invisible to the buy-side but determines the first-30-day aftermarket dynamics in specific ways.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline of running the pricing meeting, making the price / size / greenshoe decisions, and running the allocation methodology.

> Reminder: pricing and allocation decisions are governed by the underwriting agreement negotiated in chapter 1, the SEC rules covering pricing supplements (Rules 430A / 430B / 424(b)), the FINRA rules on new-issue-offering allocations (Rule 5130 / 5131), and the Reg M framework installed in chapter 5. The specific decisions are made by the pricing committee (a specific board-delegated authority described in chapter 1) in consultation with the lead-left syndicate desk.

## The initial pricing range — the 15-20%-below convention

At roadshow launch (T-10 to T-9), the S-1 is amended with an anticipated pricing range and offering size. The range and size are visible on the cover of the preliminary prospectus (the red herring) and appear on the specific S-1 amendment cover.

The convention is that the initial range is set at **15–20% below the anticipated pricing** — the specific price at which the lead-left and the CFO believe the deal will clear the book based on the pilot-fishing and cornerstone-conversation reads. The under-pricing convention serves several purposes. First, the initial range preserves optionality — if the book underclears, the deal can price within the range without a formal down-revision (a specific SEC-procedural event that signals weakness). Second, the initial range accommodates a range up-revision if the book overclears — the specific practitioner pattern of "priced above the range" is a positive-signal event that reflects the deal outperforming the initial marketed expectation. Third, the initial range gives the salesforce a specific "leaves-room-to-move" narrative to sell the deal — accounts that anchor at the range midpoint expect the pricing to trade up at least at first, and the initial range is calibrated to make that expectation reasonable.

The specific range width — typically $3–5 for a $15–$25 stock, $4–$6 for a $20–$35 stock, wider for larger-stock deals — is calibrated to give the pricing meeting flexibility while not being so wide that the specific pricing signal is diluted. The specific range dollar amount is disclosed on the prospectus cover ("we anticipate that the initial public offering price will be between $[X] and $[Y] per share"), and the specific offering-size range is similarly disclosed ("we are offering [A] to [B] shares of our common stock").

## Range revisions during the roadshow

The book-building read (chapter 3) drives the range-revision decisions. Two patterns:

**Range up-revision.** When the book is well-covered above the range within the first 2–3 days of the roadshow, the lead-left may propose filing an amendment to the S-1 with an increased range. The specific practitioner mechanics: the amendment is filed on EDGAR with the new range, the preliminary prospectus is re-circulated with the new range (subject to Rule 430A / 424(b) mechanics), the salesforce updates its account conversations, and the buy-side accounts either confirm their indications at the new range or drop out. A range up-revision is a specific positive signal — it tells the market the deal is oversubscribed and helps produce a first-day pop that reflects continued demand.

**Range down-revision.** When the book underclears within the first 4–5 days at the initial range, the lead-left may propose a down-revision. Down-revisions are a specific negative signal — they tell the market the deal has weaker demand than the initial range assumed — and the specific dynamic tends to compound (a down-revision can trigger further account dropouts as the accounts read the weaker demand as a bad signal on the deal). Down-revisions are usually a last resort before either pricing at the low end of the range, pricing below the range (specific SEC-procedural implications under Rule 430A that may require re-circulation), or postponing the pricing.

**Postponement.** In severe under-cover scenarios or in specific market-window-collapse scenarios (a sudden macro event during the roadshow), the pricing may be postponed. Postponement is a specific negative event that damages the syndicate's ability to reopen the deal when conditions improve. Postponed deals often reopen at materially wider spreads, at smaller size, or are permanently withdrawn.

**Rule 430A and pricing outside the range.** SEC Rule 430A permits the pricing to be set within specified limits without recirculating the preliminary prospectus, provided certain conditions are met. A pricing that is up to 20% higher or 20% lower than the mid-point of the last-filed range does not require recirculation; a pricing outside that spread does require recirculation. The specific 20% spread is a practitioner-known Rule 430A parameter that shapes the range-revision decision — the syndicate has flexibility within the 20% spread and needs specific procedural discipline outside it.

## The pricing meeting — T-1 evening

The pricing meeting is the specific evening event (typically 5–7 PM ET on the T-1 day) at which the final pricing decision is made. The specific attendees and choreography:

**Attendees.** The pricing committee (2–4 board members with pre-delegated pricing authority, as constituted at the org meeting under chapter 1), the CEO, the CFO, the GC, the lead-left senior banker and syndicate-desk head, joint-lead senior bankers, issuer's counsel, underwriter counsel, and specific advisors as needed.

**Book presentation.** The lead-left syndicate-desk head presents the closed book — the specific list of institutional accounts and their indications of interest at various price points. The presentation covers: total demand at each price point (typically presented as a demand curve), the specific top-10 or top-20 anchor accounts and their indications, the composition of the book by account type (long-only vs. crossover vs. hedge-fund vs. cornerstone vs. retail), the composition of the book by geography (US vs. Europe vs. Asia), the composition of the book by quality signal (fundamental long-view vs. momentum vs. flip-oriented), and the specific pricing recommendation with rationale.

**Discussion.** The pricing committee, the CEO / CFO, and the lead-left discuss the recommendation. The specific discussion topics: does the recommended price deliver the specific fundraising goal? Does the recommended price signal the specific market posture the company wants (aspirational vs. conservative)? Does the recommended price leave the specific room for the first-day-of-trading dynamics the company wants (a healthy first-day pop signals strong aftermarket demand; excessive first-day pop signals under-pricing that leaves money on the table)? Does the recommended price accommodate the specific cornerstone commitments that were made at pilot-fishing? Does the recommended allocation shape (see below) accommodate the specific accounts the company wants to build long-term relationships with?

**Decision.** The pricing committee approves the final price, the final offering size, and the greenshoe size. The specific decisions are documented in a written pricing-committee consent that becomes part of the closing record.

**Underwriting agreement execution.** After the pricing decision, the underwriting agreement is executed. The underwriting agreement is the firm-commitment purchase-and-resale contract — the underwriters commit to purchase the specific number of shares at the offering price less the gross spread, and to resell them at the offering price. The execution of the underwriting agreement is the moment the underwriters take firm underwriting risk.

**424(b) prospectus filing.** After pricing, the final prospectus (with the specific price and size and specific greenshoe terms) is filed with the SEC under Rule 424(b). The 424(b) filing is the specific SEC-declared-effective disclosure document.

**Overnight and morning coordination.** The syndicate coordinates the specific overnight book-allocation process, the transfer-agent readiness for the first-day settlement, and the exchange-listing readiness. The stock opens on the exchange the following morning with the syndicate desk managing the opening.

## Offering size and greenshoe size setting

The pricing meeting also decides the specific offering size (the base deal — the specific number of shares to be sold at the offering price) and the specific greenshoe size (the over-allotment option — typically 15% of the base deal, as discussed in chapter 5).

**Base offering size.** Set within the range disclosed in the preliminary prospectus, with the pricing committee's decision informed by the demand-curve read and the specific fundraising goal. A larger base offering raises more money but requires deeper book coverage at the target price; a smaller base offering may leave the company under-funded relative to the use-of-proceeds plan.

**Greenshoe size.** The greenshoe (formally, the "over-allotment option") allows the underwriters to purchase additional shares from the company at the offering price to cover a syndicate short position that arises when the underwriters allocate more shares to accounts than the base deal covers. The greenshoe is typically **15% of the base offering** — the specific FINRA and SEC-precedent-standard size. Larger greenshoes are permitted (up to specified limits) but are rare in practice. The specific greenshoe mechanic and its interaction with syndicate stabilisation is covered in chapter 5.

**Primary vs. secondary.** The offering size may include primary shares (new shares issued by the company, the proceeds of which flow to the company) and secondary shares (existing shares sold by pre-IPO holders, the proceeds of which flow to the selling holders). The specific primary / secondary mix is disclosed in the S-1 and is set by the specific board-authorised offering. Most IPOs are primarily primary (the company is raising capital); some IPOs include a secondary component for pre-IPO holders (specific-facts-dependent — the venture-fund holders may sell down a portion of their stake, founder holders may sell a specific portion, etc.). Secondary sales in an IPO are subject to specific SEC disclosure and cap-table-management discipline.

## Allocation methodology — the buckets and the trade-offs

Allocation is the decision about which accounts get how much of the offered securities. Allocation is not democratic — the syndicate desk exercises specific discretion under the underwriting agreement, subject to the pricing committee's approval, to shape the shareholder base in specific ways.

**Long-only mutual funds.** The long-only bucket typically receives the largest allocation share — often 50–70% of the base deal for a healthy institutional-appetite deal. Long-only allocations are prioritised for aftermarket-quality reasons — long-only holders have long holding periods (3–10 years for the specific long-view funds), which supports aftermarket price stability and reduces the flip-oriented flow that hurts the first-day / first-week dynamics. The specific allocation within the long-only bucket weights the anchor accounts (the specific accounts that anchored the pilot-fishing conversations, the accounts that took the largest indications at the pricing) and gives specific allocations to the accounts the company wants to build long-term relationships with.

**Crossover funds.** The crossover / long-short-hedge-fund bucket typically receives 15–25% of the deal. Crossover funds hold longer than pure hedge funds but shorter than long-onlys — a 6-24-month typical holding period. Crossover-fund allocations are prioritised where the specific fund brings a specific-conviction thesis (Coatue tech-crossover, D1 Capital tech-and-consumer, Baillie-Gifford-adjacent-Whale-Rock etc.) that supports the aftermarket price target.

**Pure hedge funds.** The pure-hedge-fund bucket typically receives 5–15% of the deal, with specific weighting on the accounts most likely to hold rather than flip. The allocation-weighting for tender-offer participation and for accounts that participated in the pre-IPO cornerstone or that supported prior transactions is a specific relationship-building lever.

**Cornerstone allocations.** Where cornerstone commitments were made during pilot-fishing (chapter 3), the specific cornerstone allocations are honoured — the specific dollar commitments at the pricing become the specific share allocations. Cornerstone allocations are typically 20–40% of the deal for a deal with a large cornerstone presence, less for a deal with smaller or no cornerstones.

**Employee-directed share programme (EDSP).** Many IPOs include a specific reserved allocation for employees to purchase shares at the offering price. The EDSP is disclosed in the S-1, is subject to specific eligibility criteria (typically all employees or a specific longer-tenured subset), and is subject to specific FINRA and SEC rules on new-issue-offering allocations. The EDSP allocation is typically 1–5% of the deal. EDSP participants are subject to the same 180-day lock-up as insiders (chapter 6). The EDSP is an employee-relations lever — it lets employees participate in the offering at the IPO price rather than paying the aftermarket price — and is a specific pre-IPO board decision.

**Friends-and-family (F&F) programme.** Some IPOs include a specific reserved allocation for the friends and family of founders, executives, directors, and specific significant employees. The F&F programme is subject to specific FINRA Rule 5130 and Rule 5131 restrictions — "restricted persons" (specific broker-dealer personnel, portfolio managers, and other categories) generally cannot participate in new-issue offerings, and the F&F program is designed to fit within the specific eligibility framework. F&F allocations are typically small (0.5–2% of the deal) and are administered through the syndicate desk with specific paper-trail discipline.

**Retail allocations.** Traditional IPOs allocate the vast majority of the offering to institutional accounts, with retail participation limited to whatever the syndicate desk chooses to allocate through specific broker-dealer retail networks. In recent years, specific retail-access platforms (Robinhood IPO Access, SoFi Invest IPO Investing, TD Ameritrade / Charles Schwab retail-access programs) have opened specific retail-allocation channels — the specific retail-allocation percentage varies by deal but is typically small (1–5% of the base deal). Retail allocations are more common on high-demand consumer-brand deals and less common on enterprise-software deals.

## FINRA Rule 5130 and Rule 5131 — new-issue-offering restrictions

Two FINRA rules govern who can and cannot receive new-issue-offering allocations, with specific implications for the allocation methodology.

**Rule 5130.** Prohibits FINRA-member firms from selling new-issue offerings to "restricted persons" — broker-dealers, broker-dealer personnel, portfolio managers of institutional accounts, immediate family members of specified persons, and other specified categories. The rule is designed to prevent conflicts of interest in the allocation of hot new-issue offerings. Compliance discipline requires that each purchaser (including EDSP and F&F participants) certify their eligibility.

**Rule 5131.** Prohibits FINRA-member firms from allocating new-issue offerings to executive officers or directors of investment-banking clients or companies expected to become investment-banking clients ("spinning" prohibition). Rule 5131 also imposes specific disclosure and process requirements on the allocation methodology to prevent quid-pro-quo allocations. The rule shapes the syndicate desk's discretion — specific relationships that could be viewed as quid-pro-quo require specific disclosure and specific eligibility discipline.

The specific practitioner discipline is that the syndicate desk maintains a specific paper trail for every allocation, tests each purchaser against the Rule 5130 / 5131 eligibility framework, and documents the specific rationale for the allocation methodology in a form defensible to FINRA and SEC review.

## The specific aftermarket-quality-vs-demand-clearing trade-off

The allocation methodology trades off two objectives that can conflict.

**Aftermarket quality.** A shareholder base heavy on fundamental long-only investors with long holding periods produces a stable aftermarket price with low first-week volatility. Long-only holders do not flip, do not create the specific selling pressure that produces first-week price drops, and do not require specific stabilisation support from the syndicate desk. The aftermarket-quality objective argues for a heavy long-only allocation.

**Demand clearing.** A book that excludes hedge-fund demand may not clear at the target price — the hedge funds that would have bought at the range midpoint are the marginal demand that supports the specific pricing. Excluding them entirely can force a lower pricing or a smaller deal size.

**The synthesis.** The typical allocation methodology weights long-only accounts heavily (50–70%), gives specific allocations to the crossover accounts that fit the aftermarket-quality frame (15–25%), gives smaller allocations to hedge funds (5–15%) with weighting toward the accounts most likely to hold, and honours the cornerstone / EDSP / F&F commitments (5–15% combined). The specific methodology is documented in the syndicate desk's allocation memo and is approved by the pricing committee.

**The "priced-above-range" and first-day pop.** A deal priced above the range with a healthy first-day pop (typically 10–25% above the offering price) is the industry-standard "well-priced deal" outcome — the offering priced at a level that reflects strong demand, the aftermarket confirms the pricing with continued demand, and the underwriter's-preferred-account book made money on the specific allocations. A deal with a first-day pop above 50% is a specific under-pricing signal — the syndicate desk priced below where the market cleared, and the money that could have flowed to the company flowed instead to the day-one allocated accounts. The specific 15–25% pop is the practitioner-known "sweet spot" for a well-priced deal.

## The pricing committee's specific work-product

The pricing committee's specific decisions are documented in a specific set of artefacts that become part of the closing record.

**The pricing-committee consent.** A written consent (executed by all pricing-committee members) approving the specific price per share, the specific offering size, the specific greenshoe size, and the specific allocation-methodology approval. The consent references the underwriting agreement, the FINRA Rule 5110 filing, and the specific board resolutions that delegated the pricing authority.

**The final S-1 amendment / pricing prospectus.** The Rule 424(b) pricing prospectus with the specific price and size disclosed.

**The allocation record.** The specific list of accounts, the specific allocations to each, the specific Rule 5130 / 5131 eligibility confirmations, and the specific rationale documentation.

**The underwriting-agreement counterparts.** Executed signatures from the issuer, the selling stockholders (if any), and each underwriter in the syndicate.

**The comfort letters and legal opinions.** The auditor's comfort letter (delivered at pricing and updated at closing), the issuer counsel's legal opinion, and the underwriter counsel's opinion — the specific transaction-closing package.

**The closing bringdowns.** The specific certificates delivered at the T+2 closing confirming the underwriting-agreement reps and warranties are true as of closing.

## Summary

The pricing meeting on T-1 evening is where the entire IPO workstream converges into a specific price and size. The initial pricing range is set at 15–20% below the anticipated pricing to preserve optionality; range up-revisions and down-revisions during the roadshow are made within the Rule 430A 20% spread. The pricing committee (a board-delegated authority) reviews the closed book, considers the specific fundraising goal and the specific aftermarket-signal target, and approves the specific price, offering size, and greenshoe size. Allocation is the parallel decision about who owns the offered securities — the buckets are long-only (50–70%), crossover (15–25%), pure hedge fund (5–15%), cornerstone (as pre-committed), employee-directed share programme (1–5%), friends-and-family (0.5–2%), and retail (1–5%). The specific allocation methodology trades off aftermarket-quality (fundamental long-only holders don't flip) against demand-clearing (hedge-fund demand supports the specific pricing). FINRA Rules 5130 and 5131 govern the eligibility of specific purchasers and the prohibition on spinning. The 15–25% first-day pop is the practitioner-known well-priced-deal sweet spot; above 50% signals under-pricing that left money on the table. The pricing-committee consent, the Rule 424(b) pricing prospectus, the allocation record, the underwriting-agreement counterparts, and the comfort letters and legal opinions constitute the closing package.

Chapter 5 turns to Regulation M stabilisation and the greenshoe over-allotment — the specific first-30-day aftermarket regime under which the syndicate manager (typically the lead-left) manages the offering's post-pricing dynamics.
