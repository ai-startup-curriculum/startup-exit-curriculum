# Testing the Waters and Gun-Jumping Compliance under the JOBS Act

## Why this matters

The Securities Act of 1933 prohibits *offers* to sell securities before a registration statement is filed and *sales* before the registration statement is effective. Section 5(a) prohibits sales before effectiveness; Section 5(b) prohibits use of a non-prospectus writing to offer securities after filing; Section 5(c) prohibits offers of any kind before filing. The prohibitions are known collectively as the **gun-jumping** rules, and they apply to every issuer registering securities with the SEC — not just IPO issuers. The word *offer* is defined broadly under Section 2(a)(3): any communication that is reasonably likely to condition the market for the security is an offer, whether or not it names the security or a price. A CEO who publishes a Medium post about the company's five-year growth vision three weeks before the S-1 files can create a Section 5(c) offer, which is a rescission-liability event under Section 12(a)(1) — the buyer of stock in the subsequent IPO can, in principle, demand a refund of the offering price.

Congress recognised that the gun-jumping prohibitions structurally excluded emerging-growth companies from pre-filing conversations with institutional investors that could inform whether the IPO was viable and at what price range. The **JOBS Act** (Jumpstart Our Business Startups Act of 2012) added Section 5(d) to the Securities Act to create the **testing-the-waters** (TTW) privilege — an EGC (or, since 2019, any issuer) may communicate with qualified institutional buyers and institutional accredited investors before or during the SEC-review period to gauge interest in the offering. The privilege is a specific, narrow carve-out from the Section 5(c) offer prohibition — the CFO who reads the privilege as "I can now say what I want to whom I want" has misread it and will discover the misreading in a subsequent SEC comment letter or in a post-IPO plaintiff-firm demand letter.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline of TTW communications and the broader gun-jumping perimeter — what the CFO / CEO / GC can say, to whom, when, in what form, and under what compliance discipline. The chapter is intensively rule-bound because the rules are enforced. SEC enforcement actions and issuer voluntary rescission offers have followed pre-filing communications that crossed the line; the perimeter is not a matter of style but of specific-statute compliance.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal advice. Every specific TTW communication is authored and cleared by qualified securities counsel with a documented paper trail. The gun-jumping rules are personal-liability rules — Section 12(a)(1) rescission liability attaches to the issuer and to specific control-person defendants under Section 15.

## The three periods and what is allowed in each

The pre-effective S-1 life cycle divides into three periods, each with a specific communications regime.

**Pre-filing period.** Runs from any point in the past through the moment the issuer files the initial S-1 (or the confidential DRS, in EGC filings under Section 6(e)). During this period, Section 5(c) prohibits *any offer* — no marketing communications, no pricing conversations, no investor presentations describing the offering. The narrow exceptions are: (1) ordinary-course business communications not related to the offering (Rule 168 safe harbour for reporting issuers; Rule 169 safe harbour for non-reporting issuers for factual business information already publicly available); (2) TTW communications under Section 5(d) to QIBs and IAIs; (3) Rule 135 announcements — a limited-content pre-filing announcement stating the issuer's intention to file (name, offering type, offering amount, anticipated timing, and specific-required disclaimers), no more; (4) research reports from unaffiliated broker-dealers under Rule 137 / 138 / 139 safe harbours; (5) FAQ-style employee and customer communications that stay factual and do not condition the market. The perimeter is narrow and the practitioner discipline is *when in doubt, do not publish*.

**Post-filing / pre-effective period (the "waiting period").** Runs from the S-1 filing through the moment the SEC declares the registration statement effective. Once the S-1 is on file, oral offers are permitted (Section 5(b)(1) prohibits only *written* offers that are not prospectuses meeting Section 10 requirements). Written offers must be made using the preliminary prospectus (the "red herring," so-called because of the historically required red disclaimer on the cover). Free-writing prospectuses (FWPs) under Rule 433 are permitted for post-filing written communications outside the preliminary prospectus — subject to specific filing, legend, and disclosure requirements. The roadshow slide deck is an FWP; the roadshow video (if a "bona fide electronic roadshow") is not required to be filed but must be equivalent to the prospectus in material respects.

**Post-effective period.** Runs from SEC effectiveness through completion of the distribution. Sales are permitted. The prospectus must accompany or precede all confirmations of sale (Section 5(b)(2)), practically satisfied by prospectus delivery through EDGAR access under Rule 172 for most modern deals. FWPs remain permitted with the same rules. The distribution completes when the syndicate stabilisation and greenshoe periods close (typically 30 days post-pricing under Reg M) — after distribution completes, the issuer transitions to the standard public-company Reg FD / Rule 10b-5 disclosure regime.

## The JOBS Act TTW privilege — Section 5(d) mechanics

Section 5(d) of the Securities Act, added by the JOBS Act in 2012 and expanded to all issuers by the FAST Act amendments and the SEC's 2019 Rule 163B, permits an issuer to engage in **oral or written communications with qualified institutional buyers (QIBs) or institutional accredited investors (IAIs) to gauge interest in a potential securities offering, before or after filing a registration statement**. The specific mechanics matter.

**Audience limit.** TTW communications may only be made to persons the issuer reasonably believes are QIBs (defined under Rule 144A: institutions owning and investing on a discretionary basis at least $100M in securities of non-affiliated issuers, with specific rules for broker-dealers, banks, insurance companies, investment companies) or IAIs (institutional accredited investors as defined under Rule 501(a) — banks, insurance companies, registered investment companies, business development companies, employee benefit plans meeting specified thresholds, corporations / partnerships / trusts with $5M+ in assets not formed for the purpose of acquiring the securities, and other specified institutional categories). The QIB / IAI limit is not a formality — the underwriter's institutional-sales-force qualification of each account before the TTW conversation is a specific compliance discipline that underwriter counsel enforces. TTW communications with retail investors are *not* permitted under Section 5(d); a communication with a retail investor is a Section 5(c) offer and creates gun-jumping exposure.

**No filing requirement (with an important caveat).** The Section 5(d) TTW communications themselves are not required to be filed with the SEC. However, SEC staff routinely request in the S-1 comment letter that the issuer produce copies of TTW materials and a list of persons contacted. The specific practitioner discipline is that the issuer maintains a documented paper trail — a written TTW deck with counsel-approved talking points, a log of every TTW meeting with the date / participants / firm / QIB-or-IAI qualification confirmation, and copies of any written materials shared. Underwriter counsel typically requires this paper trail as a condition of the offering.

**Content limits — the S-1 consistency rule.** TTW materials cannot say anything materially inconsistent with what will subsequently appear in the S-1. The practical rule: draft the TTW deck *after* the S-1 draft is in a stable form, or draft the TTW deck first and use it as an input into the S-1 narrative, but under no circumstance let the TTW materials drift from the S-1. Any specific claim in the TTW deck (a growth-rate projection, a market-sizing number, a customer-concentration statement, a competitive-positioning claim, a projected financial number) must be reproducible in the S-1 with the same or reconciling language. A TTW deck that says the addressable market is $50B and an S-1 that says the addressable market is $30B is a comment letter waiting to happen.

**Content limits — no specific pricing or terms.** TTW communications gauge *interest* in an offering. They do not offer securities at a specific price or with specific terms. A TTW meeting where the CFO says "we're targeting a $2B pre-money valuation and $250M raise, would you invest at that valuation?" is a marketed offer that steps outside the Section 5(d) safe harbour. The permitted conversation is more like: "we are considering an IPO in the next 6–12 months, we would appreciate your view on our positioning against the sector, and we would like to understand what disclosure content would help you underwrite the security." The valuation and pricing conversation happens during the roadshow (chapter 3), not during TTW.

**The 21-day cooling-off pattern.** Where the issuer files publicly (the "public S-1 flip" — either an initial public S-1, or the conversion of a confidential DRS to a public S-1 fifteen days before the roadshow, as required by the JOBS Act EGC provision), there is a specific practitioner convention of a cooling-off period between the most-recent TTW meeting and the initial public filing. The specific 30-day pattern — the last TTW meeting occurs 30 days before the initial public filing — is a practitioner cushion against arguments that specific TTW communications conditioned the market for the offering in an SEC-actionable way. The 30-day pattern is not a statutory requirement; it is a defensive-drafting discipline. Some deals compress the pattern (with counsel comfort), some extend it, and the SEC's own staff has never articulated a bright-line rule.

**The 15-day EGC public-filing rule.** Under JOBS Act Section 6(e), an EGC that files a confidential DRS must publicly file the S-1 at least 15 days before its road show (a specific SEC clarification places this under the 15-day rule with the road show meaning the first road show meeting with prospective investors). The 15-day rule ensures the market has access to the S-1 for a minimum period before institutional marketing begins. In practice, this means the confidential DRS filing runs through SEC review with amendments (typically 3–5 amendment cycles across 4–8 months), followed by a public filing that starts the 15-day clock, followed by the roadshow launch.

## The gun-jumping perimeter — Rule 163A, Rule 168, Rule 169, Rule 135

Beyond Section 5(d), several specific SEC rules define the pre-filing communications perimeter.

**Rule 163A — the 30-day pre-filing safe harbour.** Any communication made by or on behalf of an issuer *more than 30 days before* the filing of a registration statement is deemed not to constitute an offer under Section 5(c), *provided* the communication does not reference the securities offering. Rule 163A is the safe harbour that lets an issuer publish ordinary-course business communications, employee-town-hall content, product announcements, and press releases in the 30+ days pre-filing without gun-jumping exposure — subject to the specific "no offering reference" condition. The rule is a specific reason underwriter counsel typically requires the issuer to observe a 30-day pre-filing quiet period on any communication that could be construed as marketing.

**Rule 168 — the ongoing-communications safe harbour for reporting issuers.** For issuers that are already public (relevant in follow-on and secondary offerings, and in the confidential DRS phase of a first-time IPO where the issuer has other publicly-reporting securities), Rule 168 protects ongoing factual and forward-looking business information that is regularly released to the market. First-time IPO issuers are typically not "reporting issuers" and rely on Rule 169 instead.

**Rule 169 — the ongoing-communications safe harbour for non-reporting issuers.** For non-reporting issuers (the typical first-time IPO), Rule 169 protects *regularly released factual business information* intended for use by *persons other than in their capacities as investors or potential investors* — customers, suppliers, employees, partners. The forward-looking component protected under Rule 168 for reporting issuers is *not* protected under Rule 169 for non-reporting issuers; a non-reporting issuer in the pre-filing period cannot make forward-looking business projections in the ordinary-course communications and expect Rule 169 protection. This asymmetry is a specific reason first-time IPO issuers tighten their public communications in the pre-filing period.

**Rule 135 — the pre-filing announcement.** Rule 135 permits an issuer to make a limited-content pre-filing announcement of the intention to file a registration statement. The permitted content is narrow: the issuer's name, the type of securities to be offered, the anticipated amount, the anticipated timing, the anticipated exchange listing, and specific required disclaimers (that the announcement is not an offer, that any offer will be made only through a registration statement filed with the SEC). Rule 135 announcements are commonly used when the confidential DRS is filed (an EGC announcing that it has confidentially submitted for review) and when the S-1 is publicly filed. Anything beyond the Rule 135 content list — projected valuation, projected pricing, business commentary, use-of-proceeds narrative — is not protected and creates gun-jumping exposure.

**Rule 134 — the post-filing tombstone.** Rule 134 permits an expanded-content announcement *after* filing (post-filing, pre-effective). Rule 134 announcements can include additional content — the offering size, the identity of the underwriters, the anticipated pricing range, some biographical information about executive officers. The typical Rule 134 use case is the "tombstone" announcement of the offering placed in financial publications after filing.

## Research analyst rules — Rule 137 / 138 / 139 and FINRA Rule 2242

Sell-side research on the issuer and on comparable issuers during the offering window is subject to specific rules that protect the integrity of the offering.

**Rule 137 — research by non-participating broker-dealers.** A broker-dealer that is *not* participating in the underwriting syndicate may publish research on the issuer during the offering window if the research is a regular-course publication and the broker-dealer meets specified conditions. The rule preserves market research independence.

**Rule 138 — research on a different security of the issuer.** Underwriters and other participating broker-dealers may publish research on securities of the issuer *other than the specific security being offered* if the research is a regular-course publication meeting specified conditions. Rare in first-time IPO context (there is no "different security"); more relevant in follow-on and convertible-debt contexts.

**Rule 139 — research on the issuer or its industry by participating broker-dealers.** Participating broker-dealers may publish research on the issuer or its industry during the offering window if the research is a regular-course publication meeting specific conditions (the analyst's coverage of the issuer or the industry must have been regular before the offering; the specific coverage cannot be initiated on the deal). The rule protects sell-side research continuity through the offering window and is the reason a bank cannot "initiate coverage" on an issuer as part of the pitch — the initiation has to be part of a regular-course publication cycle.

**FINRA Rule 2242 — research analyst conflicts of interest.** FINRA Rule 2242 (the successor to NASD Rule 2711 and NYSE Rule 472) imposes specific restrictions on research analyst conduct during the offering window — no analyst participation in the pitch for the underwriting mandate, no analyst-communications with prospective institutional customers that could be construed as marketing the deal, a "quiet period" for research from underwriters in the deal that runs for a specified period after pricing (currently 10 days for lead-managers on IPOs of specified issuer size; 25 days for IPOs generally under the historical rules, with the specific current period governed by the current rule text — a specific point that underwriter counsel confirms on each deal). The quiet period matters because the research initiation from a participating underwriter is a specific aftermarket-launch signal; the analyst who initiates at Buy with a price target above the offering price is a materially positive aftermarket-launch signal, and the timing of that initiation is controlled by the rule.

**FINRA Rule 5121 — conflict-of-interest offering rules.** Where an underwriter has a conflict of interest under Rule 5121 (specific ownership or control relationships with the issuer), specific disclosure and process requirements attach. The rule shapes syndicate construction when specific banks have pre-IPO investment positions in the issuer.

## Publicity control during the offering window

The perimeter is enforced through a specific set of publicity-control disciplines maintained by the CFO / GC / IR-head with underwriter counsel oversight.

**The publicity policy.** A written policy (typically drafted at the org meeting and circulated to all executives and to the communications team) governs what the company can publish, say, and post during the offering window. The policy prohibits: press releases beyond the Rule 135 / 134 permitted content list, executive interviews with financial press, participation in investor conferences not sponsored by the syndicate, social-media posts by executives about the company or the offering, employee-town-hall content that could leak, product announcements timed to condition the market. The policy permits: ordinary-course product and customer announcements (subject to specific content review), regular-cadence employee-communications (subject to review that they are ordinary-course and not offering-related), regulatory-required filings, and TTW meetings authorised by counsel with the specific paper-trail discipline described above.

**The "no publicity" waiver.** Some pre-offering-period announcements may have compelling business reasons — a major customer win, a leadership hire, a partnership announcement, a product launch announced at an industry event long-scheduled before the offering. The specific practitioner discipline is that any such announcement is reviewed by underwriter counsel against the Rule 168 / 169 safe harbours and against the specific Rule 135 permitted content, and either fits within an exception, is deferred, or is announced with specific disclaimers and specific content controls. The decision is documented in the underwriter working-group record.

**The FAQ / Q&A discipline.** The issuer maintains a written FAQ / Q&A document that covers the specific questions likely to arise from employees, customers, partners, press, and other stakeholders during the offering window. Every answer is drafted by the communications team, reviewed by GC and by underwriter counsel, and is *consistent with the S-1*. The Q&A is used to script executive responses to questions in customer meetings, industry events, and employee town halls. The specific discipline: an executive who is asked a question during the offering window answers with the Q&A-approved answer or defers ("we cannot comment on that during the offering window; please refer to our S-1 on the SEC EDGAR system"). The specific discipline is not "stop talking to people" — it is "answer with the specific approved language."

**Social media discipline.** Executive social-media accounts (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, Medium, YouTube, TikTok, Bluesky) are a specific gun-jumping risk. The specific discipline is that executives and the corporate accounts observe a written social-media policy during the offering window — no offering-related posts, no market-conditioning content, no responses to questions about the offering, no "look-what-we're-doing" posts that could be construed as marketing the securities. The founder who tweets "excited to announce something big next month 🚀" three weeks before pricing has created a specific gun-jumping question that counsel now has to address.

## Free-writing prospectuses (FWPs) — Rule 433

After filing, the issuer and the underwriters use free-writing prospectuses to make written communications about the offering that are not part of the preliminary prospectus. FWPs are governed by Rule 433 with specific requirements.

**Filing requirement.** Most FWPs prepared by or on behalf of the issuer must be filed with the SEC on the day of first use. Certain electronic roadshows for common-equity offerings that are "bona fide electronic roadshows" available to any interested party may be excluded from the filing requirement.

**Legend requirement.** FWPs must include a specific legend directing recipients to the preliminary prospectus and stating that the issuer has filed a registration statement with the SEC.

**Consistency with the prospectus.** FWPs cannot contain information materially inconsistent with the prospectus. As with TTW materials, the consistency discipline is enforced by counsel drafting and review.

**The roadshow deck as FWP.** The specific roadshow deck used in one-on-one and small-group meetings during the marketing period is an FWP. It is filed with the SEC (or hosted on a compliant platform such as NetRoadshow or RetailRoadshow with specific access controls). The deck is drafted with content, legends, and consistency-with-S-1 discipline; every specific claim in the deck is reproducible in the S-1.

## The CFO's specific workstream — what to run

The gun-jumping regime translates into a specific workstream the CFO owns during the pre-marketing and marketing periods.

**Publicity policy adoption.** Draft the publicity policy at the org meeting, circulate to the executive team and the communications and marketing organisations, run a training session covering the specific dos and don'ts, and require written acknowledgement of receipt.

**TTW packet authoring.** Coordinate with the lead-left and joint book-runners to author the TTW packet — the QIB / IAI target list (typically 40–100 accounts across long-only, hedge-fund, sovereign-wealth, and specific-strategic categories), the TTW deck with counsel-approved talking points, the meeting logistics (in-person vs. virtual, agenda, one-on-one vs. small-group), and the paper-trail discipline (meeting log, QIB / IAI qualification confirmation, materials-distribution log).

**FAQ / Q&A maintenance.** Maintain the FAQ / Q&A document as a living artefact — every new question that comes up in an employee town hall, a customer meeting, an industry event, or a press inbound gets logged, answered by counsel, and added to the master Q&A. The Q&A is the source of truth for executive scripting.

**Cornerstone / anchor-investor conversations (where used).** Where the issuer intends to run a cornerstone process — a specific pre-pricing commitment from one or more anchor investors to purchase a specified allocation at the IPO price — the cornerstone conversations are TTW communications and follow the TTW discipline. Cornerstone commitments are disclosed in the prospectus and are subject to specific disclosure treatment.

**FWP inventory and filing.** Maintain the FWP inventory across the marketing period — every written communication the issuer or the underwriters make about the offering, tracked, cleared, filed as required, and consistent with the S-1.

**Social-media monitoring.** Monitor executive and corporate social-media accounts during the offering window and enforce the social-media policy in real time.

## The consequences of getting it wrong — enforcement and rescission

The gun-jumping rules are enforced. The specific consequences of a Section 5(c) violation include:

**Section 12(a)(1) rescission liability.** A purchaser of securities offered in violation of Section 5 has a right to rescission — to return the securities and demand a refund of the offering price plus interest. In a subsequent IPO where a pre-filing communication conditioned the market, in principle every buyer in the offering has this right for one year from the sale. In practice, the exposure is usually addressed through a voluntary rescission offer — the issuer offers to buy back the securities from each buyer, effectively refunding the offering — or through a delayed offering to let the market-conditioning effect dissipate.

**SEC enforcement.** SEC staff can seek an injunction, cease-and-desist order, disgorgement, and civil penalties. Enforcement against gun-jumping is not routine but has happened in specific cases where the conduct was flagrant. More common than SEC enforcement is the practical enforcement mechanism: SEC comment letters that delay effectiveness, and buy-side or plaintiff-firm attention that raises the deal-execution risk.

**Common law and Section 11 / Section 12(a)(2) liability.** Where a pre-filing communication is materially misleading, additional liability under the anti-fraud provisions may attach. The gun-jumping discipline runs in parallel with the Rule 10b-5 anti-fraud discipline.

The practitioner takeaway is that the gun-jumping perimeter is not a compliance formality — it is a specific liability perimeter, and the specific communications discipline described above is the practitioner mechanism for staying inside it.

## Summary

The Securities Act's gun-jumping rules prohibit offers before filing (Section 5(c)) and written non-prospectus offers before effectiveness (Section 5(b)), enforced by Section 12(a)(1) rescission liability. The JOBS Act's Section 5(d) testing-the-waters privilege permits oral and written communications with QIBs and IAIs to gauge interest, subject to specific audience limits, S-1 consistency requirements, no-specific-pricing content limits, and defensive-drafting cooling-off patterns. The pre-filing communications perimeter is defined by Rule 163A (30-day pre-filing safe harbour), Rule 168 / 169 (ordinary-course communications safe harbours), Rule 135 (limited pre-filing announcements), and Rule 134 (post-filing announcements). Research analyst conduct is governed by Rules 137 / 138 / 139 and FINRA Rule 2242 (research quiet periods). The CFO's specific workstream includes publicity-policy adoption, TTW packet authoring with QIB / IAI qualification and paper-trail discipline, FAQ / Q&A maintenance, cornerstone-conversation choreography, FWP inventory and filing, and social-media monitoring. The consequences of getting it wrong are rescission liability, SEC enforcement, and — most operationally — SEC comment-letter delay and deal-execution risk. The perimeter is enforced by counsel drafting and review of every specific communication that touches the offering window.
