# The Underwriter Bake-Off — Lead-Left Selection, Syndicate Construction, and Gross-Spread Economics

## Why this matters

The underwriter selection is the single most consequential vendor decision a founder-CEO / CFO / GC makes in the going-public transaction. The lead-left investment bank drives the S-1 drafting posture, the SEC comment-letter engagement, the valuation range that anchors the roadshow, the specific institutional buyers who get one-on-one time with management, the allocation methodology that determines who owns the stock in the first week (and therefore what happens to the aftermarket), the greenshoe execution, and the sell-side research analyst who initiates coverage at the end of the twenty-five-day post-pricing quiet period. A tier-1 bank on the lead-left signals a specific market posture to the buy-side; a specialist boutique signals a different one. The founder who selects underwriters by "which pitch was most polished" or "who our board director introduced us to" without running a structured bake-off is a founder who inherits the underwriter's map rather than mapping the underwriter.

The bake-off is also the moment at which the *economics* of the transaction are set. The **gross spread** — the underwriter's compensation, taken as a discount from the offering price rather than as a separate invoice — is typically 6–7% of the deal for a $100M–$500M IPO in the US, with the split roughly 20% management fee / 20% underwriting fee / 60% selling concession (the FINRA Rule 5110 framework and the SEC S-1 disclosure regime make the spread visible on the cover of the prospectus). A 7% gross spread on a $300M deal is $21M to the syndicate; a well-negotiated 6.25% spread on the same deal is $18.75M — a $2.25M working-capital difference that flows to the balance sheet. The bake-off is where that negotiation happens, and the founder who does not know the spread convention leaves money on the table.

The right output of this chapter is the practitioner discipline of running the bake-off, reading the pitch books, choosing the lead-left, constructing the co-manager syndicate, and negotiating the engagement letter with eyes open to the specific incentive geometry each bank brings to the deal.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / investment-banking advice. A live underwriter selection engages qualified securities counsel and the CFO's finance-function bench. The gross-spread convention updates with market conditions; verify against the FINRA Rule 5110 filings and against recent comparable-deal prospectuses before relying on any specific number.

## The syndicate structure — who does what

The US underwriter syndicate is a hierarchical structure with specific role divisions that determine both economics and workload.

**Lead-left.** The single bank listed on the left-most position of the prospectus cover. In practice, this bank runs the deal — drives the S-1 drafting from the underwriter side, hosts the organisational meeting, chairs the underwriter working-group calls, runs the analyst-day, choreographs the roadshow, prices the deal, allocates the book, and runs the stabilisation and greenshoe in the aftermarket. The lead-left's franchise is what the buy-side institutional community reads first; a book-running underwriter with a strong sales-and-trading desk and deep buy-side relationships materially affects the quality of the demand curve. For a $200M+ IPO, the lead-left is almost always a tier-1 bulge-bracket firm (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Barclays) — with Goldman and Morgan Stanley the historical market leaders in tech and healthcare IPOs by both deal count and league-table volume across the twenty years of Meritech and Renaissance Capital tracking.

**Joint-lead-left / joint book-runners.** For deals of meaningful size (typically $200M+) it is now standard to have two or three joint book-runners, all listed with parity positioning on the cover (the specific ordering left-to-right still matters — the actual lead-left drives the operational work; the joint book-runners share economics and share credit but do not share workstream ownership). The two-book-runner and three-book-runner structures let the issuer stack complementary franchises — Goldman + JPMorgan + Morgan Stanley on a large tech IPO gets the issuer three tier-1 sales-forces and three tier-1 research franchises, at the cost of a more complex working group.

**Co-managers.** The banks listed on the prospectus cover *below* the book-runners. Co-managers do not run the deal — they do not drive drafting, they do not chair the roadshow, they do not price the deal. They contribute their sales-force distribution to the marketing effort, they research-cover the stock in the aftermarket (a co-manager research analyst who initiates at Buy is a real aftermarket-quality input), and they participate in the underwriting economics at a reduced-percentage share of the gross spread. Co-managers are typically selected for specific complementary reach — a diversity-owned firm (Ramirez & Co., Loop Capital, Siebert Williams Shank, Blaylock Van, Cabrera Capital), a sector-specialist boutique with a strong analyst franchise in the issuer's sub-sector, a regional coverage firm, or a bank with a specific institutional-investor-relationship strength the book-runners lack (e.g., a specialist mid-market growth-investor bench).

**Underwriting counsel.** Separate from the underwriter economics but functionally part of the syndicate, underwriting counsel is the law firm that represents the underwriters — typically Davis Polk, Simpson Thacher, Skadden, Latham & Watkins, Ropes & Gray, or Kirkland & Ellis in tech and healthcare deals — as distinct from issuer's counsel (Wilson Sonsini, Cooley, Fenwick, Goodwin, Latham, Davis Polk depending on the firm's assignment). Underwriting counsel drives due-diligence discipline, comfort-letter negotiation with the auditor, and the underwriter's liability perimeter under Section 11 of the Securities Act.

## The bake-off — how to run one

The bake-off is a structured selection process typically run over 4–8 weeks in the pre-organisational-meeting phase, chaired by the CFO with the CEO / GC / board-selected transaction committee in attendance.

**Long-list construction.** The finance function and the board draw up a long list of 6–10 potential lead-left candidates. The long list is informed by the banker informal-advice conversations that ran in the readiness phase (mod-107 chapter 1), by recent-comparable-deal league-table leadership (Renaissance Capital and Bloomberg publish quarterly league tables by deal count, deal size, and sector), by board-director and investor-network recommendations, and by the specific franchises the issuer wants. The long list should include at least one boutique / sector specialist as a stress test on the tier-1 assumption; if the long list is 100% bulge-bracket, the selection has already been made by omission.

**RFP and pitch-book request.** The CFO sends a written RFP to the long-list banks. The RFP asks for a pitch book covering: (1) the bank's sector coverage and specific-team credentials (which MDs, which analysts, which recent-deal proof points), (2) the bank's proposed valuation range for the issuer with a sourced comp-set analysis, (3) the bank's roadshow plan (target cities, target investors, cornerstone-conversation approach), (4) the bank's proposed syndicate construction, (5) the bank's aftermarket-support commitment (specific research analyst who would cover, sales-force training, market-making commitment), and (6) the bank's proposed gross-spread economics. The RFP is a competitive tension device — banks that know they are in a competitive process invest materially in the pitch book, invest their most-senior bankers in the pitch, and negotiate on economics.

**Short-list to 4–6 banks.** After the pitch books arrive, the CFO short-lists to 4–6 banks who each get an in-person pitch meeting with the CEO / CFO / GC / board transaction committee. The pitch meeting is 60–90 minutes, structured to test the pitch-book claims — specific questions on the analyst's sector conviction, specific reference calls to prior-deal issuers, specific tests on the salesforce's institutional coverage. The pitch meeting is also where the founder learns whether the specific senior banker who would run *this* deal (as opposed to the senior banker who did the pitch) is actually the person on the ground; a bank that pitches with the sector-head MD and staffs the deal with a first-year VP is a specific pattern the CFO diligences against.

**Reference calls.** For each short-list bank, the CFO conducts 3–5 reference calls with CFOs of companies the bank took public in the prior 24 months. The reference-call script asks: Did the analyst deliver the coverage promised? Did the salesforce work the book? Did the stabilisation and greenshoe execute cleanly? Did the aftermarket research initiations arrive on schedule at the price target promised? Did the syndicate hold together in a difficult market moment? Were the after-close relationships (subsequent follow-on offerings, convert issuance, M&A advisory) offered on competitive terms? Reference calls are the single most-underweighted input into underwriter selection — the pitch is a marketing document; the reference call is the operating truth.

**Selection meeting and the signal to the losers.** The transaction committee selects the lead-left, joint book-runners, and initial co-manager slate in a decision meeting. The CFO calls each losing bank personally within 24 hours of the decision — the specific market convention is that losing banks are told which bank was selected as lead-left, which are joint book-runners, and (importantly) which co-manager slots may still be open. Many losing lead-left candidates accept a co-manager slot on the deal; the syndicate stacking benefits from the additional sales-force and additional research analyst, and the losing bank preserves the issuer relationship for future work. The "you were second on our list" call is not a courtesy — it is a real market-standard signal that keeps the door open for future transactions.

## What to look for in the pitch book — the six diligence dimensions

Every pitch book covers approximately the same content in approximately the same order. The practitioner reads the pitch book against six specific diligence dimensions.

**Industry expertise.** The specific senior banker's sector coverage depth — how many transactions in the sub-sector, how many years in coverage, which specific issuers the banker has advised. A senior banker who has covered enterprise-SaaS-observability for 15 years and taken 12 companies public in that sub-sector is a materially different asset than a generalist tech banker who last covered SaaS three years ago. The pitch book claims are tested in the reference calls.

**Comparable-transaction credentials.** The specific IPOs the bank has priced in the issuer's sub-sector in the prior 24 months, with the specific aftermarket performance of each. A bank that priced three deals in the issuer's sub-sector, all of which are trading above issue 12 months later, has a specific credential. A bank that priced three deals in the sub-sector, two of which are trading below issue, has a different credential. The Renaissance Capital and PitchBook databases let the CFO verify the pitch-book claims independently.

**Research-analyst reputation and reach.** The specific sell-side research analyst who would cover the issuer post-quiet-period. The analyst's Institutional Investor All-America rankings (or the McLagan / Extel / Greenwich rankings in other markets) are the practitioner shorthand for institutional-buy-side respect. A top-3-ranked analyst in the sub-sector initiating coverage at Buy is a materially different aftermarket-launch signal than an unranked analyst initiating at Hold. The pitch book states the specific analyst; the CFO reads the analyst's recent research on comparable issuers to test the sector thesis alignment; the reference calls test whether the analyst delivered the promised coverage on prior deals. FINRA Rule 2242 (research analyst rules) and the SEC-cleared research-and-marketing-review process shape what the analyst can and cannot say during the deal — the pitch conversation about analyst coverage is subject to those rules and the marketing process makes them concrete.

**Sales-force distribution.** The specific institutional-sales coverage the bank offers. A bank with 40 institutional-equity salespeople covering long-only, hedge-fund, and sovereign-wealth accounts across US, Europe, and Asia has different reach than a bank with 12 salespeople covering US-only accounts. The pitch book states the coverage; the CFO tests it against the target-investor list the CFO wants approached (the buy-side account list the CFO would ideally see in the book — Fidelity, Wellington, T. Rowe Price, Capital Group, BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, Franklin Templeton, Baillie Gifford, D1 Capital, Coatue, Tiger Global, GIC, Temasek, and the specific sector-focused funds relevant to the sub-sector).

**Aftermarket-support commitment.** The specific commitments the bank makes on aftermarket support beyond the required syndicate stabilisation — market-making commitment, research-initiation timing and price target, post-IPO conferences and non-deal roadshows offered to the newly-public company, follow-on-offering-execution posture. The commitments are aspirational in the pitch book; the reference calls test whether the bank delivered on the aspirations for prior issuers.

**Cornerstone-and-tender participation history.** For issuers running a pre-IPO cornerstone process (typical in Asia-Pacific listings, increasingly appearing in US mega-IPOs) or an employee-directed-share-programme or a friends-and-family programme, the bank's operational capability and prior experience with these adjacent workstreams is a specific pitch-book claim. The CFO who is not planning to run a cornerstone or EDSP can skip this dimension; the CFO who is planning either should not.

## Tier-1 bulge-bracket vs. sector boutique vs. Big-Four alternative

The lead-left decision is often framed as tier-1-bulge-bracket vs. sector-boutique. Both frames have defensible answers depending on issuer specifics.

**Tier-1 bulge-bracket.** Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, and Barclays each have deep tech / healthcare / consumer / fintech / industrials coverage benches, deep institutional-sales-and-trading distribution, top-3-ranked analyst benches in most sub-sectors, and multi-decade franchises with the buy-side institutional community. For a $200M+ tech / healthcare / consumer / fintech IPO with a majority-US institutional target book, the base-case lead-left is a tier-1 bulge-bracket firm and the case for going elsewhere needs specific justification. Goldman and Morgan Stanley in particular dominate tech-IPO league tables across the twenty-year lookback; the "Goldman-and-Morgan-Stanley" pairing is close to a default for large tech deals.

**Sector boutique.** Firms with narrow-and-deep sector coverage — Cowen (biotech / life sciences before the TD acquisition), Piper Sandler (biotech, financial services, industrials), Stifel (financial services, healthcare, industrials), Jefferies (broad tech / healthcare / consumer with a boutique execution culture despite the bulge-bracket-scale balance sheet), Evercore ISI (research-forward with growing IB), Guggenheim Securities (broad sector coverage with a research-first posture), Raymond James (financials, healthcare), Baird (industrials, consumer, healthcare), William Blair (industrials, healthcare, technology). A sector boutique on the lead-left is defensible when the boutique's sector-analyst franchise is meaningfully deeper than the bulge-bracket alternatives — a biotech IPO where the boutique's biotech analyst is the ranked-#1 in the sub-sector and the bulge-bracket analyst is ranked #7 is a specific case for the boutique lead-left. Boutique lead-lefts are more common in biotech and specialty healthcare, in industrials and specialty financials, and in specific media / consumer sub-sectors than in horizontal SaaS or fintech.

**Big-Four accounting firm as underwriter (rare in the US, common in some cross-border deals).** The Big-Four accounting firms (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG) do not underwrite US IPOs — the auditor-independence rules under SEC Regulation S-X and the AICPA / PCAOB independence framework preclude the auditor from also being the underwriter (the specific concept: the auditor cannot audit its own advocacy of the offering). The auditor is a separate workstream. The phrase "Big-Four alternative" in the underwriter-selection context typically refers to using a large accounting firm for the auditor role and using a separate underwriter for the offering; the specific decision "which auditor" is upstream in mod-107 chapter 3, and the CFO who is trying to compress the two workstreams is fixing the wrong problem.

The tier-1-vs-boutique choice does not have a universal answer — it has a specific answer per issuer per window. The bake-off is the mechanism for arriving at that answer.

## Gross-spread economics — the 6–7% convention

The underwriter compensation on a US IPO is structured as a **gross spread** — a discount from the public offering price rather than a separately invoiced fee. The convention for a $100M–$500M IPO in the US is **6–7% gross spread**, with the specific figure typically 7% for smaller deals (under $150M) and negotiable down toward 6% or slightly below for larger deals (over $300M). For mega-IPOs ($1B+), the gross spread compresses further — 4–5% on deals like Facebook (1.1% on the base offering plus fee), Alibaba (~1.2%), and Uber (~2.5%) — reflecting the underwriter's willingness to accept a lower percentage on a very large absolute-dollar deal. For sub-$100M IPOs, the gross spread often exceeds 7% and can reach 8% for smaller deals with more retail-sales-force effort per dollar raised.

The gross spread is disclosed on the front cover of the prospectus (a specific SEC S-1 requirement) and in the underwriting section (Item 508 of Regulation S-K). The specific breakdown — how much goes to the management fee, the underwriting fee, and the selling concession — is disclosed in the underwriting agreement filed as an exhibit to the S-1. The convention is roughly:

- **Management fee** — 20% of the gross spread. Compensation for the book-runners' management of the offering process.
- **Underwriting fee** — 20% of the gross spread. Compensation for the underwriters' commitment to purchase and reoffer the securities (the firm-commitment underwriting risk).
- **Selling concession** — 60% of the gross spread. Compensation paid to the syndicate members based on shares actually sold. This is where co-managers' economics live; a co-manager's participation percentage in the selling concession is often 1–3% of the deal for a smaller co-manager and 5–10% for a larger participation.

The gross-spread negotiation happens in the engagement letter drafting, typically with issuer's counsel driving specific language. Levers include: the base gross-spread percentage (6.25% vs. 7% on a $300M deal is $2.25M), the greenshoe economics (whether the greenshoe pays the full gross spread or a reduced spread), reimbursable-expense caps (the underwriter's expenses are separately reimbursable subject to negotiated caps — travel, counsel, marketing costs), and specific fee credits (some banks offer fee credits for prior M&A advisory work or for financing commitments in the pre-IPO round). The CFO who negotiates the engagement letter with a benchmarked view of the current spread convention (from recent comparable-deal prospectuses and from the FINRA Rule 5110 filings that public-filed the underwriting arrangements) has meaningful leverage; the CFO who accepts the first draft leaves basis points on the table.

**FINRA Rule 5110 — the underwriting-compensation review.** Every US public offering underwriting arrangement is filed with FINRA under Rule 5110 for a review of the underwriting compensation for fairness and reasonableness. The FINRA review looks at aggregate underwriter compensation (gross spread plus reimbursable expenses plus any securities issued to the underwriter). The rule imposes specific caps and prohibitions — the CFO does not need to run the FINRA analysis (underwriting counsel does) but should understand that a specific arrangement was cleared under Rule 5110 and appears on the FINRA public database.

## Syndicate construction — the diversity co-manager, the regional arm, the growth-investor boutique

Beyond the lead-left and joint book-runners, the syndicate is constructed by adding co-managers with specific complementary reach. The three most common co-manager patterns:

**Diversity co-manager.** A minority-owned or women-owned broker-dealer added to the syndicate for specific institutional-investor reach, board-diversity-signal purposes, or CalPERS / CalSTRS / New York State Common / other public-pension-investor requirements. The specific firms include Ramirez & Co. (Latino-owned), Loop Capital Markets (Black-owned), Siebert Williams Shank (Black- and women-owned), Blaylock Van (Black-owned), CastleOak Securities (Black-owned), Cabrera Capital Markets (Latino-owned), and several other MWBE-certified firms. The specific reach is real — public pension funds and specific ESG-mandate funds hold allocations open for MWBE-broker-directed subscriptions — and the syndicate-diversity signal is a real board-and-investor conversation. Most tier-1-lead-led deals of meaningful size now include at least one diversity co-manager.

**Regional-coverage arm.** A bank with strong coverage of a specific institutional-investor geography — for example, a US-lead-led deal adding a European co-manager (BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, Credit Suisse pre-UBS, UBS post-Credit-Suisse) for the European long-only book, or an Asia-Pacific co-manager (Nomura, Mizuho, Daiwa, CLSA, DBS) for the Asian institutional book. The regional arm is most valuable when the issuer specifically wants a meaningful non-US institutional book (typical for large-cap deals, deals with substantial non-US operating footprint, or deals with cornerstone-investor interest from Asia-Pacific sovereign-wealth funds).

**Growth-investor-relationship boutique.** A boutique with specific coverage of the growth-investor / crossover-investor / hedge-fund community — the specific accounts most valuable in a hot deal (Tiger Global, Coatue, D1 Capital, Whale Rock, Lone Pine, Viking, Maverick, Discovery Capital, TCV, DST). Some boutiques (Piper Sandler, Cantor Fitzgerald, Needham, Oppenheimer, Wedbush, Roth Capital) have specific franchises with subsets of this community and are added to the syndicate for the specific cornerstone-conversation reach they enable.

The specific syndicate the CFO constructs is a mapping between the specific reach the issuer wants (which accounts, which sub-sectors, which geographies, which ESG mandates) and the specific franchises the co-manager banks offer. The mapping is documented in the engagement-letter drafting and is one of the specific board-approval agenda items in the organisational meeting.

## The engagement letter and the underwriting agreement — the two contracts

Two separate legal instruments govern the underwriter relationship.

**The engagement letter.** Signed at or near the organisational meeting. Names the syndicate (lead-left, joint book-runners, initial co-manager slate), the transaction (IPO of common stock, indicative offering size range, indicative use-of-proceeds), the compensation (gross-spread percentage or gross-spread range, reimbursable-expense treatment, specific fee credits), the exclusivity (the issuer commits to work exclusively with this syndicate on the transaction for a specific period), the indemnification (the issuer indemnifies the underwriters for specified deal-related claims subject to Section 11 / Section 12 / Section 17 backstops), and the termination (either party can terminate under specified conditions before pricing). The engagement letter is *not* the underwriting purchase commitment — the underwriters are not yet legally committed to purchase the securities. The engagement letter is negotiated by issuer's counsel and underwriting counsel with CFO / CEO / GC engagement.

**The underwriting agreement.** Signed at pricing (the T-1 to T evening at the pricing meeting). This is the firm-commitment purchase commitment — the underwriters agree to purchase the offered securities at the specified price and to resell them at the offering price. The underwriting agreement includes the greenshoe over-allotment option (typically 15% of the base deal), the reps and warranties from the issuer (paralleling the S-1 disclosure and Rule 10b-5 negotiated language), the comfort-letter deliverables from the auditor, the negative-assurance letters from counsel, and the conditions-precedent to the pricing (SEC effectiveness, no material adverse change, delivery of the prospectus and specified certificates). The underwriting agreement is signed at the pricing meeting after the pricing decision is made, and is the operative purchase commitment for the closing which occurs T+2 (a business-day settlement lag). ## The board handoff — what the board approves

The underwriter selection is a board-approval item. The typical board resolutions cover: (1) authorisation of the syndicate composition (lead-left, joint book-runners, co-managers), (2) authorisation of the engagement letter (with specific reference to the gross-spread economics, the reimbursable-expense treatment, and the exclusivity period), (3) delegation to management to negotiate the underwriting agreement within a specific price-and-size range that will be pre-approved by the board (or by the pricing committee — a subset of the board typically constituted to make the final pricing decision within pre-agreed parameters, since the pricing decision happens the evening of pricing and cannot wait for a full board meeting), (4) authorisation of specific ancillary agreements (research-support agreements, custody agreements, transfer-agent engagement).

The **pricing committee** is a specific board-governance construct. The board delegates to a committee of 2–4 directors (typically including the CEO or the board chair, the audit committee chair, and one or two other independent directors) the authority to approve the final pricing decision within pre-approved parameters (e.g., "price within a range of $18–$22 per share, with an offering size of 10–14 million shares and a greenshoe of up to 15% of the base offering"). The pricing committee meets the evening of pricing (typically 4–6 PM after the buy-side book has closed for the day), reviews the book, hears the lead-left's pricing recommendation, and approves the final price and size. The committee's authorisation is documented in a written consent that becomes part of the closing record. Mod-109 covers the transaction-governance mechanics of the pricing committee in depth.

## Summary

The underwriter selection is the highest-consequence vendor decision in the IPO — the lead-left drives S-1 drafting, valuation, roadshow choreography, allocation, and aftermarket support. The syndicate is a hierarchical structure with lead-left, joint book-runners, and co-managers each carrying specific economics and specific workload. The bake-off is a structured 4–8-week process — long-list construction, RFP with pitch-book request, short-list to 4–6 banks with in-person pitches, 3–5 reference calls per short-list bank, and a documented selection meeting with a professional call to the losers within 24 hours. The six diligence dimensions in the pitch book are industry expertise, comparable-transaction credentials, research-analyst reputation and reach, sales-force distribution, aftermarket-support commitment, and specific cornerstone / EDSP participation history. The tier-1 bulge-bracket lead-left is the base case for $200M+ tech / healthcare / consumer / fintech deals with US-institutional-majority target books; sector boutiques are defensible where their sub-sector analyst franchise is materially stronger. The gross-spread convention is 6–7% of the deal for $100M–$500M IPOs, compressing on mega-IPOs and expanding on sub-$100M deals, with roughly 20% management fee / 20% underwriting fee / 60% selling concession split. The FINRA Rule 5110 review clears the underwriting arrangements. The syndicate is constructed by adding co-managers for specific complementary reach — a diversity co-manager, a regional-coverage arm, and a growth-investor-relationship boutique are the three most common patterns. The engagement letter is signed at the organisational meeting; the firm-commitment underwriting agreement is signed at the pricing meeting. Board resolutions authorise the syndicate, the engagement letter, and the pricing-committee delegation.
