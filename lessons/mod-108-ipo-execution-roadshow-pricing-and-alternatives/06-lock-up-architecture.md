# Lock-Up Architecture — 180-Day Standard, Carve-Outs, and Staggered Releases

## Why this matters

The **lock-up agreement** is a contract signed by insiders, pre-IPO holders, and specific significant employees, in favour of the underwriters, prohibiting the signer from selling or transferring their shares for a specified period after the IPO. The standard US-IPO lock-up is **180 days**. The lock-up is not a legal requirement of the offering — it is a specific market-standard contract negotiated by the underwriters as a condition of taking the deal public, designed to prevent a specific first-6-month aftermarket dynamic in which pre-IPO holders dump their newly-liquid shares onto the aftermarket immediately post-IPO, flooding the market with supply, and breaking the price the underwriters just spent 6-8 weeks building demand for.

The lock-up matters because the specific supply-demand dynamic at lock-up expiry is a known-and-observable aftermarket event. Empirically, the median newly-IPO'd stock experiences a **specific negative price effect around lock-up expiry** — the aftermarket knows the specific lock-up expiry date, discounts the anticipated additional supply into the price in the weeks before expiry, absorbs the specific selling pressure in the days following expiry, and recovers over the subsequent weeks. The specific magnitude varies with deal specifics (hot deals absorb the supply more cleanly; soft deals can see specific price damage), but the pattern is consistent enough that the specific IR-and-communications workstream around lock-up expiry is a specific pre-planned discipline, not an ad-hoc response.

The lock-up architecture — the specific length, the specific covered parties, the specific carve-outs, the specific staggered-release patterns, the specific early-release discretion — is negotiated at the underwriting-agreement stage (with the specific lock-up terms filed as an exhibit to the S-1 and later disclosed in the final prospectus). The specific negotiation reflects a trade-off between the underwriters' preference for tight lock-ups (which support aftermarket price stability and protect their reputation) and the pre-IPO holders' preference for shorter lock-ups (which give them earlier access to their liquidity, particularly for the VC funds that need to return capital to their LPs and for the founders / employees who want to diversify).

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline of designing and negotiating the lock-up architecture — the 180-day standard, the specific carve-outs, the underwriters' early-release discretion, staggered releases, and the aftermarket-pricing implications at expiry.

> Reminder: the lock-up agreement is a specific contract that engages qualified securities counsel and the underwriters. The specific terms and specific negotiation vary by deal; the chapter is education, not deal-specific legal advice.

## The 180-day standard

The US market convention for the IPO lock-up period is 180 days from the pricing date. The 180-day standard emerged organically over decades and has held through repeated market cycles; specific attempts to shorten the standard (some 2020-2021-cohort deals experimented with 90-day or 120-day lock-ups on specific holders) have generally reverted to the 180-day baseline in subsequent cycles as the aftermarket-quality benefits of the longer lock-up became visible.

**Rationale.** The 180-day period covers two full quarterly reporting cycles post-IPO. Public-market investors want to see the newly-IPO'd company report Q1 and Q2 as a public company before the specific supply from lock-up expiry hits. Two quarters of beat-and-raise (the mod-107 chapter 1 discipline) build the specific aftermarket confidence that lets the lock-up-expiry supply be absorbed cleanly.

**Who signs.** The lock-up covers, at minimum: all executive officers, all directors, all pre-IPO shareholders holding above a specific de-minimis threshold (typically 1% of the class), all employees participating in the employee-directed-share-programme (EDSP), and all friends-and-family-programme participants. Some deals extend the lock-up to all pre-IPO shareholders regardless of size (a specific "lock everyone" pattern typical in tech IPOs); some deals apply a specific de-minimis carve-out for smaller pre-IPO holders.

**Format.** Each covered party signs a specific lock-up agreement in favour of the underwriters (typically in a form drafted by underwriter counsel and negotiated with issuer counsel). The lock-up is a bilateral contract; the specific enforcement mechanism is that the underwriters can seek an injunction or damages against a lock-up breach.

## Covered dispositions and specific carve-outs

The lock-up prohibits specific "dispositions" of the covered securities. The definition typically covers: sale, pledge, hypothecation, hedging (including short-sale, put-purchase, forward-sale-contract, prepaid variable forward contract, and other derivative-based hedging), and any transfer or disposition to a third party. The specific breadth of the definition matters — a founder who thinks they can execute a specific hedge during the lock-up because the hedge is not a "sale" will find that the specific lock-up definition covers hedging transactions.

**Standard carve-outs.** The lock-up typically permits specific pre-approved transfers that do not create aftermarket supply:

- **Gifts and estate-planning transfers.** Transfers to family trusts, family members, and specific estate-planning vehicles are typically permitted, subject to the transferee agreeing in writing to be bound by the lock-up for the remaining period. The carve-out lets founders execute specific tax-planning transfers (e.g., grantor-retained annuity trusts, family limited partnerships) without disturbing the lock-up discipline.
- **Charitable transfers.** Transfers to charitable organisations or donor-advised funds are typically permitted, again subject to the transferee lock-up.
- **Transfers to affiliates and portfolio distributions.** VC funds and PE funds are typically permitted to distribute lock-up securities to their limited partners (a "portfolio distribution"), subject to the LP-recipients agreeing to be bound by the remainder of the lock-up. The carve-out is important for VCs who have a fund-level obligation to return securities to LPs; the LP distribution is not a market sale.
- **Exercise of options and vesting of RSUs.** The exercise of stock options and the vesting of RSUs is not a disposition (the exercise / vesting produces newly-held shares that themselves are subject to the lock-up), so the carve-out is a clarifying provision rather than a substantive one.
- **Tender offers.** Where a tender offer for the company's shares occurs during the lock-up (a specific rare scenario for a just-IPO'd company but not impossible in specific M&A-during-lock-up situations), the lock-up may permit tender-offer participation.
- **10b5-1 plan adoption.** Some lock-ups permit specific 10b5-1 trading plans to be *adopted* during the lock-up (but not executed) so that specific-schedule sales can begin at the lock-up expiry without a specific plan-adoption delay.
- **Sales pursuant to underwriter release.** Sales specifically released by the underwriters in writing (see below) are permitted.

**Earnings-window and material-event carve-outs.** Some lock-ups include a specific carve-out that permits underwriters to release specific holders (typically insiders subject to specific 10b5-1 plans, or specific pre-IPO investors) to sell into a specific window after an earnings release. The carve-out is negotiated to give specific holders known liquidity access points; the specific window and eligibility criteria vary by deal.

**Selling-security-holder carve-out.** Where selling security holders participated in the IPO (secondary component), the holders may have specific sold their pre-IPO position at pricing and may be exempt from the lock-up on any residual position — the specific terms depend on the deal.

## The underwriter's early-release discretion

The lock-up is a contract *in favour of the underwriters* — the underwriters can, in their sole discretion, release any specific holder (or all holders) from the lock-up at any time. The specific early-release discretion is what makes the lock-up an underwriter-controlled instrument rather than a rigid legal restriction.

**When early releases happen.** Early releases are rare and are typically granted only in specific circumstances:

- **Estate-planning need.** A founder with a specific tax-planning event that requires liquidity during the lock-up window.
- **Founder-departure or specific personal circumstance.** A founder who leaves the company (voluntary or involuntary) and needs to unwind position for specific personal reasons.
- **VC-fund-lifecycle need.** A VC fund near the end of its fund life that needs to return capital to LPs; the fund's specific general-partner request for early release is a specific practitioner scenario.
- **Post-earnings-window release.** Where the specific first quarterly earnings beat significantly, the underwriter may release specific holders for a specific window — the "post-strong-Q1 release" pattern that some deals see.

**How early releases interact with the aftermarket.** An early release is a specific supply event. The underwriter typically announces the specific early release (via press release or 8-K) with specific timing to minimise aftermarket disruption. The specific release quantum matters — releasing a small holder (2% of shares outstanding) is minimally impactful; releasing a large VC-fund holder (10%+) is a specific market event that may require additional stabilisation activity.

**Underwriter incentives.** The underwriter has specific incentives that shape early-release decisions: (1) the underwriter's aftermarket-reputation with the buy-side depends on the aftermarket-price behaviour, which biases against early releases in soft deals; (2) the underwriter's relationship with the specific pre-IPO holders (VCs and founders who are repeat clients) biases toward specific accommodative early-release grants; (3) the specific engagement letter and syndicate agreement may include specific commitments about how the underwriter will exercise early-release discretion. The specific balance is decided case-by-case.

## Staggered lock-up releases — the 25% / 50% / 75% pattern

Some deals structure the lock-up with **staggered releases** — specific percentages of the covered securities become releasable at specific dates before the full 180-day expiry. The specific patterns:

**Full 180-day standard.** All covered securities are locked for 180 days, then all released at once. The specific single-expiry-event dynamic is the standard baseline.

**25% / 100% pattern.** 25% of covered securities become releasable at day 90 (typically conditional on specific milestones — e.g., the stock trading above the offering price for a specified consecutive-day period); the remaining 75% released at day 180. The pattern is designed to give specific holders (typically founders and long-tenured pre-IPO investors) earlier partial liquidity while maintaining the majority-lock-up discipline.

**33% / 66% / 100% pattern.** 33% released at day 60, 66% cumulative at day 120, 100% at day 180. A more aggressive early-release pattern.

**Milestone-conditional releases.** Some lock-ups condition early releases on specific milestones — e.g., release 25% if the stock trades above 120% of the offering price for a specified consecutive-day period following the Q1 earnings release. The specific milestone conditions align the specific holders' interests with the aftermarket-price target.

The trade-off in the staggered-release design: earlier partial liquidity for holders trades off against specific aftermarket-supply distribution (spread over multiple release events rather than concentrated at one). The specific aftermarket-pricing pattern for staggered-release deals differs from the single-expiry-event pattern — the specific price effects are distributed and typically smaller-per-event but occurring more frequently. Deals with high founder / early-employee lock-up concentration and specific fund-lifecycle-timing needs typically negotiate specific staggered-release patterns; deals with mostly-institutional-VC lock-up composition typically stick with the single 180-day baseline.

## The tender-offer participant carve-out (post-mod-106 interaction)

Where the pre-IPO company ran a tender-offer secondary programme (mod-106) — allowing employees or specific investors to sell a portion of their shares to a buyer in a pre-IPO secondary — the specific tender participants may have specific carve-outs in the lock-up.

The specific rationale: the tender-offer participants were given the specific ability to monetise a portion of their holdings *before* the IPO, precisely to reduce the specific supply pressure at lock-up expiry. In exchange for this specific pre-IPO liquidity, the tender-offer participants may agree to a longer lock-up on the remaining position (e.g., a 270-day or 365-day lock-up on the post-tender remaining shares in exchange for specific pre-IPO liquidity on the tendered shares), or may agree to a specific staggered-release pattern on the remaining position.

The specific tender-offer / lock-up interaction is a specific pre-IPO cap-table-management lever. Companies that run structured tender programmes 12-24 months before IPO (with specific longer-lock-up terms on the remaining post-tender position) enter the IPO with specific pre-negotiated aftermarket-supply-management arrangements. Companies that arrive at the IPO without having run a specific tender programme have to negotiate the standard 180-day lock-up on the full position with all the specific holders.

**Price effect around expiry.** The median newly-IPO'd stock experiences a specific negative abnormal return in the days around lock-up expiry, with the specific magnitude typically in the range of 1-3% in aggregate over the expiry window. The specific magnitude is larger for deals with higher lock-up-holder concentration (more shares locked up = more potential supply released), larger for deals in soft aftermarket conditions, larger for deals with less institutional-quality lock-up-holder composition, and smaller for deals in hot aftermarket conditions or with well-choreographed 10b5-1-plan-based selling that spreads the supply over time.

## Disclosure of the lock-up

The specific lock-up terms are disclosed in the S-1 (specifically in the "Shares Eligible for Future Sale" or equivalent section, and in the "Underwriting" section) and in the prospectus. The specific disclosure includes: the specific lock-up period (180 days), the specific covered parties, the specific covered dispositions, the specific carve-outs, the specific underwriter early-release discretion, the specific number of shares subject to the lock-up, and the specific number of shares that will become releasable at expiry. **Ongoing disclosure.** After the IPO, the specific lock-up expiry is not a specific 8-K trigger (there is no specific SEC rule requiring 8-K disclosure of routine lock-up expiry), but the specific expiry is a well-known event that appears on specific IR calendars and specific market-tracking databases.
