# Founder-Transition-to-Chair / Chief Innovation Officer / Chief Product Officer Role Scoping

## Why this matters

A founder-CEO who transitions to a continuing role at the company — Chair, Chief Innovation Officer, Chief Product Officer, Vice-Chair, Founder-at-Large — occupies a specific structural seat that is easy to scope badly. The specific pathology is not the founder or the incoming CEO acting in bad faith; it is that both are acting on incomplete mental models of the seat because the seat itself was not scoped explicitly. The founder shows up on day 91 believing they retain specific decision-rights on product / strategy / people / customers that the incoming CEO now believes belong to the CEO seat. The incoming CEO makes a specific first-90-days move (a hire, a product-portfolio decision, a customer-relationship shift) that the founder reads as an infringement on their continuing role. Neither party is wrong on the merits — both are working from a specific unspoken model of what the founder's role is — and the friction that follows corrodes the working relationship, undermines the transition, and often produces the founder's premature departure or the incoming CEO's departure within 12–18 months.

The remedy is the specific discipline of **explicit role scoping**. Before the founder returns from the off-ramp (chapter 3), a specific role-scoping memo names what the role *is*, what specific decision-rights it carries, what specific decision-rights it does not, how it is compensated, and when it ends. The memo is authored jointly by the founder, the incoming CEO, the board chair or lead independent director, and specific counsel. It is the specific instrument through which the founder's continuing presence becomes a *help* to the incoming CEO rather than a *drag* on them.

This chapter installs the role-scoping discipline. It covers the specific role-shape choices (executive vs. non-executive), the specific decision-rights taxonomy (decides / informs / informed-of), the specific compensation architecture, and the specific formal end-date pattern.

> Reminder: this module is education, not employment / executive-comp / tax / corporate-governance advice. Every specific founder-continuing-role is negotiated through specific counsel and reviewed against specific tax and exec-comp implications. The specific framework below is a founder / board / incoming-CEO framework for scoping the negotiation, not a substitute for the specific counsel-mediated authoring.

## The role-shape choices

The specific first question the role-scoping discipline asks is: what is the *shape* of the founder's continuing role? The typical shapes:

**Founder & Chair (executive chair).** The founder holds the board chair seat *and* remains an executive of the company with a specific operating scope. Common when the founder is retaining a specific meaningful operating footprint (product, strategy, external-relations) alongside board leadership. The specific advantage: the founder's continuing operating value is retained under an appropriate executive title. The specific risk: the "executive chair" seat has a specific historical pattern (Bill Gates at Microsoft in his 2000s "Chairman and Chief Software Architect" period, Larry Ellison at Oracle in his 2014–onwards "Executive Chairman and CTO" role, Jeff Bezos at Amazon in his 2021-onwards "Executive Chair" role) of being a structural fit for the specific founder who is stepping back from CEO-day-to-day but not from operating impact.

**Founder & Chair (non-executive chair).** The founder holds the board chair seat but is not an executive of the company. Common when the founder wants to step further back and let the incoming CEO run the operating company fully. The specific advantage: the incoming CEO has a specific clean operating mandate. The specific risk: the founder's continuing value on the operating side is lost, and some founders find the non-executive chair seat under-utilises their capacity.

**Founder & Chief Innovation Officer.** The founder does not hold the chair seat but continues as an executive with a specific mandate for long-horizon product / research / strategy work. Common when the founder's specific strengths (as read against chapter 1 signal 2) are inventive / long-horizon rather than operating-at-scale. The specific advantage: the founder is doing the specific work they are best-fit for; the incoming CEO owns the operating company; the board chair is separate. The specific risk: the CIO title has a specific ambiguity — CIO can also mean Chief Information Officer, and the specific external-market read of the title varies by sector.

**Founder & Chief Product Officer.** Similar to CIO but with a specific operating scope (product organisation) that the founder actually runs day-to-day. Common when the founder's specific strengths are product-and-vision at the current stage of the product organisation. The specific advantage: the founder has a specific operating scope that is clearly bounded. The specific risk: the CPO reports to the CEO, which creates a specific structural inversion (founder reports to a professional executive they hired) that some founders find corrosive to the working relationship.

**Founder & Vice-Chair.** The founder does not hold the chair seat but sits as vice-chair. Common in specific transitions where the founder is stepping back further and the chair role is going to an independent director (chapter 5). The vice-chair seat carries specific ceremonial and specific advisory weight without a specific operating scope.

**Founder (no operating title, board seat only).** The founder retains a board seat but has no operating title. Common in specific transitions where the founder is departing operating work fully — either because their signal-2 read (chapter 1) resolved as "the operating work no longer fits me" or because the transition is coupled with a specific transaction (M&A close, PE take-private) where the acquirer / sponsor has requested a specific full operating handoff.

**Founder-Advisor (advisor, no board seat).** The founder departs the board and remains as a specific external advisor on a specific consulting or advisor agreement. Less common in mature-stage transitions but common in specific compressed / involuntary transitions where the founder-board relationship has broken enough that continued board service is not appropriate.

**The specific factors that shape the shape.** Four specific inputs:

- **The founder's signal-2 read (chapter 1).** A founder whose signal 2 is strong-fit-with-a-specific-narrow-scope (product, invention, long-horizon strategy) fits CIO / CPO / executive-chair naturally. A founder whose signal 2 is mis-fit-across-the-board fits non-executive chair or board-seat-only.
- **The founder's signal-5 read (chapter 1).** A founder whose signal 5 is examined-and-ready and who has a specific personal off-ramp plan fits non-executive chair or board-seat-only. A founder whose signal 5 is examined-and-ambivalent typically fits an executive-chair or CIO / CPO role that preserves continuing operating engagement while the founder works through the ambivalence.
- **The incoming CEO's mandate.** An incoming CEO hired with a specific transformative mandate (turn the company through an IPO, execute a specific strategic pivot, run through an M&A integration) typically needs a specific clean operating mandate that fits with a non-executive chair or board-seat-only founder role. An incoming CEO hired with a specific continuity mandate typically has more room for an executive-chair or CIO / CPO founder role.
- **The board's read.** The board's specific view on what the founder's continuing role should be — often shaped by the specific investor-directors' fiduciary read of what best serves the company's forward strategy.

**A specific pathology.** The role-shape is chosen based on what the founder wants and communicated to the incoming CEO as a fait accompli. The specific consequence: the incoming CEO accepts the role because they want the job, but with private reservations about the shape that surface as friction three months in. The remedy is the specific discipline of choosing the role-shape *with* the incoming-CEO's specific input during the finalist stage of the search, so the incoming CEO is signing up for the specific shape they are prepared to work within.

## The decision-rights taxonomy

Once the role-shape is chosen, the specific next question is: what decision-rights attach to the role? The specific instrument that most reduces friction is a **decision-rights matrix** — a written taxonomy that names, for each specific decision-domain, what the founder *decides*, what the founder is *informed about with a chance to weigh in*, and what the founder is simply *informed of* after the fact.

**The three-tier taxonomy.**

- **Decides.** The founder retains specific decision-rights. The founder's decision is the specific decision-of-record; the incoming CEO informs the board and executes.
- **Informs.** The incoming CEO informs the founder in advance of a specific decision and gives the founder a specific consult opportunity. The incoming CEO makes the specific final decision but with the founder's input on-record.
- **Informed-of.** The incoming CEO informs the founder after the fact. The specific decision is fully the incoming CEO's; the founder is kept in the loop as a courtesy and as a matter of ongoing relationship.

**Typical decision-domains and their typical tier for each role-shape.**

For an **executive chair** or **founder-CIO** role, the typical decision-rights pattern:

| Decision-domain | Typical tier |
|---|---|
| Board composition and committee assignment | Decides (as chair) or Informs (as CIO) |
| CEO compensation | Decides (as chair, through comp committee) or Informed-of (as CIO) |
| Executive-team hiring, firing, and comp | Informs |
| Company strategy (annual plan) | Informs |
| Product-portfolio decisions | Decides (CIO / CPO domain) or Informs |
| Long-horizon R&D and innovation | Decides (CIO domain) |
| Capital-allocation (major deals, financings) | Informs |
| Customer-and-partner relationships (top-N) | Informs |
| External communications and press | Informs |
| Ordinary-course operational decisions | Informed-of |
| Ordinary-course hiring below executive layer | Informed-of |

For a **non-executive chair** role, the pattern shifts toward Informs and Informed-of, with Decides limited to the chair's board-level responsibilities (agenda, board composition, board-committee assignments, CEO evaluation).

For a **board-seat-only founder**, the pattern is largely Informed-of, with Informs on specific matters that come to the board (strategy review, annual plan, major decisions) and Decides on nothing outside the board vote itself.

**Why the matrix matters.** The specific value of the matrix is not that it perfectly predicts every future decision — it will not — but that it establishes a specific *default* the founder and incoming CEO can refer to. When a specific decision arises that is ambiguously scoped, the matrix is the specific reference document that the founder and incoming CEO consult together, and if the matrix does not name the specific decision, the specific discipline is to escalate to the board chair or lead independent director to name it before the decision goes wrong.

**Ambiguity zones — where matrices most often break.** Three specific decision-domains where the matrix most often needs specific attention:

- **Product-and-vision.** A founder-CIO or founder-CPO with a Decides right on product-portfolio decisions, and an incoming CEO with a specific strategic mandate that touches the product portfolio (e.g., "sunset the founder's legacy product line to focus on the growth product line"), will collide. The specific matrix needs to name where the founder's decides-right stops and the CEO's strategic-decides-right begins.
- **Executive-team composition.** A founder who hired most of the current executive team, and an incoming CEO who inherits them and (over the first 12–18 months) may change composition, will collide if the founder retains a Decides or Informs right on executive-team composition. The specific matrix needs to name the incoming CEO's Decides right on executive-team composition explicitly, and the founder's role as (typically) Informed-of.
- **Customer-and-partner relationships.** A founder who had personal relationships with specific top-N customers, and an incoming CEO who is now the executive sponsor for those accounts, will collide if the founder continues to engage independently with those customers. The specific matrix names the specific customer-relationship transfer, and the founder's continuing role (typically Informed-of with occasional Informs).

**A specific pathology.** The matrix is drafted at role-scoping but then not consulted. The specific consequence: the specific decisions the matrix was designed to disambiguate are made without reference to it, and the friction the matrix was designed to prevent surfaces. The remedy is a specific discipline of the incoming CEO and founder using the matrix as an explicit reference at specific inflection points — the day-90 plan (chapter 3), the day-180 retrospective, the annual planning cycle, the specific-major-decision moment.

## The compensation architecture

The specific compensation architecture for the founder's continuing role is a specific negotiated instrument with specific tax, board-governance, and market-signal implications.

**The three specific architectures.**

- **Board chair compensation.** For a non-executive-chair founder, the specific compensation is board-chair compensation — typically cash retainer plus equity plus committee-chair-additional (if the chair also chairs specific committees). The specific compensation is materially lower than C-suite compensation and is set by the compensation committee against a specific board-comp market benchmark (typically referencing NACD board-comp studies or specific peer-company board-comp data). Typical range for a mid-cap or growth-stage company chair: $150k–$500k cash + $100k–$500k equity annually, though wide sector-and-stage variation.
- **C-suite compensation.** For a founder-CIO or founder-CPO in an executive role, the specific compensation is C-suite compensation — cash salary plus bonus plus equity — set against the specific role's market comp benchmark. The specific level depends on the specific role scope (a full-scope CPO with product-org P&L is compensated at CPO-market rates; a specific narrow-scope innovation officer is compensated at a specific lower band).
- **Dual role compensation.** For an executive-chair role (chair *and* executive), the specific compensation is a specific dual structure — chair compensation for the chair function plus executive compensation for the executive function. The specific dual structure is subject to specific board-governance scrutiny (an executive chair is not typically counted as an independent director, and specific committee-independence rules may apply — see chapter 5 and mod-109).

**The specific tax and exec-comp guardrails.**

- **IRC §280G (golden-parachute).** If the founder-transition is coupled with a specific change-of-control transaction, the founder's continuing compensation may implicate §280G excise-tax analysis. Mod-103 develops the specific framework.
- **IRC §409A (nonqualified deferred compensation).** Any specific deferred-compensation or equity-grant structure in the founder-transition contract must be reviewed for §409A compliance. Mod-104 develops the specific framework.
- **IRC §162(m) (executive-compensation deductibility).** For public companies, the founder's continuing compensation (if the founder is a covered employee) is subject to the specific $1M deduction cap.
- **SEC executive-compensation disclosure.** For public companies, the founder's continuing compensation is disclosed in the specific Compensation Discussion and Analysis (CD&A) section of the proxy. The specific structure must be defensible to the proxy-advisor scrutiny (ISS / Glass Lewis — mod-109).
- **Delaware fiduciary duty on interested-director compensation.** The compensation committee's decision on the founder's continuing compensation must be defensible under the specific Delaware fiduciary standard (mod-109). Particularly for a founder-executive-chair or founder-CIO role, the compensation committee's process discipline (independent directors, market-comp benchmarking, specific rationale) matters materially.

**Specific market-signal considerations.** Public and later-stage private companies face specific market-signal pressure on the founder's continuing compensation:

- **Over-compensation signal.** A founder-continuing role with a specific over-market compensation package signals to proxy advisors and to the wider investor base that the board is not acting at arm's length on founder matters. This is corrosive to broader governance credibility.
- **Under-compensation signal.** A founder-continuing role with a specific under-market compensation package signals that the founder is being pushed out under specific pressure. This can be corrosive to founder-team morale and to specific narrative-management around the transition.
- **The specific "founder discount"** — many founders accept a specific below-market compensation for the continuing role because the founder already holds material equity and does not need the cash. The specific discount is often acceptable *if* it is presented as a specific founder choice rather than as a specific board move.

**A specific pathology.** The founder's compensation for the continuing role is drafted late — often only after the incoming CEO's compensation is finalised — under time pressure. The specific consequence: the compensation is either too high (drafted in specific "make the founder feel valued" mode) or too low (drafted in specific "get this done before the announcement" mode), and the specific board-governance and market-signal implications surface only later. The remedy is the specific discipline of drafting the founder-transition compensation *in parallel* with the incoming CEO's offer, with specific comp-committee review and specific external comp-consultant review (Compensia, Semler Brossy, Frederic W. Cook, Aon Radford, Willis Towers Watson) if the specific stage warrants.

## The formal 12–24-month founder-transition end-date

The specific structural discipline that most preserves the incoming CEO's operating mandate over the medium term is the specific **formal end-date** on the founder's continuing role. Without an explicit end-date, the founder's continuing presence has no natural sunset, and the specific "when does the founder actually leave" question becomes an ongoing source of friction that neither the founder nor the incoming CEO can resolve.

**The typical end-date pattern.** The founder's continuing role (whether Chair, CIO, CPO, or another shape) is scoped for a specific 12–24-month period, with a specific end-date named in the founder-transition contract. At the end-date, one of three things happens:

- **The founder departs.** The role sunsets. The founder retains the board seat (if applicable), retains vested equity, and departs operating work. This is the modal pattern.
- **The role is renewed.** The founder and the incoming CEO and the board mutually agree to a specific renewal for a specific additional period. The renewal is a specific active decision rather than a default, which is the specific instrument that makes the arrangement durable.
- **The role changes shape.** The founder shifts from (e.g.) executive chair to non-executive chair, or from CIO to board-seat-only. The specific shape-change is another active decision.

**Why the end-date matters.** Three specific reasons:

- **Incoming-CEO operating latitude.** The specific end-date gives the incoming CEO a specific horizon within which they know the founder is present and after which they will run the seat fully. This is the specific instrument that lets the incoming CEO plan the medium-term without ambiguity.
- **Founder personal planning.** The specific end-date gives the founder a specific horizon around which to plan their own post-CEO life — what specific new work they will start, what specific personal-life restructuring they will do. Founders whose continuing role has no end-date often report a specific "drift" pattern where the personal-life planning stalls because the ending is undefined.
- **Board-and-market credibility.** The specific end-date signals to the board and to the market that the transition is genuinely a *transition* rather than a specific perpetual co-CEO arrangement. This is particularly important for the specific pre-IPO or post-IPO stage where the market's read of "is the founder actually stepping back" shapes stock-price and investor-relations dynamics.

**The specific negotiation of the end-date.** The specific length (12 vs. 18 vs. 24 months) depends on:

- The specific founder-fit signal (chapter 1 signal 2). A founder with strong-fit for a specific narrow scope (CIO for a research-heavy company, CPO for a product-heavy company) may have a longer end-date because the specific value they add extends further.
- The specific incoming-CEO mandate. A transformative mandate benefits from a shorter founder-end-date; a continuity mandate can accommodate a longer one.
- The specific transaction context. If the transition is coupled with a specific transaction (M&A close, PE take-private, IPO), the specific end-date is often shaped by the specific transaction-adjacent obligations (earn-out period, lock-up period, retention window).
- The specific founder personal-readiness signal (chapter 1 signal 5). A founder who is examined-and-ready fits a shorter end-date; a founder who is examined-and-ambivalent typically needs a longer one, though a longer end-date without a specific active re-scoping mechanism can extend the ambivalence.

**A specific pathology.** The founder-transition contract does not name an end-date, or names an open-ended "as mutually agreed" pattern. The specific consequence: the founder's continuing role runs on for 3, 5, 7 years, the incoming CEO's operating latitude never fully lands, the market reads the arrangement as a specific unresolved co-CEO situation, and eventually a specific breaking event forces the founder's departure under conditions much worse than a planned end-date would have produced. The remedy is the specific discipline of naming the end-date at the specific initial role-scoping and treating any specific extension as an active board decision, not as a default.

## The role-scoping memo

The specific artefact this chapter's discipline produces is the **role-scoping memo** — a specific 5–10-page document authored jointly by the founder, the incoming CEO, the board chair or lead independent director, and specific counsel, before the founder returns from the off-ramp. Its specific contents:

- **Role title and shape.** The specific title (Founder & Chair, Founder & CIO, etc.) and shape (executive vs. non-executive vs. board-only).
- **Scope statement.** One-to-two paragraphs on what the role does day-to-day and week-to-week.
- **Reporting relationship.** Who the role reports to (typically the board for chair; the CEO for CIO / CPO), and how the incoming CEO and founder work together.
- **Decision-rights matrix.** The specific taxonomy (decides / informs / informed-of) across specific decision-domains.
- **Compensation.** The specific compensation architecture — cash, equity, benefits, board-comp elements if applicable.
- **Communications channel.** Specific meeting cadence, escalation channel, external-communications discipline.
- **Board involvement.** Specific board seat (yes/no), specific committee assignments, specific chair role (if applicable).
- **End-date.** The specific date and the specific renewal / re-scoping mechanism.
- **Departure terms.** The specific circumstances under which the role ends (mutual agreement, board decision, founder decision, for-cause termination) and the specific separation terms that attach.
- **Review cadence.** The specific pattern of reviewing the arrangement (typically an annual review by the compensation committee and the board chair; a specific check-in with the incoming CEO at day 90, day 180, and quarterly thereafter).

The role-scoping memo is a specific standing reference document — updated when the arrangement is re-scoped, referenced when a specific ambiguity arises, and archived in the board's records alongside the specific founder-transition contract.

## Summary

The founder's continuing role — Chair (executive or non-executive), Chief Innovation Officer, Chief Product Officer, Vice-Chair, board-seat-only — is a specific structural seat that requires explicit scoping to avoid the specific pathologies of ambiguous decision-rights, mis-aligned compensation, and open-ended tenure. The role-scoping memo names the specific shape, the specific scope, the specific decision-rights matrix (decides / informs / informed-of across decision-domains), the specific compensation architecture (board-chair compensation vs. C-suite compensation vs. dual role), and the specific formal 12–24-month end-date with an explicit renewal / re-scoping mechanism. The specific discipline is choosing the shape based on the composite of the founder's signal 2 (fit) and signal 5 (personal readiness) reads, the incoming CEO's mandate, and the board's forward-strategy view; drafting the memo jointly with the incoming CEO's input during the finalist stage of the search rather than after; and using the memo as an ongoing reference document rather than a one-time artefact.
