# Founder-CEO Succession Readiness — Reading the Five Signals

## Why this matters

The single most common failure mode in founder-CEO succession is *late diagnosis*. A board waits for a specific breaking event — a missed quarter, a departed head of sales, an activist letter, a term sheet that arrives with a "new CEO" clause — and then runs a compressed 90-day search under pressure, lands a candidate the process would not have chosen with more time, and installs that candidate against a founder who has had no runway to make peace with the transition. The compressed search costs 12–24 months of company momentum on the front end (the search itself, plus the trust rebuild inside the executive team) and 24–48 months on the back end (the incoming CEO who did not fit, the founder who left angry, the direct-reports who left with the founder, the board that spent two years relitigating the decision). The Charan / Nadler / Bower / Wasserman canon on CEO succession — Ram Charan's *Boards at Work*, David Nadler's *Building Better Boards*, Joseph Bower's *The CEO Within*, Noam Wasserman's *The Founder's Dilemmas* — converges on the same finding from different angles: the transitions that succeed are the transitions that were named and scoped 12–24 months before the handoff, and the transitions that fail are the transitions that were forced to move on a shorter timeline.

The remedy is not to force a succession early. The remedy is to install a *reading discipline* — a small set of specific signals the founder-CEO, the board chair, and the lead independent director are tracking continuously, so that when the signals cross a threshold the conversation is already in motion rather than starting from cold. That reading discipline is what this chapter installs. Its output is not a decision to transition; its output is a *timing read* — 24 months out, 12 months out, or already overdue — that shapes every subsequent chapter in this module.

> Reminder: this module is education, not executive-search or executive-coaching advice. The reading discipline below is a founder / board / lead-director framework, not a diagnostic that substitutes for a specific search-partner scoping conversation or a specific executive-coaching engagement. Every live succession consideration engages qualified advisors.

## The five signals

Founder-CEO succession readiness is not one signal. It is a composite of at least five, any subset of which can reach threshold on its own, and all five of which appear at some point in almost every eventually-successful transition. In order of how founders and boards typically read them:

1. **Operating-scale signal** — has the company crossed the scale at which the sector-typical founder-CEO seat becomes a professional-CEO seat?
2. **Founder-fit-with-current-stage signal** — do the tasks that dominate the seat right now still play to the founder's strengths, or has the seat drifted away from the founder's comparative advantage?
3. **Board-composition signal** — has the board matured to include directors with CEO-transition experience who can hold the conversation credibly?
4. **Funding-stage signal** — is the next capital round (growth equity, crossover, IPO, PE take-private, secondary at scale) likely to demand a professional-CEO transition as a condition or preference?
5. **Founder-personal-readiness signal** — has the founder made peace with the possibility of the transition, or is the transition still a taboo topic inside the founder's own head?

The specific discipline this chapter teaches is (a) how to read each signal independently, (b) how to read the composite, and (c) how to translate the composite into a specific timing horizon — 24 months out (start the board conversation, build the bench), 12 months out (retain the search partner, scope the role), 6 months out (candidates in play, founder-transition contract in draft), 90 days out (hand-off choreography active), and *already overdue* (compressed-search-under-pressure, damage-limitation regime).

## Signal 1 — Operating scale

The most objective of the five signals: has the company crossed the operating-scale threshold at which the CEO seat's day-to-day tasks — the ones that determine 60–80% of where the CEO's calendar goes — become tasks that a *founder* is typically comparatively less well-suited to than a *professional CEO*?

**The typical threshold.** Across sector and stage the empirical bands cluster around:

- **~$50M ARR** for a SaaS / B2B-software company, plus or minus a factor depending on gross margin, sales-motion complexity, and customer concentration. Below this scale the founder-CEO's product-and-vision-driven leadership generally still dominates the calendar; above this scale the calendar drifts toward organisational-scale management, executive-team development, board / investor / customer / regulator / public-facing communications, and cross-functional operating-cadence discipline that a professional-CEO seat is architected around.
- **~250 headcount** as a headcount-side proxy for the same threshold. Below this scale the founder can maintain a personal relationship with everyone above the individual-contributor line and reach into any function; above this scale the founder is running an organisation-of-organisations and cannot personally know the leaders two levels down.
- **Sector-specific variants.** Consumer companies often hit the threshold later on ARR (a $100M consumer company may still feel like a small operation) and earlier on headcount (a 150-person consumer company with a heavy operations arm may be past the founder-fit inflection). Enterprise infrastructure / open-source-commercial companies hit the threshold earlier on ARR (a $30M enterprise-infrastructure company selling into Fortune-100 procurement may already have crossed the operating-scale inflection). Hardware / deep-tech / biotech companies operate on stage-based readings (pre-clinical vs. Phase-1 vs. commercial) rather than ARR bands.

**Why the threshold is where it is.** The specific calendar-shift the founder feels as they cross the threshold:

- Executive-team management goes from "I hired most of them and I know them personally" to "I inherited most of them from a search partner I have not previously worked with and I am managing them as a portfolio."
- Board management goes from "quarterly meetings with the same three investors" to "quarterly meetings with a board that has independent directors I do not know as well as I know the investors, a compensation committee, an audit committee, and increasingly formal proxy-adjacent disciplines."
- Customer-and-partner-facing time goes from "I close the top-5 deals personally" to "I meet the top-5 customers annually and my CRO closes."
- Product-and-engineering time goes from "I am in the codebase / product-review room daily" to "I get a weekly product review and a monthly roadmap review from a CPO / CTO who now owns the surface I built."

The threshold is not a hard line — it is a band across which the founder-fit-with-current-stage signal (signal 2) starts to strain. The operating-scale reading is what tells the board *when the strain is a scale problem rather than a founder problem*.

**How to read the signal.** Track the specific quantitative anchors quarterly:

- ARR trajectory and the next-12-months ARR projection. Are we past the sector-typical threshold or is the projection about to cross it?
- Headcount trajectory and the next-12-months headcount plan. Are we past 250 and heading to 400? Past 400 and heading to 800?
- Direct-report count for the CEO seat. Is it still six-to-nine (manageable at any scale) or is it drifting up to twelve-plus (a specific sign that the CEO is being asked to manage more than the seat can hold)?
- Number of second-level managers (managers who report to the CEO's direct reports). Above ~30 the organisation is running as a two-level abstraction that the CEO reaches only through the direct reports.

Not every founder should transition at the threshold. Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, Larry Page (in different arcs), Reed Hastings, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Marc Benioff — a specific set of founder-CEOs scaled past the operating-scale inflection and remained the right seat-holder. Their common characteristic is a specific *founder-fit-with-current-stage* signal (signal 2) that stayed strong through the scale transition, and a specific *founder-personal-readiness* signal (signal 5) that resolved as "I want to keep doing this" rather than "I feel obligated to keep doing this." The operating-scale signal is *necessary but not sufficient* to name a founder as a succession candidate; it is the signal that gets the conversation on the calendar.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The most common misread of the operating-scale signal is the "we're small for our sector" defence — a $75M ARR SaaS company whose founder-CEO argues the company is still too small for a professional-CEO transition because "other companies are $200M when they transition." The defence conflates *when a transition becomes forced* with *when a transition becomes appropriate to plan*. The Charan finding is unambiguous: the boards that plan at $50M ARR have the option to transition at $100M or $200M or never; the boards that do not plan at $50M ARR find themselves reacting at $150M under pressure. Signal 1 is a *planning trigger*, not a *action trigger*.

## Signal 2 — Founder-fit-with-current-stage

The most consequential of the five signals but the hardest to read objectively: do the tasks that dominate the CEO seat *right now* still play to the founder's strengths, or has the seat drifted away from the founder's comparative advantage?

**What "seat drift" means concretely.** Every founder-CEO started the company doing a specific mix of tasks — product invention, first-customer selling, first-hire recruiting, first-money fundraising — and rose to comparative-advantage in that mix. As the company scales, the mix changes:

- Product invention → product prioritisation across a portfolio.
- First-customer selling → CRO management and top-account executive-sponsorship.
- First-hire recruiting → CHRO management and executive-team calibration.
- First-money fundraising → CFO management, board / investor management, and (for public-track companies) analyst / regulator / press management.
- (New tasks that were not on the founder's original calendar) → executive-team development, org-design at scale, regulatory-and-policy engagement, board-and-committee-cadence discipline, capital-allocation across a mature P&L, culture-at-scale maintenance.

Some founders' comparative advantages transfer across the shift; some do not. The specific test is not "is the founder capable of learning the new mix" — most founders are — but *is the founder's marginal hour better spent on the new mix than a professional-CEO's would be, given the company's current forward path?*

**How to read the signal.** Two specific diagnostic patterns:

- **The calendar audit.** Track a quarter of the founder-CEO's calendar. Categorise every hour into (a) product-and-vision, (b) executive-team management, (c) board / investor / regulator / public-facing, (d) customer-and-partner, (e) recruiting, (f) internal-communications-and-culture, (g) operations-and-finance-review. Then ask: of the categories where the founder spends the most hours, which are the categories the founder finds *energising* vs. *draining*? A founder who spends 45% of their time on executive-team management and finds it draining is a founder whose calendar has drifted past their comparative-advantage envelope.
- **The executive-team feedback pattern.** Track the specific patterns in exec-team 1:1s and skip-levels. Are the CTO, CPO, CRO, CFO, CHRO, and GC coming to the founder-CEO with questions the founder-CEO is well-positioned to answer, or with questions where the founder-CEO's answer visibly reflects "I have not had time to develop a view here" or "my instinct says X but I know the CFO / CPO / CRO has better information"? A pattern of the second type across multiple direct reports is a signal that the founder-CEO is at the edge of the seat's task envelope.

**The specific role of the executive coach.** A founder-CEO working with a specific senior executive coach — the Reboot / Kim Scott / Bill Campbell / Marshall Goldsmith / Jerry Colonna canon of executive coaches who specialise in founder-CEO stages — often reads this signal earlier than the founder alone. The coach's specific value in the readiness read is a neutral perspective on the calendar-and-energy patterns the founder is often too close to see. A founder-CEO who has *not* engaged an executive coach by the time signal 1 (operating scale) reaches threshold is a founder who is reading signal 2 without the specific diagnostic instrument the readiness discipline depends on.

**What signal 2 does not say.** Signal 2 is *not* "the founder is doing a bad job." A founder can be operating at the top of their game and still be a mis-fit for the current seat — the game has changed while the founder was still winning at the last version of it. Boards and founder-CEOs sometimes confuse "the CEO's performance metrics look strong" with "the CEO is well-fit to the seat." A CEO whose performance is strong *because they are running the version of the company that suited them* is a CEO the board should be reading against forward-stage fit, not backward-stage performance.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The most common signal-2 misread is the "the founder built it, the founder should run it" defence, particularly from founder-friendly boards. The defence conflates founder *legitimacy* (the founder built this and has a specific relationship with employees, customers, and mission) with founder *fit* (the current seat's tasks match the founder's comparative advantage). Legitimacy is durable and transferable — the founder retains it whether or not they hold the CEO seat, and it becomes the specific asset the founder brings to a Chair / CIO / CPO role (chapter 4). Fit is a stage-specific match that changes as the company scales. The signal-2 discipline is naming the distinction and reading fit honestly.

## Signal 3 — Board composition

The board is the specific institution that runs a CEO succession. If the board is not composed to run one, the succession will not be well-run — regardless of how well signals 1, 2, 4, and 5 are read. The board-composition signal asks: does the current board include directors with the specific experience, credibility, and standing to hold a founder-CEO-succession conversation to the standard the transition requires?

**What "CEO-transition experience" means concretely.** A director with credible CEO-transition experience typically has one or more of:

- Sat as a director on a prior board that successfully executed a founder-CEO-to-professional-CEO transition. The specific pattern-recognition on what makes the process go well vs. badly is not learnable from books.
- Sat as an outgoing founder-CEO themselves and made the transition on the other side. This is a specific credibility asset in the founder's own eyes — the founder knows the director has been in the seat.
- Sat as an incoming professional CEO into a founder-CEO succession. This is the reciprocal credibility asset.
- Held a senior executive-search-firm partnership seat (Heidrick, Spencer Stuart, Egon Zehnder), a specific board-advisory-firm seat (Bolster, NACD), or a specific PE-firm operating-partner seat (Vista, Thoma Bravo, Silver Lake, KKR) with a documented track record of CEO-succession execution.

**How to read the signal.** Look at the current board roster:

- Which directors have one or more of the credentials above? Name them specifically.
- Which directors are venture-stage investor-directors whose primary experience is early-stage investing rather than mature-stage governance? Their specific value is not CEO-succession execution — it is prior-stage judgement, network access, and relationship continuity.
- Is there a lead independent director or independent chair? (See chapter 5 for depth on the chair-vs-CEO separation.) In many venture-stage boards the chair role is held by the founder-CEO themselves; a board that is starting to prepare for a founder-CEO succession typically installs a lead independent director well before the succession begins.
- Is there a nominating-and-governance committee (or its functional equivalent) with a specific mandate over CEO succession? For pre-IPO companies this may be an informal board-chair-and-lead-director standing arrangement; for post-IPO or advanced-pre-IPO companies it should be a chartered committee.

**The specific consequence of a board that is not composition-ready.** A founder-CEO reads signals 1, 2, 4, and 5 and concludes the transition should begin. They take the conversation to the board. The board — because it lacks the CEO-transition experience — either (a) treats the conversation as premature ("you are still the right CEO"), (b) treats it as reactive ("this is a personnel decision, let us handle it in six months when we have a specific candidate"), or (c) treats it as a search-firm exercise ("let us retain Spencer Stuart and get some candidates in front of us in Q3"). All three responses miss the *process discipline* the succession requires. The founder-CEO is left running the succession themselves, which is a specific structural conflict of interest.

**The remedy — install the bench 12–24 months ahead.** The specific discipline is that a board reading signal 1 approaching threshold and signal 2 starting to strain should add at least one director with CEO-transition experience 12–24 months before the succession conversation goes live. This is not "adding a director to run the succession"; it is adding a director to *hold the conversation to the standard the succession will require* once it goes live. Boards that install this bench proactively find themselves with the option to transition on their own timeline; boards that install it reactively — after the founder-CEO has already raised the question — signal to the founder that the bench-add is a specific move against them, which is corrosive to trust.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The "we don't need a specific director for that" defence — a founder-CEO or an existing investor-director arguing that the current board is fine because "we'll bring in the right advisors when the time comes." The defence misreads what the bench does. The advisors (search partner, coach, counsel) execute the workstream; the board *governs* it. Advisors cannot substitute for the specific board-level standing that a CEO-transition-experienced director brings to the conversation with the founder-CEO. Signal 3 is a *board* signal, not an *advisor* signal.

## Signal 4 — Funding stage

The specific external forcing-function that can compress signals 1, 2, and 5 into a short timeline: is the next capital event likely to demand a professional-CEO transition as a condition or a strong preference?

**The typical patterns.**

- **Late-stage growth-equity round.** Growth-equity investors (Tiger Global, Coatue, Insight, TCV, General Atlantic, TA Associates, Summit, Vista, Thoma Bravo, KKR, Silver Lake at growth-stage) often have specific patterns for the CEO-transition question at the round they lead. Some (Tiger Global historically, Coatue) are indifferent to founder-CEO continuity and will underwrite the founder as the ongoing CEO. Others (traditional PE growth-equity, and specifically PE take-private) frequently have a specific bias toward a professional-CEO seat post-close, either as a condition of the round or as a strong preference expressed in the term sheet.
- **Crossover round pre-IPO.** Crossover investors (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, BlackRock, Wellington, Sands Capital, Franklin Templeton) invest in pre-IPO growth rounds with the intent of holding through the IPO and beyond. Their view on the CEO seat is typically *forward-stage-fit* — is this CEO the right seat-holder for the public-company period the fund is underwriting? The read is often more nuanced than "founder or professional" and closer to "is the founder ready for the public-company stakeholder set."
- **IPO track.** The IPO itself introduces a specific public-market-CEO seat with a specific stakeholder set (public investors, sell-side analysts, ISS / Glass Lewis, journalist / activist / short-seller exposure, Reg FD / 10-Q / 10-K / 8-K discipline). Some founder-CEOs make this transition inside the seat; others do not. The board's read of the founder-CEO's fit for the public-company seat is typically installed 18–24 months pre-IPO and can result in a pre-IPO succession.
- **PE take-private.** A PE take-private (mod-102 develops the market structure; mod-103 develops the deal structures) frequently comes with a specific CEO-transition either at close or within the first 12 months post-close. The PE sponsor's operating-partner group typically has strong views on the incoming CEO seat and often has a specific candidate in mind before the deal signs.
- **Strategic acquisition.** A strategic acquirer's post-close plan (mod-111) frequently retains the founder-CEO for a specific transition period (12–36 months) with a departure at the end of the earn-out or retention window. Signal 4 in this context is not about the *next* round but about the *acquisition-close event* forcing the transition on a specific pre-negotiated timeline.
- **Down round / distressed round.** In a distressed round, existing investors (or an incoming turnaround investor) may demand a CEO change as a condition of the round. This is the compressed / involuntary version of signal 4.

**How to read the signal.** Look at the specific capital-event calendar:

- What is the next capital event on the 24-month horizon? (Priced round, secondary, IPO, take-private, strategic sale, none.)
- What is the market-shape read for that event? (Which investor class is likely to lead? What are their sector-typical patterns on the CEO-continuity question?)
- What is the specific *conversation* pattern the founder-CEO and CFO have had with those investors on prior rounds or in the ongoing relationship? Have any specific investors raised the CEO-transition question directly or indirectly?
- What is the board's read? (Do the investor-directors currently on the board expect a specific CEO-transition read from the next-round-lead? Are they preparing for it?)

**The specific pathology of a signal-4-driven succession.** When signal 4 is the forcing function — the founder-CEO transitions because the next round or the transaction demands it — the succession is *reactive* rather than *proactive*. The founder is transitioning at someone else's pace and often with less runway than the 12–24-month arc the Charan / Nadler / Bower canon calls for. The specific consequences: (a) the founder is more likely to leave angry, (b) the search is more compressed, (c) the founder-transition contract is more likely to be transactional rather than aligned-partnership, and (d) the incoming CEO inherits a founder who has not made peace with the transition. The remedy is not to avoid signal 4 — it is often unavoidable — but to *anticipate* it: to read signals 1, 2, 3 in the two years *before* signal 4 becomes the forcing function, so the succession is running on the proactive timeline when the funding event arrives.

## Signal 5 — Founder personal readiness

The most personal of the five signals and often the last to resolve: has the founder-CEO made peace with the possibility of the transition — with the identity shift, the loss of the CEO seat's specific privileges and responsibilities, the shift in the public narrative from "founder-CEO" to "founder and Chair" (or "former CEO"), and the specific relationships (with the board, the executive team, employees, customers, investors, press) that will restructure through the transition?

**Why this signal is so hard to read.** A founder who is not personally ready will often *tell themselves and the board* that they are ready — because saying they are not ready feels like admitting weakness, admitting the transition is imminent, or admitting they have been avoiding the topic. The Wasserman *Founder's Dilemmas* research on founder-CEO successions found a specific pattern: founders who reported "I am ready when the board is ready" 12 months out often revealed a much more complicated internal picture in the actual succession conversation, and the compressed nature of the eventual conversation was frequently caused by the founder's earlier over-reporting of readiness.

**The specific diagnostic patterns for signal 5.**

- **The identity-articulation test.** Ask the founder to describe themselves in a specific hypothetical 18 months post-transition — "you are the Chair, the incoming CEO has been running the company for a year, an old friend at a conference asks you what you do." A founder who is ready has a specific comfortable answer; a founder who is not ready has a hesitation, a "well, I mean, technically I'm the Chair but I still..." qualification, or a reframe of the question. The hesitation is data.
- **The calendar-projection test.** Ask the founder to draft a specific projected calendar for the 12 months post-transition. What does a week look like? How much time on the company? How much time on other pursuits (writing, teaching, angel-investing, board-of-others, personal life)? A founder who is ready can produce a specific projection with plausible-feeling detail; a founder who is not ready produces a calendar that reads as "same as now but with a different title," which is a signal that the founder has not psychologically vacated the seat.
- **The successor-mental-model test.** Ask the founder to describe the specific attributes of an ideal successor. A founder who is ready describes attributes complementary to their own or specifically adapted to the forward-stage — "someone with public-company-CEO experience, someone with a specific comp-and-scale management track record." A founder who is not ready describes attributes identical to their own — "someone who thinks about the product the way I do, someone who understands the mission the way I do" — which is a signal that the founder is still evaluating candidates against a "replace me with me" frame that no candidate will pass.
- **The executive-coach-and-close-confidant reading.** A founder-CEO's executive coach (if there is one) and a small set of close confidants (spouse or life partner, one or two long-standing peer-founders, a specific mentor) typically have the truest read on signal 5. The specific discipline for a founder-CEO approaching the readiness question is to have those readings *available* — to have engaged an executive coach, to have talked with the confidants openly, to have built a small "readiness sounding board" that can reflect the founder's actual state back to them.

**Why signal 5 must be read by the founder first.** Signals 1, 2, 3, and 4 can be read by the board and shared with the founder-CEO. Signal 5 has to be read by the founder-CEO first — the board cannot force the founder's personal readiness, and a board that raises signal 5 with the founder-CEO before the founder has done their own reading typically triggers a defensive response that closes the conversation rather than opening it. The specific choreography (chapter 2) is that the board chair or lead independent director creates the *space* for the founder-CEO to raise signal 5 — a specific standing 1:1, a specific board-executive-session pattern, a specific "how are you doing" cadence — and lets the founder-CEO bring it forward on their own timeline.

**The specific role of the executive coach in signal 5.** A founder-CEO with a specific executive coach (typically engaged 6–24 months before the succession conversation would go live) has a specific instrument for reading signal 5 that a founder-CEO without a coach lacks. The coach's role is not to *push* the founder toward transition — a specific anti-pattern is a coach who has decided the transition is the right move and is subtly pushing — but to *reflect* the founder's state back to them so the founder's own reading can be as honest as possible. Founders who report the transition conversation as "I felt like the coach was in my corner" are typically working with a coach doing this reflection well; founders who report the transition conversation as "the coach was pushing me toward the transition" are typically working with a coach whose specific mandate had drifted.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The most common signal-5 misread is the "I've been thinking about it and I'm ready" self-report from a founder who has not actually done the identity-articulation, calendar-projection, or successor-mental-model work above. The self-report is well-intentioned but is not diagnostic. The board and the executive-search partner will read this founder as ready at the conversation, agree to move to the search, and then encounter the founder's actual unreadiness at candidate-finalist-meetings, at contract-negotiation, or (most damagingly) at the handoff itself. The remedy is not to distrust the founder's self-report but to complement it with the specific diagnostic patterns above.

## The composite read and the timing horizon

Individually the five signals are informative but not sufficient. The specific decision the board and founder-CEO need is a *composite read* — a synthesis across the five signals that translates into a specific timing horizon for the succession workstream.

**The composite-read discipline.** For each signal, rate the reading on a specific band:

- **Signal 1 — operating scale.** BELOW-THRESHOLD / APPROACHING-THRESHOLD / PAST-THRESHOLD / WELL-PAST-THRESHOLD.
- **Signal 2 — founder-fit-with-current-stage.** STRONG-FIT / STRAINED-FIT / MIS-FIT / ACUTE-MIS-FIT.
- **Signal 3 — board composition.** COMPOSITION-READY / PARTIAL-BENCH / NOT-READY.
- **Signal 4 — funding stage.** NO-FORCING-EVENT-24MO / FORCING-EVENT-12-24MO / FORCING-EVENT-6-12MO / FORCING-EVENT-IMMINENT.
- **Signal 5 — founder personal readiness.** UNEXAMINED / EXAMINED-AND-NOT-READY / EXAMINED-AND-AMBIVALENT / EXAMINED-AND-READY.

**The typical timing horizons.** The specific combinations that map to the specific horizons:

- **24 months out — start the conversation, build the bench.** Signal 1 approaching-threshold, signal 2 strong or slightly strained, signal 3 partial-bench, signal 4 no-forcing-event-24mo, signal 5 unexamined-or-examined-and-not-ready. The board chair / lead independent director opens a specific standing conversation with the founder-CEO. The board adds a director with CEO-transition experience. The founder-CEO engages an executive coach if they have not already.
- **12 months out — scope the role, retain the search partner.** Signal 1 past-threshold, signal 2 strained-fit, signal 3 composition-ready, signal 4 forcing-event-12-24mo, signal 5 examined-and-ambivalent-or-ready. The board formally scopes the successor role. A specific search partner is retained. The founder-transition contract is drafted. Chapter 2 develops this workstream.
- **6 months out — candidates in play, founder-transition contract in draft.** Signal 1 past-threshold, signal 2 mis-fit, signal 3 composition-ready, signal 4 forcing-event-6-12mo, signal 5 examined-and-ready. Candidates are being met by the founder-CEO and the search committee. The founder-transition contract is being negotiated in parallel. Chapter 3 develops this workstream.
- **90 days out — hand-off choreography active.** All signals resolved; the incoming CEO is named; the announcement is being prepared; the onboarding programme is being scheduled. Chapter 3 develops the hand-off.
- **Already overdue — compressed-search-under-pressure, damage-limitation regime.** Any combination where signal 4 is forcing-event-imminent and the other signals have not been read in advance, or any combination where signal 2 has reached acute-mis-fit and the board is only now noticing. The workstream shifts to damage-limitation: retain the search partner immediately, consider a fractional / interim-CEO bridge (chapter 2), name a specific board committee to run the succession, engage counsel on the founder-transition-under-pressure contract.

**A specific worked-example composite.** A $75M ARR B2B-software company, 300 headcount, founder-CEO 8 years into the seat, board composed of the founder plus three venture-stage investor-directors plus one independent (a former CFO of a mid-cap public company). The founder-CEO's calendar audit shows 40% executive-team management (which the founder finds draining), 20% board / investor time, 25% product-and-vision (which the founder finds energising), 15% customer time. The next capital event is a planned crossover round in ~15 months, potentially onto an IPO track. The founder has recently engaged an executive coach and has raised the succession question with their spouse and one peer-founder confidant. Composite read: signal 1 = past-threshold, signal 2 = strained-fit, signal 3 = partial-bench (needs to add a CEO-transition-experienced director), signal 4 = forcing-event-12-24mo, signal 5 = examined-and-ambivalent. Timing horizon: 12–18 months out. Next actions: board adds a director with CEO-transition experience in the next 90 days, board chair / lead director opens a specific standing conversation with the founder-CEO, the founder-CEO continues coaching, no search partner retained yet.

## What this signal reading does *not* do

Three specific clarifications, because founder-CEOs and boards sometimes misread the discipline:

- **The signal reading does not decide anything.** It produces a timing horizon and a specific set of next actions. The actual decision to transition — including the decision to *not* transition — is a separate board decision that depends on candidate quality, founder-transition-contract terms, and forward-strategy fit that this chapter's signals inform but do not determine.
- **The signal reading is not a performance review.** A founder-CEO can be reading as signal-1-past-threshold and signal-2-mis-fit and still be a strong performer against their current OKRs; the signals are about *forward-stage-fit*, not backward-performance-quality. Boards that use the signal reading as a performance-review substitute typically produce corrosive founder relationships that make the actual succession worse.
- **The signal reading is not a substitute for the specific search-partner scoping conversation.** The search partner will do their own signal read (from a different angle — candidate-market shape, sector-typical incoming-CEO profile, comp-and-terms-market read) that complements the board's read. The two reads together are the input to the actual succession decision.

## Summary

Founder-CEO succession readiness is a composite of five signals — operating scale, founder-fit-with-current-stage, board composition, funding stage, and founder personal readiness — that individually inform but only together decide the timing horizon for the succession workstream. The reading discipline is *continuous* (tracked quarterly by the founder-CEO, the board chair or lead director, and the executive coach if there is one) and *composite* (the timing horizon comes from the synthesis, not from any single signal). The specific output of the reading is a timing horizon: 24 months out, 12 months out, 6 months out, 90 days out, or already overdue. That horizon shapes every subsequent chapter — chapter 2 develops the board conversation and search-partner selection that a 12–24-month horizon calls for; chapter 3 develops the onboarding programme that a 6-month-to-90-day horizon calls for; chapters 4–8 develop the surrounding leadership-transition workstreams that a well-run succession pulls on.

The chapter that follows turns to the specific board conversation on succession and the executive-search-partner selection that translates the signal reading into an actionable workstream.
