# Choreographing the Board Conversation and Selecting an Executive-Search Partner

## Why this matters

Between the founder-CEO's composite signal-reading (chapter 1) and the incoming-CEO's onboarding programme (chapter 3) sits a specific 6–12-month workstream that determines almost everything about how the transition lands: the *board conversation* — how the succession decision moves from a private read in the founder-CEO's head to a specific board decision with a named search partner, a scoped role, and a running candidate pipeline. Founders and boards routinely underestimate this workstream. They assume the hard part is finding the CEO; in fact the hard part is (a) getting the conversation started with the confidentiality and pace the founder can tolerate, (b) reaching board alignment on the role scope before any candidate is met, (c) selecting a search partner whose specific practice-area matches the specific role scope, and (d) running the search without the founder-CEO's ambivalence leaking into the candidate market. Each of these has a specific choreography — one the founder-CEO, the board chair or lead director, and the search partner develop *together* — and the specific pathologies of a mis-choreographed succession trace back to a specific mis-step in this workstream.

This chapter installs the board-conversation-and-search-partner-selection workstream. It assumes the composite signal-reading from chapter 1 has landed at a specific timing horizon (typically 12–24 months out) and picks up at the pre-conversation between the founder-CEO and the board chair or lead independent director.

> Reminder: this module is education, not executive-search or executive-coaching advice. The specific search-firm typology below is a founder / board framework for scoping the RFP, not an endorsement of any specific firm for any specific search. Every live search retains a specific partner at a specific firm on a specific engagement letter with specific terms; the framework informs the selection but does not substitute for a specific scoping conversation with two or three candidate firms.

## The pre-conversation — founder-CEO to board chair or lead independent director

The specific first move of a well-choreographed succession is a *private, off-cycle, off-agenda* conversation between the founder-CEO and a specific single board member — typically the board chair (if the chair is independent of the founder-CEO) or the lead independent director. This conversation is not a board meeting agenda item, not a board-executive-session item, and not documented in board minutes. It is a specific relationship-preserving 1:1 that lets the founder-CEO raise the succession question at a specific low-commitment altitude.

**Why this specific first move matters.** Three specific reasons:

- **Confidentiality boundary.** The founder-CEO needs to be able to explore the succession question without any risk of it becoming a "the CEO is considering stepping down" narrative — inside the executive team, inside the investor base, inside the acquirer / partner / customer network, or inside the press. A single trusted board interlocutor is the narrow confidentiality channel that lets the founder-CEO think out loud without triggering a cascade.
- **Founder-first-mover discipline.** As chapter 1 developed on signal 5, the founder-CEO must raise the succession question first. If the board raises it first — even with the best intentions — the founder-CEO reads the raise as a specific move against them and the trust that the workstream depends on is compromised. The pre-conversation is the specific instrument that lets the founder move first at a low altitude.
- **Chair / lead-director calibration.** The chair or lead director gets the specific first read of the founder-CEO's actual state (as opposed to the founder-CEO's board-facing state). This calibration is what lets the chair / lead director then bring the conversation to the full board with the specific framing the founder can support.

**The specific choreography of the pre-conversation.** Typical patterns:

- **Founder-CEO requests a specific standing 1:1** with the board chair or lead director — off the ordinary board-meeting cadence, typically an in-person coffee / dinner / walk, ideally in a specific neutral setting outside the office.
- **Founder-CEO frames the conversation as an exploration**, not a decision: "I've been reading the signals and I want to think out loud with you about what the next 24 months should look like from my seat." The specific framing gives the chair / lead director the low-altitude opening.
- **Chair / lead director listens more than talks in the first pre-conversation.** The specific value of the pre-conversation is not that the chair / lead director offers a plan — it is that they hear the founder-CEO's actual reading of signals 1–5, ask the specific diagnostic questions (chapter 1), and reflect the reading back.
- **Chair / lead director does not raise the search question in the first pre-conversation** unless the founder-CEO does. The specific discipline is to let the founder-CEO name the pace — "I think we should start the conversation now," or "I want to keep reading the signals for another six months" — and follow that pace in the first pre-conversation.
- **Chair / lead director schedules the second pre-conversation** typically 30–60 days later, and uses the intervening period to consider (privately) the specific board-conversation choreography the founder's actual reading calls for.

**The specific pathology of skipping the pre-conversation.** The most damaging succession failures often trace back to a founder-CEO who raised the succession question directly at a scheduled board meeting or at a board-executive-session — either because the founder felt the topic was urgent enough to skip the pre-conversation, or because there was no chair / lead director with whom the pre-conversation was possible. Raising the question in front of the full board (including investor-directors whose fiduciary duty is to the fund's LPs, not the founder's personal narrative) exposes the founder-CEO to a specific dynamic where the investor-directors — reading their own signal 4 (funding stage) — may push the succession onto a much more compressed timeline than the founder is ready for. The pre-conversation is the specific instrument that lets the founder retain pace-control on the workstream.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The founder-CEO who is *also* the board chair, and who therefore does not have a natural pre-conversation interlocutor. In this case the specific discipline is to install a lead independent director *before* raising the succession question — a specific move that requires a formal board action (adding the LID designation to the board charter or the equivalent private-company document) but that is worth the specific overhead for the confidentiality-and-pace-control the LID relationship provides. Chapter 5 develops the independent-chair pattern that structurally addresses the same question at a higher altitude.

## The confidentiality boundary

Once the pre-conversation begins, the founder-CEO faces a specific confidentiality question: with whom else — outside the pre-conversation with the chair / lead director — can they talk about the succession?

**The specific tight-boundary rule.** The empirical convention across the Charan / Nadler / Bower / First Round canon is a specific tight boundary:

- **Spouse or life partner.** The founder's specific personal partner, who will live with the consequences of the transition and whose read on the founder's personal readiness is often the most honest one available.
- **One or two peer-founder confidants.** Founders who have been through their own succession (in either direction — as the outgoing founder or as the incoming CEO into a founder-CEO seat) and can offer specific pattern-recognition. The confidant should be someone with no financial or operational stake in the outcome — a peer-founder friend, not an investor, not a current or prior board member.
- **The executive coach, if there is one.** The coach's specific role in reading signal 5 (chapter 1) makes them a natural interlocutor. The coach relationship is bound by specific coaching-ethics confidentiality that most other advisor relationships do not have.
- **The board chair or lead independent director.** As above.

**Who is *not* in the confidentiality boundary.** Explicitly:

- **The rest of the board** — until the pre-conversation with the chair / lead director has resolved into a specific plan for the board conversation.
- **The rest of the executive team** — including the CFO, the president / COO, the CHRO, the CTO, the CRO, the GC. Even in a founder-CEO seat where the executive team is very close, raising the succession question at the executive team creates specific dynamics (competitive positioning among direct reports, retention-and-loyalty questions, information-leakage to the investor base) that the pre-conversation choreography is specifically designed to avoid.
- **The investor base outside the board.** Even a specific trusted early investor who is not on the board carries specific fund-and-LP fiduciary dynamics that make them an inappropriate confidant.
- **The press.** Even a specific trusted journalist relationship carries specific "off the record" boundary risks that the specific fragility of the succession conversation cannot absorb.
- **Any specific candidate for the incoming-CEO seat.** Founder-CEOs are sometimes tempted to have a specific "coffee" with a specific external executive they admire before the search formally begins. The specific pathology is that a candidate who is aware of a founder's private thinking before the search has been formally scoped often becomes a specific candidate the founder feels psychologically committed to, which distorts the actual search.

**When the boundary can expand.** The boundary expands *only* when the chair / lead director agrees to a specific expansion — typically:

- **From "pre-conversation with chair / lead director" to "full board discussion"** — a specific board-executive-session item, typically 3–6 months after the first pre-conversation, once the founder-CEO's signal-reading and the chair / lead director's calibration have converged on a specific direction.
- **From "full board discussion" to "search committee"** — a specific subset of the board (typically the chair / lead director, one or two other independents, and often one investor-director) chartered to run the search.
- **From "search committee" to "search partner retained"** — the specific search-partner engagement letter names a specific set of individuals inside the search firm and inside the search committee who share candidate information.
- **From "search partner retained" to "founder-CEO's executive team"** — a specific and typically late-stage expansion, often only after a specific finalist candidate has been named and the founder-CEO is preparing the executive team for the transition.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The most common confidentiality-boundary failure is the founder-CEO who raises the succession question at a specific individual investor-director's 1:1 (typically the founder's "friend on the board" — the earliest investor, the lead investor, or a specific personal-relationship investor). The specific pathology: the investor-director's fiduciary duty runs to the fund's LPs, not to the founder's personal narrative, and the investor-director is structurally obligated to share the information with the rest of the board. The founder-CEO's expectation of confidentiality collides with the investor-director's actual obligations, and the founder learns of the collision only when the information reaches the wider board without the founder's pre-conversation choreography having been installed.

## Timing rules — the 12–18-month horizon

Chapter 1's signal reading produces a specific timing horizon (24 months out, 12 months out, 6 months out, 90 days out). The board-conversation-and-search workstream itself has its own specific internal timeline that shapes when each move happens.

**The empirical timing pattern.** Across the Charan / Nadler / Bower canon and the practitioner literature (First Round Review, Elad Gil, Bolster), the typical time-from-first-pre-conversation to founder-CEO handoff on a successfully-executed founder-CEO succession is:

- **Pre-conversation with chair / lead director:** initiated 18–24 months before intended handoff. Runs for 3–6 months as a series of standing 1:1s.
- **Board-executive-session discussion of succession:** occurs 15–20 months before handoff. Board reaches alignment on the succession direction and charters the search committee.
- **Search-partner RFP and selection:** occurs 12–18 months before handoff. Two or three search firms are considered; one is retained. Typically a 6–8-week process.
- **Role-scoping and success-profile authoring:** occurs 12–15 months before handoff. The search partner and search committee jointly author the specific success profile the search will run against.
- **Candidate sourcing and initial screening:** runs from 10 months to 6 months before handoff. Typical mature-stage CEO search generates a candidate universe of 60–120, a screened long-list of 20–30, a short-list of 8–12, and a final-round set of 3–5.
- **Finalist candidate meetings with founder-CEO and search committee:** occur 4–8 months before handoff. Typically each finalist has 3–6 meetings across the founder-CEO, the search committee, additional board members, and (in later rounds) specific senior executives and specific customers / partners.
- **Finalist selection and offer negotiation:** occurs 3–5 months before handoff. Employment agreement negotiation is typically 4–8 weeks.
- **Founder-transition contract negotiation:** occurs in parallel with finalist offer negotiation. Chapter 3 develops the founder-transition contract.
- **Announcement and hand-off choreography:** occurs 2–4 months before handoff. Chapter 3 develops the choreography.
- **Actual handoff:** occurs at the announcement date, at a specific board-meeting date, or (increasingly common) at a specific 60–90-day transition-window inside the incoming CEO's first days (chapter 3).

**Why the timeline compresses so often.** Founders and boards frequently report an actual timeline shorter than this — 6–9 months from first-pre-conversation to handoff rather than 12–18. The compression is almost always driven by signal 4 (a funding event with a specific date) or by signal 2 acute-mis-fit (an operating situation that has deteriorated). The specific consequence of compression: the search partner sees fewer candidates, the finalist set is smaller and less-well-fitted, the founder-transition contract is negotiated under time pressure, and the incoming CEO inherits a less-well-prepared organisation. Boards that install the reading discipline of chapter 1 and hold to the 12–18-month timing on the workstream generally produce better outcomes than boards that compress.

**A specific counter-pattern — the "start the search early even if we're not sure we'll transition" discipline.** Some mature-stage boards start the search-partner-selection workstream 24 months out on a specific "not-yet-committed" basis. The search partner is engaged on a scoping-and-informal-sourcing basis rather than a full active-search retainer. The specific value: the board and founder-CEO get to see the shape of the candidate market ("what would the candidate universe look like for our seat?") without committing to a specific handoff date. If signal 5 resolves as "not yet," the workstream pauses; if signal 5 resolves as "ready," the workstream converts to a full search with a running start. This pattern is more common at PE-owned or PE-adjacent companies than at pure-venture-backed companies but is spreading.

## Executive-search partner selection — the firm typology

The specific search firm that runs a founder-CEO succession determines almost as much about the outcome as any other single choice. The search firm's specific practice-area, specific partner-and-associate team, specific sector-relationships, specific candidate-development methodology, and specific past-track-record on founder-CEO successions all shape which candidates surface, how they are pitched, how the process feels to the founder-CEO and the search committee, and how the finalist offer negotiation runs.

**The typical search-firm typology.** The specific firms are the well-known ones; the specific practice-areas within each firm are what matters:

- **Heidrick & Struggles.** One of the largest and longest-established global executive-search firms. Deep bench across sector, stage, and function. Specific technology-and-growth-company practice with a strong pre-IPO and post-IPO CEO-search track record. Typical retainer + placement-fee economics on a CEO search of a fee at ~33% of first-year cash-and-equity compensation, structured as a retainer paid in tranches. Website: <https://www.heidrick.com/>
- **Spencer Stuart.** Similarly large and long-established. Specific strength in board-of-directors work as well as CEO search, which is a specific advantage when the succession is coupled with a board-composition change (chapter 8). Typical fee economics similar to Heidrick. Website: <https://www.spencerstuart.com/>
- **Egon Zehnder.** Zurich-headquartered global firm with a specific single-tier partnership structure (no distinction between principals and associates) that produces a specific partner-hands-on-every-search experience. Deep in board-and-CEO work. Website: <https://www.egonzehnder.com/>
- **Russell Reynolds Associates.** Historically strong in financial-services and industrial sectors; growing technology practice. Website: <https://www.russellreynolds.com/>
- **Korn Ferry.** Large-firm competitor with a specific strength in the "search-plus-organisational-design" workstream — often engaged for a founder-CEO succession that is coupled with a broader organisational-design work (chapter 7). Website: <https://www.kornferry.com/>
- **True Search.** Newer (founded 2012) technology-focused search firm with a specific "true data" candidate-market-mapping methodology that produces more transparent and faster candidate-universe reads than the traditional partnership model. Frequently retained by growth-stage technology companies for CEO / CxO / VP-level searches. Website: <https://truesearch.com/>
- **ON Partners.** Mid-sized boutique with a specific focus on technology, life sciences, and industrials. Website: <https://onpartners.com/>
- **DHR Global.** Mid-sized global search firm with a specific track record on private-equity-backed CEO searches. Website: <https://www.dhrglobal.com/>
- **Daversa Partners.** Specialist in venture-backed and growth-stage technology executive search, particularly on the CTO / CPO / CRO axis, with a growing CEO practice. Frequently retained for the "we're a Series-B/C tech company and we need a specific senior executive who understands our stage" pattern. Website: <https://www.daversapartners.com/>
- **Bolster.** Newer (founded 2020, co-founded by Matt Blumberg and others) "on-demand executive marketplace" with a specific fractional-and-full-time hybrid model. Frequently retained for interim / fractional CEO placements, board-director searches (particularly independent-director diversity searches), and the "we need someone in the seat for the next 6 months while we run a longer search" pattern. Website: <https://bolster.com/>
- **Sector-and-stage-boutique firms.** For specific sectors (biotech, deep-tech, fintech, media, consumer) there are specific sector-boutique firms with deep sector-specific candidate networks. The search-committee's specific first move is often to ask the current board what firms they have used or heard used well in the sector.

**The specific practice-area vs. firm-brand distinction.** A specific mistake founder-CEOs and search committees make is selecting on firm-brand rather than on partner-and-practice. Heidrick or Spencer Stuart with a partner who has never run a founder-CEO succession in the specific sector is a worse fit than True Search or Daversa with a partner who has run three in the last two years. The specific scoping conversation with candidate search firms should ask: (a) which specific partner will lead the search, (b) what specific founder-CEO successions has that partner led in the last 3–5 years, (c) what is the specific candidate network the partner brings to this specific role scope, and (d) what is the partner's specific view on the trade-offs the search committee will face (external-vs-internal, sector-experience-vs-scale-experience, IPO-track-experience-vs-M&A-track-experience).

**The specific fractional / interim-CEO bridge pattern.** For a specific class of succession — the "we need to transition now but do not yet have the right permanent CEO in place" pattern — a fractional or interim CEO can serve as a specific bridge. The typical use cases:

- **Health / unexpected-departure emergency.** The founder-CEO departs (health, family emergency, board loss of confidence with no runway) and the permanent-search workstream is 6–9 months out. An interim CEO (typically a specific senior executive from the interim-CEO market — Bolster, TechCXO, and the sector-specific interim firms — with prior CEO experience) runs the company on a specific 3–12-month engagement while the permanent search is executed.
- **Restructuring bridge.** The company is in a specific restructuring situation (down-round, distressed sale process, PE take-private in progress) that requires a specific interim executive with restructuring / turnaround experience. The interim CEO's specific brief is the restructuring, not the ongoing leadership; a permanent CEO is retained after the restructuring completes.
- **Search-during-integration bridge.** In a post-close situation where the founder-CEO has departed and the acquirer's plan is to install a specific permanent CEO who is not yet in the seat, an interim CEO runs the acquired business for a specific transition window.

**A specific pattern often mis-attributed to the fractional-CEO bridge.** The "founder-CEO takes a specific sabbatical while the search runs" pattern is *not* a fractional / interim-CEO placement in the ordinary sense. It is a specific founder-departure choreography that is best negotiated as part of the founder-transition contract (chapter 3) and typically involves the *president / COO* (chapter 6) taking the seat on an acting basis rather than an external interim CEO. Boards sometimes confuse the two patterns; the specific choreography is different.

## The search-committee construction

The specific subset of the board that runs the search is a specific structural choice. The construction shapes who sees which candidates, how candidate confidentiality is maintained, how the founder-CEO participates, and how the finalist decision is made.

**The typical search-committee composition.**

- **The board chair or lead independent director** — chairs the search committee.
- **One or two additional independent directors** — with CEO-transition experience (chapter 1 signal 3).
- **One investor-director** — typically the lead-investor board seat, whose fund's LPs have a specific interest in the succession going well. Some search committees include two investor-directors, one from a "founder-friendly" fund and one from a "value-maximisation" fund, specifically to preserve balance.
- **The founder-CEO** — as a *participant*, not a decision-maker on their own successor. The specific structural nuance: the founder-CEO has a specific veto-through-relationship (the founder can decline to hand off to a specific candidate, and the practical consequences of forcing a handoff over founder objection are severe) but does not have a formal committee vote on their own successor. Some boards handle this by having the founder-CEO as a formal committee member; others have the founder as an observer / participant without a committee vote. The specific choice depends on board culture and founder relationship.

**The specific "search committee reports to the board" discipline.** The search committee runs the workstream day-to-day but reports the specific finalist decision to the full board for approval. This preserves the specific fiduciary discipline that the full board is accountable for the CEO decision, even where the search committee has done the work.

**A specific pattern often overlooked.** The search committee's specific relationship with the search partner is *institutional*, not *personal-to-a-single-committee-member*. The engagement letter names the search committee (or the full board) as the client, and specific decisions on candidate handling, finalist selection, and offer terms are made by the committee. The search partner's specific practice pattern is to have a specific single point-of-contact on the search committee (typically the chair / lead director) for day-to-day workstream management, but the finalist-level decisions run to the full committee.

## The success-profile authoring workstream

Before any candidate is met, the search committee and search partner jointly author a specific **success profile** — the artefact that names what the incoming CEO must do in the first 24–36 months, what specific attributes and experiences make them likely to do it, and what specific trade-offs the search committee is prepared to make.

**The typical success-profile structure.**

- **Company context.** One-to-two pages on the specific current state — stage, ARR / revenue, headcount, product portfolio, GTM motion, board composition, capital position, market position.
- **Forward-strategy summary.** One page on the specific 24–36-month strategy the incoming CEO will execute — the specific market position the company is targeting, the specific product / GTM / capital-plan moves, the specific specific-transaction path (independent, IPO-track, M&A-optionality, dual-track).
- **Role scope.** Two-to-three pages on the specific CEO seat — the direct reports, the board relationship, the specific decision-rights that the founder-CEO retains (chapter 4), the compensation architecture, the location expectation.
- **The success-profile attributes.** Five-to-eight specific attributes the incoming CEO must have to succeed. Examples: "scaled a technology company from $50M to $500M ARR through an IPO," "led a successful M&A integration at $500M scale," "built and managed a public-company board relationship over 3+ years," "led a company through a specific market-window compression," "recruited and retained a specific set of senior executives at scale." Each attribute has a specific reason-why, so candidates can be assessed against it defensibly.
- **The trade-offs the committee is prepared to make.** Two-to-four specific trade-offs. Examples: "we will prefer prior public-company CEO experience over sector-specific product depth," "we will prefer a specific CEO who has scaled from $50M to $500M over one who has run larger but at slower growth," "we will accept a candidate without prior CEO experience if the candidate has other specific attributes."
- **Compensation range.** A specific range for cash, equity, and total comp that the committee has aligned on.
- **Timing.** Target handoff date and any specific date-sensitive requirements (IPO window, deal close, board meeting).

**Why the success profile matters.** The specific value of the success profile is that it *pre-commits* the search committee to a specific set of criteria before any specific candidate is met. Without the pre-commit, the committee tends to evaluate each candidate against the specific attributes of the *last* candidate they met, which produces a specific "consensus drift" pattern where the final selection is more a function of order-of-presentation than of specific fit. The success profile is the specific instrument that keeps the committee anchored.

**A specific anti-pattern.** The "we know it when we see it" success profile — a search committee that resists authoring a specific success profile because "we'll know the right candidate when we meet them." The specific consequence is a search that runs longer, generates more finalist candidates who don't fit, produces more consensus drift, and often lands on a candidate the committee ends up debating for weeks. The success profile is a specific up-front cost that pays back in search-cycle-time and decision-quality.

## Running the search — what the founder-CEO does and does not do

Once the search is running, the founder-CEO occupies a specific structural role that is easy to mis-play in either direction.

**What the founder-CEO does.**

- **Meets finalist candidates.** Typically starting at the short-list stage (~5–8 candidates) and always including the final-round set. The founder-CEO's specific value is the "will I be able to hand off to this person" read, which no one else on the search committee can give.
- **Provides company-context input to the success profile.** The founder-CEO knows the company best and has specific input on what the incoming CEO's actual seat will feel like.
- **Signals authentic willingness to transition.** The specific most-important thing the founder-CEO does through the search is signal, both to the search partner and to the candidates, that the transition is authentic. Candidates who read the founder-CEO's signal as ambivalent typically decline to advance to finalist status — the specific pattern is well-known in the search community and shapes which candidates the partner can even source.
- **Prepares the executive team for the transition.** In the specific windows the search committee approves, the founder-CEO briefs specific senior executives on the transition and prepares them for the incoming CEO. Chapter 3 develops the direct-report onboarding waves.

**What the founder-CEO does not do.**

- **Meet candidates outside the search-partner-mediated process.** Coffee meetings, back-channel introductions, LinkedIn conversations with specific executives the founder-CEO knows — all of these disrupt the search partner's ability to run a clean process and often distort the founder's own evaluation.
- **Discuss specific candidates with the executive team** outside the specific windows the search committee approves.
- **Have veto power over specific candidates without a specific defensible reason.** A founder-CEO who declines to hand off to a specific candidate on a vague "I don't feel it" basis exposes the search committee to specific process risk. The specific discipline is that the founder's reservations get named specifically ("candidate X has not demonstrated Y in a way that gives me confidence in the handoff") so the search committee can assess them against the success profile.
- **Negotiate the incoming CEO's offer directly.** The offer negotiation runs between the search committee (or an appointed compensation-committee subset) and the candidate. The founder-CEO participates in the strategic direction but not in the specific offer negotiation.

**The specific most-common founder-CEO derailment.** The founder-CEO who, in the middle of the search, begins to have second thoughts about the transition and starts signalling ambivalence to the search partner or the candidates. The specific consequence: the finalist candidates begin to withdraw, the search partner loses market credibility, and the search committee is left choosing among a diminished candidate set. The remedy is the specific coaching relationship the founder should have installed in chapter 1's signal 5 workstream — a coach whose specific value at this stage is helping the founder work through the ambivalence *without* leaking it into the search.

## The pathologies to name

Three specific pathologies of the board-conversation-and-search-partner-selection workstream that derail otherwise-well-designed successions:

- **The "we already know who it will be" pathology.** A founder-CEO or board that pre-decides the successor (typically the president / COO, chapter 6) and runs the search as a specific box-check. The specific consequence: the pre-decided candidate is often correct (which is why they were pre-decided), but the process runs without the specific comparison-set discipline the search generates, and if the pre-decided candidate falls through the search committee is left restarting from scratch under time pressure.
- **The "let's talk to a lot of people" pathology.** The opposite failure — a search committee that resists narrowing the finalist set and continues to expand the candidate universe past the specific short-list-to-finalist transition. The specific consequence: search fatigue, candidate withdrawal, and a compressed offer-negotiation at the end that produces worse terms.
- **The "we hired a search firm so we're done" pathology.** A search committee that assumes the search firm's engagement replaces the committee's own work. The specific consequence: the success profile is thin, the finalist decision drifts, and the founder-transition contract (chapter 3) is under-negotiated because no one on the committee was tracking that workstream.

## Summary

The board conversation and search-partner selection is the specific workstream that translates the founder-CEO's composite signal reading (chapter 1) into an active succession. It runs on a specific pace: pre-conversation between founder-CEO and chair / lead independent director 18–24 months out, board-executive-session and search-committee chartering 15–20 months out, search-partner RFP and selection 12–18 months out, success-profile authoring 12–15 months out, candidate sourcing and short-listing 10–6 months out, finalist selection and offer negotiation 5–3 months out, and handoff at 3 months out or less. The specific confidentiality boundary is tight (spouse, one or two peer-founder confidants, executive coach, chair / lead director, and expanding only with explicit chair / lead-director consent); the specific search-firm typology is broad (Heidrick, Spencer Stuart, Egon Zehnder, Russell Reynolds, Korn Ferry, True Search, ON Partners, DHR, Daversa, Bolster, plus sector-boutique firms), and the specific selection is on partner-and-practice more than firm-brand; the specific fractional / interim-CEO bridge is a legitimate pattern for emergency, restructuring, or search-during-integration situations. The founder-CEO participates in the search but does not run it; the search committee runs the search under the board's fiduciary authority; the search partner sources candidates, manages the pipeline, and stewards the offer negotiation.

The chapter that follows turns to the specific 30 / 60 / 90 / 180-day onboarding programme for the incoming CEO — the operational spine that determines whether the search's output actually lands.
