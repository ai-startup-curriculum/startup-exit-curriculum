# The Going-Public Decision Framework — Readiness Metrics, Market Window, and the Board Conversation

## Why this matters

An initial public offering is a one-shot governance and capital-markets event. A company that files an S-1 with the SEC, prices at the top of a marketed range, breaks issue on the first day, and then misses its first two quarterly guides has not merely had a bad quarter — it has permanently damaged its access to the public equity markets, its ability to use listed stock as acquisition currency, its executive-recruiting narrative, and its private-follow-on optionality (a public company that de-lists to go back private after a broken debut is a small, distinctive population and rarely a happy one). The "you can only IPO once" framing is not a slogan. The buy-side institutional community — the Fidelities, Wellingtons, T. Rowe Prices, Capital Groups, and hedge-fund crossover pods (Coatue, Tiger, D1, Whale Rock, Lone Pine) who bought the deal — remembers who missed. Broken IPOs trade below issue for quarters or years, forcing an overhang of unhappy long-only holders whose portfolio managers have marked the position and whose analysts have withdrawn coverage. The underwriter syndicate that took the company public does not want to price the follow-on. The lock-up expiry, when it arrives 180 days after pricing, becomes the exit event for pre-IPO holders desperate to escape rather than the natural transition to normal-course secondary-market trading.

The readiness bar exists because the public-market buyer is buying a *forecastable, quarterly-reportable, guidance-issuing, SOX-compliant, PCAOB-audited* asset with liquid secondary-market trading and analyst coverage — a fundamentally different asset than the private growth-stage company the same investors may hold in an earlier vintage. The bar filters out companies that cannot yet produce that asset. This chapter installs the practitioner framework for deciding whether the company clears the bar today, would clear it in twelve months with specific work, or should be pursuing an alternative path (staying private, dual-tracking with M&A, delaying, or selling).

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / tax / accounting / investment advice — a live IPO engages qualified counsel, a PCAOB-registered auditor, an SEC-registered underwriter, and specialist advisors.

The core seat this chapter addresses is the founder-CEO / CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev at a mature or pre-IPO company running the readiness assessment as a specific board-authorised workstream. The chapter's output is the vocabulary and decision framework that lets that seat run the assessment competently, decide with the board whether to proceed, and hand the transaction-execution work to the rest of the module (S-1 drafting in chapter 2, PCAOB auditor selection in chapter 3, SOX programme design in chapter 4, EGC-status navigation in chapter 5, dual-track structure in chapter 6, listing-governance and Rule 10A-3 in chapter 7, EDGAR mechanics in chapter 8).

## Why the readiness bar is where it is — public-market-buyer economics

Understanding why the quantitative readiness bar sits where it does requires understanding the economics of the buy-side institutional book. A long-only mutual fund with a $10B assets-under-management growth mandate cannot take a position smaller than roughly $30-50M in a single name without the position being immaterial to fund performance and non-worth-the-analyst-time to monitor. A $50M position in a single stock, however, requires that the fund be able to buy and (eventually) sell that position without materially moving the market — the standard rule of thumb is that a fund cannot own more than a small fraction of average daily trading volume without market-impact concerns dominating the position's economics. For a $50M position to trade in and out cleanly, the stock needs meaningful daily float — typically implying a market capitalisation of at least $1-2B at listing, with a public float sized so that daily volume can accommodate institutional turnover.

That market-cap floor cascades backward into the revenue floor. A software / SaaS company at $150M ARR trading at typical mature-market multiples (5-12× forward revenue depending on growth and profitability) arrives at a market cap of $750M-$1.8B — the low end of what a long-only book can meaningfully own. Consumer, marketplace, and hardware companies with lower revenue multiples require higher revenue floors to reach the same market cap; a marketplace at 2× forward revenue needs $500M+ ARR to reach the same absolute size. This is why the practitioner rule of thumb sits at **~$150M ARR minimum** for a software / SaaS IPO, **$200M+** for a hot window where underwriters can push the size, and materially higher revenue floors for lower-multiple sectors.

The aftermarket-support economics are the second reason the bar exists. An underwriter — the lead-left investment bank plus the co-managers who form the syndicate (chapter 8 covers syndicate construction) — is not merely pricing and selling the deal. The underwriter is providing aftermarket support: sell-side research coverage (typically initiated at the end of the twenty-five-day post-pricing quiet period), sales-desk relationship-management with the buy-side book, market-making in the aftermarket, and — critically — the *reputation* of the underwriter's franchise with the buy-side. An underwriter who prices a series of broken deals loses buy-side allocations on subsequent deals. Every lead underwriter therefore filters aggressively: the deals they take public need to work in the aftermarket, or the underwriter's next deal is harder to sell. That underwriter filtering is a second, entirely private gate that runs before the SEC filing gate and is often the binding constraint on whether a company can go public in a given window.

The cost of a broken IPO for the company itself compounds beyond the aftermarket damage. Executive equity granted at the IPO price is underwater; retention is harder. The next equity refresh cycle either resets strikes lower (a specific-facts SEC-disclosure and shareholder-communication problem) or accepts that the equity book is not doing its job. Acquisition currency using listed stock is compromised — a target that will accept public-company stock at $30/share when the stock trades at $18 is either desperate or extracting a specific-facts premium. The IR function absorbs quarters of aftermarket-holder complaints. The board absorbs quarters of narrative-repair work. None of this is fatal but all of it is expensive; running the readiness assessment to a specific bar before deciding to file is the discipline that avoids the entire scenario.

## Quantitative readiness metrics — the practitioner rules of thumb

The buy-side institutional community and the underwriter analyst benches converge on a specific set of quantitative signals when triaging IPO candidates. These are rules of thumb, not laws — every metric has sector variation and every window relaxes or tightens the specific thresholds — but a company that clears these signals is a plausible candidate; a company that misses several is usually not, whatever the founder's or the board's conviction.

**Revenue floor and ARR.** The practical minimum ARR for a software / SaaS IPO is ~$150M with meaningful visibility toward $200M in the following four quarters. In hot windows (2020-2021, mid-2024, 2025), the underwriter community will push the floor lower for companies with exceptional growth profiles — a 100%+ YoY grower at $100M ARR may be pushable in a hot window — but the base-case floor is $150M. Consumer, marketplace, and hardware companies typically need materially more revenue at listing to reach the same market cap, given their lower revenue multiples; a consumer / e-commerce candidate typically targets $300M+ revenue, a marketplace $500M+ GMV-driven revenue, a hardware company $250M+ revenue. Fintech candidates vary by sub-sector but typically fall between SaaS and consumer floors.

**Growth rate.** Year-over-year revenue growth at IPO is the single strongest predictor of aftermarket stock performance in the two years post-listing, according to repeated Meritech and Bessemer analyses of the SaaS IPO cohort. The practitioner floor is **30%+ YoY growth** at the last completed fiscal year and forward guidance. Below 30%, the deal prices at compressed multiples and the aftermarket rarely rewards it; above 40-50%, the deal can price at premium multiples and the aftermarket rewards it disproportionately. A company decelerating through the IPO window — 60% last year, 40% this year, 25% guided — is a materially harder deal to sell than a company holding growth flat at 40% across the same window. Predictable growth is more valuable than high-but-decelerating growth.

**Rule of 40.** The SaaS-durability signal underwriters use is *growth rate percentage plus free-cash-flow margin percentage exceeding 40*. A company growing 40% with a 0% FCF margin clears Rule of 40; a company growing 30% with a 10% FCF margin clears Rule of 40; a company growing 60% with a -20% FCF margin clears Rule of 40. Companies that clear Rule of 40 comfortably (60+) price at premium multiples; companies that miss (30-) price at compressed multiples or do not price at all. The Rule of 40 has become an almost-universal underwriter-analyst framing question and shows up in every IPO pitch deck.

**Gross margin.** For SaaS, the market expects **70%+ gross margins** at IPO — a signal that the underlying unit economics scale. Companies with structurally lower gross margins (usage-based infrastructure with heavy cloud pass-through, consumer marketplaces with take-rate compression, hardware) can go public at lower gross margins but need to compensate with stronger unit economics elsewhere or with the sector's own margin benchmark. The gross-margin conversation at the underwriter analyst bench is always framed against sector comparables.

**Net Revenue Retention.** For enterprise SaaS, the market expects **115%+ NRR** at IPO — the signal that the installed base grows organically even without new-logo acquisition. 120%+ prices at premium multiples; 110-115% is acceptable; below 105% is a specific-facts question for the underwriter. For SMB SaaS and consumer subscription, the NRR floor is meaningfully lower (100%+ is the typical acceptable band) because churn dynamics are structurally different at the smaller-customer end.

**Payback period and CAC efficiency.** The **CAC payback** — the number of months of gross-margin-adjusted revenue required to recover the fully-loaded customer-acquisition cost on a new customer — is a secondary signal but shows up in every underwriter model. Enterprise SaaS at 12-24 months of payback is healthy; below 12 months is exceptional; above 30 months is a red flag. The **magic number** (net new ARR divided by prior-quarter sales-and-marketing expense) is the underwriter's shorthand for capital efficiency of growth.

**Revenue concentration.** Public-market disclosure rules (Reg S-K Item 101, plus the general anti-fraud disclosure obligations) require disclosure of any customer accounting for 10% or more of revenue. A revenue concentration where a single customer exceeds 10% is not disqualifying but is a specific-facts negative — the deal becomes a bet on that customer's continuation as well as on the company's growth. Underwriters strongly prefer a revenue base where the top-ten customers are each below 10% and the top-customer concentration is below 15%. Companies with structural concentration (a company with one anchor cloud-marketplace-partner, or a company selling into a small set of hyperscalers) need to address the concentration narrative explicitly.

**Predictability — the "beat and raise" signal.** The most-underweighted quantitative signal is the finance function's demonstrated ability to *forecast quarterly revenue with accuracy sufficient to beat-and-raise for the first four to eight post-IPO quarters*. Public-market investors reward beat-and-raise sequences and punish first-quarter guidance misses disproportionately. A finance function that has been running quarterly forecasting with a rolling forecast, a hardened bookings-to-revenue model, and a demonstrated eight-quarter track record of hitting internal plan within 1-2% is ready; a finance function that has been running annual planning with quarterly reforecasts and a 10%+ variance track record is not. The forecasting discipline is what underwriter CFO-diligence sessions test in the pre-filing readiness assessment.

## Qualitative readiness — organisation, board, and finance-function maturity

Beyond the quantitative signals, a specific set of qualitative readiness signals determines whether the company can operate as a public company on day one. These are the signals that show up in the underwriter's organisational-readiness assessment and in the audit committee's own readiness self-review.

**Series C+ operating maturity.** The company operates with the process rigour of a growth-stage-and-beyond organisation — documented sales methodology, formal deal-desk approval, quarterly business reviews, structured forecasting cadence, a competent middle-management layer, and an executive team that has each operated at scale. Pre-Series-C companies occasionally IPO on the strength of exceptional growth, but the qualitative-readiness bar is materially harder to clear at earlier stages.

**IPO-experienced CFO.** The market strongly prefers a CFO who has taken a company public before, or has been the deputy CFO / VP-Finance of a public company. The reasons are practical: the IPO-execution workload is enormous and specific (drafting the MD&A, coordinating the auditor's SAS 100 review of the interim periods, running the roadshow, handling the underwriter Q&A, managing the aftermarket IR launch), and a CFO learning the job on the deal is a CFO whose learning curve intersects with the deal's most-fragile moments. Companies that recruit an IPO-experienced CFO 12-24 months before the anticipated filing consistently outperform companies that arrive at the S-1 draft with the founding CFO still in seat.

**Public-company-experienced GC.** The same logic applies to the general counsel. The GC's role during the S-1 process, the SEC comment-letter cycle, the drafting of the risk-factor section, the coordination with underwriter counsel, and the post-IPO Section 16 / Rule 10b5-1 / Reg FD compliance programme is intensely specific to the public-company regime. A GC who has done it before adds compounding value across every workstream.

**Board composition and audit committee.** By the IPO, the board must be **majority-independent** (with independence tested under the specific listing rules — Nasdaq Rule 5605 or NYSE Listed Company Manual §303A definitions), and the audit committee must be **fully independent** under SEC Rule 10A-3. This means at least three independent directors on the audit committee, each satisfying both the SEC's independence definition and the applicable listing rule's independence definition, with at least one qualifying as an audit committee financial expert under Item 407(d)(5) of Reg S-K. Chapter 7 covers the listing-governance mechanics in depth; for readiness-assessment purposes, the question is whether the current board has the independent-director bench in place, or whether a specific director-recruitment workstream needs to run 12-18 months before the anticipated filing. Independent-director recruitment at public-company quality is not fast — a competent search takes months, and the specific candidates most in demand (former public-company CFOs, former CEOs of similar-vintage public companies, audit-committee-qualified financial experts) run their own selection processes on the seat.

**IR bandwidth and function.** The investor-relations function is typically stood up 6-12 months before the anticipated filing — the head of IR (often recruited from a public-company IR seat or from the sell-side analyst community), the IR analyst who handles the operating model and the analyst-request queue, the IR calendar (earnings calls, investor days, sell-side non-deal roadshows, industry conferences). A company arriving at the S-1 draft without an IR function stood up will build it in flight, which works but adds risk to the aftermarket launch.

## Market-window signals — reading the tape

The public-market IPO window opens and closes on a cadence driven by macro conditions, sector-multiple compression and expansion, and specific idiosyncratic catalysts. The window that produced the 2020-2021 SaaS IPO cohort at 20-40× forward revenue multiples closed abruptly in early 2022 as rates rose and the software index compressed; the window remained functionally closed for most of 2022 and 2023, reopened partially in 2024, and expanded further in 2025 as rate expectations moderated and sector multiples recovered. A company that would have priced comfortably in Q4 2021 could not price at all in Q4 2022 and could price at a materially different multiple in Q4 2025. The market-window question is therefore a live variable in every readiness assessment.

The practitioner tools for reading the window are a specific set of public trackers and comp sets.

**Meritech public-comps analysis.** The Meritech Capital public-comps deck (published on their website, refreshed regularly) tracks the SaaS public cohort's forward-revenue multiples, growth rates, Rule of 40, NRR, and gross margins, with historical time-series data. The Meritech data is the practitioner standard for SaaS-comp analysis and shows up in every underwriter pitch deck.

**Bessemer Cloud Index and State of the Cloud.** Bessemer's public cloud index tracks the aggregate multiples and performance of the public cloud cohort; their annual State of the Cloud report contextualises the year's IPO activity, aftermarket performance, and forward-looking commentary. Both are practitioner-standard references.

**PitchBook and Renaissance Capital IPO trackers.** For IPO-specific data — deals filed, deals priced, deals withdrawn, aftermarket performance, size and pricing statistics — the PitchBook IPO tracker and the Renaissance Capital IPO Center provide the tape. Renaissance publishes the Renaissance IPO ETF (IPO) whose performance is a rough public proxy for the aftermarket performance of the recent IPO cohort. Renaissance also publishes weekly IPO calendar previews that let the finance function read the window in near real time.

**Sector-multiple compression and expansion.** The window is not uniform across sectors — the AI-infrastructure sector may be open at a moment when the horizontal-SaaS sector is closed. Reading the specific sector's forward-revenue multiple against the historical mean and against private-market last-round valuations is the practitioner check on whether *this* company's IPO window is open regardless of the broader tape.

**The window-closing signal set.** Windows close when the equity indices sell off materially over a short window, when rates move sharply, when a high-profile IPO breaks issue and trades meaningfully below offering price in the first week, when a series of deals get pulled during the marketing phase, or when a macro event (banking-system stress, geopolitical shock, sovereign-debt crisis) drives risk-off flow. The window-opening signal set is the reverse — indices recovering, rates stabilising, a high-profile IPO priced at the top of the range and trading up in the aftermarket, a series of positive comparable prints. The board conversation about whether to file into an ambiguous window is materially informed by which side of these signal sets is currently active.

## The "should we go public at all?" question — alternatives

The readiness assessment is not solely a "can we?" question — it is also a "should we?" question. A company that clears the quantitative and qualitative bar may still be better served by staying private, selling, or waiting. The alternatives worth explicit consideration:

**Stay private with structured secondary liquidity.** For companies with strong private-market access, the secondary-transaction apparatus covered in mod-106 (founder secondary sleeves, employee tender offers, structured secondaries, secondary-market-platform-cleared volume) provides meaningful liquidity to founders, employees, and early investors without triggering the public-company reporting regime. A company that can raise growth capital in private rounds at attractive terms, run employee tenders every 18-24 months, and let founders take periodic secondary sleeves may have no operational need to go public and may prefer the reduced-disclosure and reduced-guidance regime of staying private. The trade-off is that the public-market exit is deferred (potentially permanently), acquisition currency remains cash-plus-private-stock rather than listed stock, and the employee-recruiting narrative loses the specific "we're a public company" signal.

**Sell to a strategic or financial acquirer.** The M&A path covered in mod-101 through mod-105 is the alternative most companies eventually take. A strategic acquisition often delivers a materially higher per-share outcome than an IPO for a company at the lower end of the readiness bar (a company that would price a soft IPO may extract a strategic premium from an acquirer for whom the asset is uniquely valuable). The trade-off is loss of independence, integration risk (mod-111), and the founder's own post-close narrative.

**Dual-track.** Running a formal dual-track — preparing the S-1 filing while simultaneously running a sell-side M&A process — creates competitive tension between the two paths and often produces the best per-share outcome. Chapter 6 of this module covers the dual-track structure in depth. The trade-off is cost (two workstreams running in parallel, both expensive), timing complexity, and the risk that neither path closes cleanly.

**Delay 12 months.** For a company that misses one or two of the readiness signals but is on a trajectory to clear them within 12 months, delaying the filing to the following window is often the correct answer. A company at $130M ARR growing 40% with a plausible path to $180M ARR in 12 months is a materially better candidate at the later date than the earlier one; forcing the filing at $130M produces a smaller, harder deal than waiting.

The board conversation runs through these alternatives explicitly. A readiness memo that recommends filing without addressing the alternatives is an incomplete memo.

## Board conversation choreography

The going-public decision is a board decision, not a CEO decision. The choreography that lets the board make the decision competently is a specific workstream owned by the CFO and the general counsel, with the CEO as the sponsor.

**The CFO-authored IPO readiness memo.** The memo is the artefact that anchors the board conversation. It covers the quantitative readiness assessment against the specific practitioner benchmarks (with each metric stated against the sector-specific bar), the qualitative readiness assessment (board composition, audit committee, CFO / GC bench, IR function, forecasting discipline), the market-window read (with specific comp-set analysis and window signals), the alternatives analysis (stay private / sell / dual-track / delay), and a specific recommendation with the timeline that recommendation implies. The memo is drafted 4-8 weeks before the board meeting at which the decision is discussed; it is circulated to the board 2 weeks in advance with time for follow-up questions before the meeting. Lab-01 of this module operationalises the memo drafting.

**The readiness committee.** For most companies approaching the going-public decision, the board constructs a **readiness committee** (or IPO committee, or transaction committee) — a small subset of the board (typically the audit committee chair, one or two other independent directors, and one or two investor directors) empowered to oversee the readiness workstream between full board meetings and to make specific execution-timing decisions the full board delegates. The readiness committee's charter is documented in a board resolution and referenced in subsequent minutes. The committee typically meets every 2-4 weeks in the pre-filing phase and every 1-2 weeks during the S-1 drafting.

**External-advisor tap.** The board conversation typically includes external-advisor input at specific stages. **Bankers' informal advice** — a "bake-off" of two to four potential lead-left banks (typically drawn from Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Barclays, plus specialist boutiques like Allen & Company for media, or specialist mid-market firms for smaller deals) — is often solicited in the readiness phase before the formal underwriter selection. Each bank provides a preliminary market-window read, a preliminary comp-set analysis, a preliminary valuation range, and a preliminary syndicate-construction proposal. The board reads across the banks' views to triangulate the honest read on the window. **IPO counsel selection** — the specific law firm that will drive the S-1 drafting and SEC-comment cycle — is typically decided in the readiness phase and typically drawn from firms with deep public-company practices (Wilson Sonsini, Cooley, Latham & Watkins, Davis Polk, Skadden, Simpson Thacher, Kirkland & Ellis, Fenwick, Goodwin). The choice of counsel signals the specific market posture the company is taking. Chapter 2 covers the S-1 workstream in depth.

**The decision meeting.** The board meeting at which the go / no-go decision is made typically covers the CFO memo, the audit committee chair's independent read, the readiness committee's recommendation, external advisor input (in person or in written summary), and a specific board vote on whether to proceed to the organisational meeting (the "org meeting" that formally kicks off the S-1 workstream, covered in chapter 2). A "proceed" decision typically commits the company to 4-9 months of intensive workstream ahead of the SEC filing; a "delay" decision commits the company to a specific set of readiness gaps to close before the question is revisited (typically at the next quarterly board meeting or at a specific catalyst milestone).

## The ownership handoff — what this module owns and what it doesn't

A clean ownership statement prevents coordination confusion between the transaction-execution work owned by this module and the ongoing-company finance-function work owned by the adjacent tracks.

**This module owns** the transaction-execution work on top of a functioning finance function — the S-1 drafting and SEC-comment cycle (chapter 2), the PCAOB auditor selection and reaudit programme (chapter 3), the SOX 302 / 404 programme design for the public-company regime (chapter 4), the EGC-status navigation under the JOBS Act (chapter 5), the dual-track structure (chapter 6), the listing exchange selection and Rule 10A-3 compliance (chapter 7), the EDGAR filing mechanics and post-effective disclosure regime (chapter 8), the roadshow and pricing execution (mod-108), the transaction-governance and fiduciary-duty layer (mod-109), and the transaction-communications work (mod-110).

**`startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum` mod-111 owns** the ongoing-company finance-function design — the month-end close discipline, the controller and accounting-manager staffing plan, the R&D tax credit and sales-tax nexus workstream, the payroll-and-benefits infrastructure, the FP&A / rolling-forecast discipline, the cap-table administration platform, the ongoing-company treasury and cash-management work, and the general finance-function scaling from Series C through pre-IPO. The going-public transaction assumes that finance function exists; a company that arrives at the going-public decision without that finance function in place is fixing the finance function *and* running the transaction simultaneously, which is expensive and slow.

The coordination between the two tracks in the readiness phase is that the CFO memo diagnoses whether the ongoing-company finance function is at the maturity required to support the transaction, and — where it is not — flags the specific finance-function build work that must happen before the transaction can proceed. That finance-function build work runs under the ongoing-company track; the transaction-execution work runs under this module once the finance-function bar is met.

## Summary

The going-public decision is a filtered, high-stakes governance choice made against a specific set of quantitative readiness metrics (roughly $150M+ ARR for SaaS with sector variation, 30%+ YoY growth as the strongest single predictor of aftermarket performance, Rule of 40 as the durability signal, 70%+ gross margins for SaaS, 115%+ NRR for enterprise SaaS, sub-10% customer concentration, and demonstrated beat-and-raise forecasting discipline), a specific set of qualitative readiness signals (Series C+ operating maturity, IPO-experienced CFO, public-company-experienced GC, majority-independent board with fully-independent Rule 10A-3 audit committee, IR bandwidth), and a live market-window read informed by Meritech, Bessemer, PitchBook, and Renaissance Capital trackers together with the specific sector's multiple compression or expansion. The alternatives — staying private with mod-106 secondary liquidity, selling under mod-101 through mod-105, dual-tracking under chapter 6, or delaying twelve months — are explicit parts of the board conversation, not defaults selected by omission. The board choreography is a CFO-authored readiness memo, a readiness-committee construction, external-advisor input from a banker bake-off and IPO-counsel selection, and a documented board vote authorising the organisational meeting that formally starts the S-1 workstream in chapter 2.

Chapter 2 turns to the S-1 registration-statement workstream — the drafting process, the specific Reg S-K item-by-item obligations, the audited-financial-statement footprint, the MD&A construction, the risk-factor discipline, and the SEC comment-letter cycle that follows initial filing.
