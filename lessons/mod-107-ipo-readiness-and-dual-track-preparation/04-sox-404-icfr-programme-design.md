# SOX 404 Internal-Control Programme Design and ICFR Testing

## Why this matters

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 was enacted after Enron, WorldCom, and Tyco to restore public-market trust in SEC-registered financial statements. The mechanism it created — a management assessment of internal control over financial reporting (**ICFR**), an auditor attestation on the same, and personal CEO / CFO certifications on every periodic report — is now the most operationally invasive compliance regime a first-time public company must build. A company that files its S-1 disclosing an unremediated **material weakness** does not merely take a haircut on the offering price; it may find the deal repriced, restructured, or pulled. A newly-public company that discovers a material weakness in its first post-IPO 10-K triggers a specific cascade: an 8-K under Item 4.02 if a restatement is required, plaintiff-firm demand letters within days, a putative securities class action, an SEC investigation opened as a matter of course, and a stock-price reaction that in the tracked cohort of adverse ICFR opinions has averaged into the double-digit-percentage range. The CEO and CFO who signed the Section 302 certifications on the prior 10-Q filings face personal exposure — SEC enforcement under Section 302, Rule 10b-5 private-litigation exposure, and, for a *knowing* false certification, criminal exposure under Section 906 (18 U.S.C. §1350) with fines up to $5M and imprisonment up to 20 years.

The SOX programme is therefore not an accounting exercise appended to the IPO checklist — it is the operating discipline that lets the public-company finance function survive the quarterly reporting cadence. It is built in the 18–24 months before the first public filing, dry-run in the year before the first 404(a) management assessment, and hardened in the year before 404(b) auditor attestation becomes applicable. This chapter installs the practitioner framework for scoping, designing, testing, remediating, and reporting on a public-company-grade ICFR programme.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / accounting / tax / audit advice. A live SOX programme is designed with a PCAOB-registered auditor, a qualified SOX advisory firm (typically a Big Four or a specialist), securities counsel, and the audit committee. Every specific scoping, testing, and deficiency-classification decision is a facts-and-circumstances call made with those advisors.

## The SOX 404 framework — Section 404(a), Section 404(b), and Section 302

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 imposes three interlocking control-and-certification obligations on SEC-registered issuers, each with a specific rule-set and a specific liability profile.

**Section 404(a) — management's ICFR assessment.** Section 404(a) requires management to include in the 10-K (1) a statement of responsibility for establishing and maintaining adequate ICFR, (2) identification of the framework used (in nearly every US case, the **COSO 2013 Internal Control — Integrated Framework**), (3) management's effectiveness assessment as of fiscal year end, including disclosure of any material weakness, and (4) a statement that the auditor has issued an ICFR attestation (where 404(b) applies) or that no such report was required. The SEC's implementing rules are at **Item 308(a) of Regulation S-K** and Exchange Act Rules 13a-15 and 15d-15. Management cannot conclude ICFR is effective if any material weakness exists as of the assessment date — a single unremediated material weakness produces an adverse ICFR opinion regardless of the programme's overall maturity.

**Section 404(b) — auditor attestation.** Section 404(b) requires the independent auditor to attest to and report on management's ICFR assessment. The auditor's opinion is integrated with the financial-statement audit under **PCAOB Auditing Standard AS 2201, *An Audit of Internal Control Over Financial Reporting That Is Integrated with An Audit of Financial Statements***. Section 404(b) applies to **accelerated filers** and **large accelerated filers** under Rule 12b-2; **non-accelerated filers** and SRCs that are also non-accelerated filers are exempt by SEC rule as amended in 2020. **Emerging growth companies (EGCs)** are separately exempt from 404(b) for the duration of EGC status under the JOBS Act — the deferral and loss-of-status triggers are covered in chapter 5. A company that loses EGC status (by exceeding revenue, non-affiliate common-equity, non-convertible-debt-issuance, or the five-year-anniversary triggers) becomes subject to 404(b) in the first 10-K filed after the trigger.

**Section 302 — CEO and CFO certifications.** Section 302 requires the CEO and CFO to personally certify in every 10-K and 10-Q that they reviewed the report; that it contains no untrue statement or material omission and fairly presents the financials in all material respects; that they are responsible for disclosure controls and ICFR and have designed, evaluated, and reported conclusions on them; that they disclosed to the auditor and audit committee all significant deficiencies, material weaknesses, and any fraud involving management or ICFR-role employees; and that they disclosed any material change in ICFR. The text is prescribed in **Exchange Act Rule 13a-14(a)** and filed as Exhibit 31.1 / 31.2. A CEO / CFO who signs while knowing of an unremediated material weakness faces SEC civil enforcement, Rule 10b-5 private-litigation exposure, and — for a knowing false certification — the Section 906 exposure below.

**Section 906 — criminal certification.** Section 906, codified at **18 U.S.C. §1350**, requires the CEO and CFO to certify that the periodic report *fully complies with the requirements of Section 13(a) or 15(d)* and that *the information fairly presents, in all material respects, the financial condition and results of operations*. A knowing violation carries fines up to $1M and imprisonment up to 10 years; a *willful* violation up to $5M and 20 years. The certification appears as Exhibit 32.1 / 32.2. The exposure is why every mature finance function operates a written sub-certification protocol — process-owner sign-offs, disclosure-committee minutes, and SOX-testing-status memos funnel up to give the CEO / CFO a reasonable basis rather than relying on personal knowledge alone.

## The IPO on-ramp — when the first assessment is due

The timing of the first Section 404(a) management assessment and the first Section 404(b) auditor attestation (where applicable) is governed by the SEC's initial-registration transition period and by the accelerated-filer-status rules.

The S-1 does not require a management ICFR assessment or a 404(b) opinion. It does require disclosure of any material weakness known to management as of filing (a risk-factor and MD&A disclosure covered in chapter 2), and the auditor's PCAOB audit of the S-1 financials obtains an ICFR understanding sufficient to plan the audit — but neither attestation appears in the S-1 itself.

Under the SEC's transition rule (2004 Section 404 adopting release, confirmed in subsequent staff guidance), a newly-public issuer is not required to include the 404(a) assessment in its **first** 10-K. The first assessment is due in the **second** 10-K after the IPO — a company pricing in mid-2025 with a first 10-K in early 2026 for FY2025 makes its first 404(a) assessment in the 10-K filed in early 2027 for FY2026. The programme design work must therefore be substantially complete — with a first full-cycle TOE — throughout the fiscal year subject to the first assessment.

For non-EGCs, 404(b) applies at the same time as 404(a). For EGCs, 404(b) is deferred; the first 404(b) opinion appears in the first 10-K after EGC status ends. The accelerated-filer-status determination is made annually against the second-fiscal-quarter public-float measurement, and a company approaching a transition reruns its 404(b) applicability analysis with the auditor and counsel each year at that measurement date.

## Filer-status definitions — accelerated, large accelerated, non-accelerated, and SRC

**Rule 12b-2** defines the filer categories driving Form 10-K deadlines and 404(b) applicability; the current text (following the SEC's March 2020 amendments) is the governing standard. Public float is measured as of the last business day of the issuer's most recently completed second fiscal quarter.

- **Non-accelerated filer.** Does not meet accelerated thresholds. 90-day 10-K, 45-day 10-Q. Exempt from 404(b) under the 2020 amendments' revenue-based carve-out.
- **Accelerated filer.** Public float **$75M or more but less than $700M**, subject to Exchange Act reporting for at least 12 months, has filed at least one annual report, and — under the 2020 amendments — is *not* eligible for SRC status by virtue of exceeding the revenue-based SRC thresholds. 75-day 10-K, 40-day 10-Q. Subject to 404(b) unless separately exempt (e.g., EGC).
- **Large accelerated filer.** Public float **$700M or more**. 60-day 10-K, 40-day 10-Q. Subject to 404(b).
- **Smaller reporting company (SRC).** Under **Item 10(f) of Regulation S-K**: public float less than **$250M**, *or* annual revenues less than **$100M** with either no public float or a public float below **$700M**. SRC status enables scaled Reg S-K disclosure accommodations, and an SRC that is also a non-accelerated filer is exempt from 404(b) under the 2020 amendments.

The intersection of SRC and accelerated-filer status is the specific analytic that determines 404(b) applicability for a smaller issuer — a year-two design conversation with the auditor rather than a bright-line rule. <!-- needs-research: verify current $250M SRC public float and $100M revenue thresholds have not been further amended for 2024/2025 -->

## The COSO 2013 framework — five components and 17 principles

COSO published the **Internal Control — Integrated Framework** in 1992 and updated it in 2013 (transition deadline December 15, 2014). It is the near-universal framework for the SOX 404(a) assessment. Management's 404(a) statement identifies the framework used; in practice it reads that management "used the criteria set forth in Internal Control — Integrated Framework (2013) issued by the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO)." The framework organises internal control into **five components** and **17 principles**.

- **Control environment (principles 1–5).** Tone at the top and culture — integrity and ethical values, board and audit-committee independence, organisational structure, commitment to competence, accountability for ICFR responsibilities. Where audit-committee oversight, whistleblower programmes, code-of-conduct enforcement, and the segregation-of-duties framework live.
- **Risk assessment (principles 6–9).** Specifying objectives, identifying and analysing risks, considering fraud risk, assessing change. Where the top-down scoping exercise lives.
- **Control activities (principles 10–12).** Selecting and developing control activities and general technology controls; deploying through policies and procedures. The operational layer of manual and automated controls.
- **Information and communication (principles 13–15).** Generating quality information; internally and externally communicating ICFR matters. The disclosure-controls-and-procedures layer that feeds SEC reporting.
- **Monitoring activities (principles 16–17).** Ongoing and separate evaluations; communicating deficiencies. Where internal audit, management-review controls, and the audit-committee reporting cadence live.

Every principle must be "present and functioning" for management to conclude ICFR is effective at the entity level. A principle not present and functioning is a material weakness at the COSO framework level, independent of any process-level failure. The mapping of control activities to principles is documented in the SOX programme's **risk-and-control matrix** and referenced in the management-assessment memo.

## The scoping exercise — top-down risk-based approach

SEC Commission guidance issued May 2007 (*Commission Guidance Regarding Management's Report on Internal Control Over Financial Reporting*) and PCAOB AS 2201 both adopt a **top-down risk-based approach**. Scoping walks from the financial statements down through significant accounts and disclosures, to relevant assertions, to the processes that generate those balances, to the controls that address the assertions. It is not "document every control in the company"; it is a risk-targeted exercise focused on the controls that matter to material accounts and assertions.

- **Step 1 — significant accounts and disclosures.** An account is significant if there is a reasonable possibility of a material misstatement (quantitative or qualitative). The scoping team (SOX programme lead, controller, external SOX advisor, with auditor input) reviews the trial balance and disclosure inventory against quantitative materiality (a percentage of pre-tax income or audit-planning benchmark) and qualitative factors (complex judgement, related parties, misstatement history, regulator/investor interest).
- **Step 2 — relevant assertions.** For each significant account, identify the relevant financial-statement assertions: existence/occurrence, completeness, valuation/allocation, rights/obligations, presentation/disclosure. Not every assertion is relevant to every account — for cash, existence and completeness dominate; for revenue, occurrence, completeness, accuracy, cut-off, and classification; for goodwill, valuation.
- **Step 3 — significant processes.** Map each account to the business process that generates it — order-to-cash for revenue and receivables, procure-to-pay for inventory and payables, hire-to-retire for payroll, the financial close and reporting process, the tax provision, the equity and stock-based-compensation process, and any issuer-specific processes (deferred revenue for subscription businesses, loan-loss allowance for lenders).
- **Step 4 — specific controls.** Within each process, identify the controls addressing the relevant assertions. This is the **RACM** exercise: each control is mapped to the risk, the assertion, the process, the frequency, preventive-vs-detective classification, manual-vs-automated classification, the owner, and the evidence produced when the control operates.

**Materiality calibration.** Quantitative materiality is typically 3–10% of pre-tax income as planning materiality, with tolerable-misstatement or performance-materiality thresholds at the account level (often 50–75% of overall materiality). Qualitative materiality adjusts for factors that matter even below the quantitative threshold — related-party transactions, non-GAAP inputs, executive-compensation disclosures, segment reporting, known-risk trends. A $50,000 error in an account with a $500M balance is treated differently from a $50,000 error in a $50,000 account because the second is a 100% misstatement of the account — the *relative* size, not the absolute, drives the qualitative call even when the absolute amount is below quantitative threshold.

## The IT control layer — GITCs, application controls, and the cloud

The financial-reporting information systems — the ERP (NetSuite, SAP, Oracle EBS, Workday Financials, Dynamics, Intacct), revenue subledger (Zuora, Stripe Billing, Salesforce Revenue Cloud), payroll (ADP, Workday HCM, Rippling), equity administration (Carta, Shareworks), consolidation (BlackLine, FloQast, OneStream, Oracle EPM), treasury (Kyriba, GTreasury), tax provision (Corptax, OneSource, Longview), and the GL-adjacent spreadsheets that inevitably feed the close — are covered by **general IT controls (GITCs / ITGCs)**.

**The four GITC domains.** (1) **Logical access management** — user provisioning and deprovisioning, periodic access reviews, privileged-access management, segregation-of-duties conflict analysis; (2) **change management** — authorisation, testing, and approval of changes to financial-reporting applications before deployment; (3) **computer operations** — job scheduling, batch processing, backup and recovery, incident management; (4) **system development and acquisition** — new-system implementation, data migration, post-implementation validation.

**The ITGC-to-application-control dependency chain.** Application controls (automated controls embedded in financial-reporting systems — three-way match in AP, credit-limit enforcement in order entry, automated revenue-recognition schedule generation) rely on GITCs to be effective. If a user with unauthorised access can change the credit-limit configuration without a change-management control catching it, the application control's design is compromised. Auditors therefore test GITCs *before* concluding on application-control operating effectiveness — an ITGC failure can invalidate reliance on all downstream application controls, forcing scope expansion to manual controls covering the same assertions.

**Cloud and SaaS — the shared-responsibility model.** Modern finance stacks run on hyperscale cloud infrastructure (AWS, Azure, GCP). The ICFR treatment leverages the **AICPA SSAE 18** framework for sub-service organisations — the SaaS provider issues a **SOC 1 Type II** report (design and operating effectiveness over a specified period) that the issuer's SOX programme relies on for GITCs at the SaaS application. For non-financial-reporting controls (security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy), the provider issues a **SOC 2**. Non-US sub-service organisations issue equivalents under **ISAE 3402**. The issuer's SOX programme documents the **complementary user-entity controls (CUECs)** identified in the SOC 1 — controls the provider expects the issuer to operate at its end — and tests those CUECs as part of its own ICFR programme. A common first-year finding is a programme that relied on the SOC 1 without operating the associated CUECs — a design gap the auditor will require remediation for.

## The control taxonomy — types, frequency, and precision

Controls are classified across several dimensions that drive the testing approach, sample size, and deficiency-classification analysis.

- **Entity-level vs. process-level.** **Entity-level controls (ELCs)** operate organisation-wide — tone at the top, audit-committee oversight, whistleblower programme, disclosure committee, internal audit, period-end financial-reporting-review controls. A strong ELC layer can reduce the scope of process-level testing. **Process-level controls** operate within a specific process and address assertions for specific accounts.
- **Preventive vs. detective.** **Preventive controls** stop errors before they enter the records (system-enforced three-way match, credit-limit blocks, dual-authorisation for wires above a threshold). **Detective controls** catch errors after (bank and account reconciliations, journal-entry reviews, flux analytics). Auditors prefer preventive where the risk is high; a detected-but-uncorrected error is still an error.
- **Manual vs. automated.** **Manual** controls are performed by a person (controller review of a journal entry, ASC 606 contract review by a revenue accountant). **Automated** controls are system-enforced (three-way match, workflow-enforced approval thresholds). Automated controls, once tested for design and one operation, can often be relied on with a single test of operating effectiveness for the period *if* the underlying GITCs are effective. Manual controls require larger samples because operator variability is intrinsic.
- **Frequency.** Daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual controls all appear, with sample sizes scaling to frequency (see testing programme below).
- **Precision.** A **specific-transaction control** operates on individual items (three-way match on one invoice). A **review-of-reports control** operates on aggregated output (a controller reviewing AR aging). Precision drives whether the control can detect assertion-level misstatement; a reviewer who signs off on the aging report without specific criteria (aged balances above a threshold, unusual reversals, specific customer patterns) is operating a low-precision control that will not detect assertion-level misstatement. Auditor pushback on management-review controls almost always focuses on precision — what was the reviewer looking for, how were exceptions identified, what did the reviewer do with exceptions, and where is the evidence the review actually happened.

## Control-documentation artefacts

The SOX programme produces a documentation inventory supporting the management assessment and — where 404(b) applies — the auditor's ICFR audit.

- **Process narratives** describe the flow of transactions from initiation through recording through reporting, identifying input sources, system touchpoints, control points, and evidence produced.
- **Process flowcharts** are the visual companion, typically with swim-lanes by function and marked control points.
- **Risk-and-control matrices (RACMs)** are the core artefact. For each process, a spreadsheet or GRC-tool database lists the risks, assertions, controls, owners, frequency, preventive/detective and manual/automated classifications, evidence, design conclusion, TOE approach and results, and deficiency conclusion. The RACM is the single most important artefact and the one the auditor works from most directly.
- **Test-of-design (TOD) documentation** shows that the control, operating as designed, would prevent or detect assertion-level misstatement. TOD is typically established through a **walk-through** — tracing a single transaction end-to-end.
- **Test-of-operating-effectiveness (TOE) documentation** shows the control operated as designed over the period, based on inspection of a sample with documented procedures (inquiry, observation, inspection, re-performance).
- **Management-assessment memo** summarises scoping, testing, deficiency identification, and aggregation, supporting the Item 308(a) statement. Approved by the CFO, reviewed by the disclosure and audit committees, retained as primary support for the 302 certification.

## The testing programme — walk-throughs, sample sizes, and procedures

The testing programme executes TOD and TOE for every in-scope control across the assessment period.

**Walk-throughs.** For each significant process, at least one walk-through at the start of the period establishes TOD — tracing a live transaction end-to-end, confirming each control operates as documented, identifying design gaps, and updating the narrative and RACM. Walk-throughs are repeated when a process changes materially (a new ERP go-live, a new revenue-model launch, a business-combination integration).

**Sample sizes for TOE.** Practitioner conventions drawn from AICPA guidance and Big Four methodologies scale to frequency:

- Daily controls — typically **25** executions.
- Weekly controls — typically **10–15**.
- Monthly controls — typically **5–10** (often 2–3 from H1 and 2–3 from H2 for period coverage).
- Quarterly controls — typically **2–5**.
- Annual controls — typically **1** (but thorough).
- Automated controls with effective GITCs — typically **1** after establishing automation and GITC effectiveness.

Higher-risk controls take larger samples. All sample-size decisions are documented in the testing plan and subject to auditor challenge.

**Test procedures — the four approaches.** **Inquiry** (asking the control owner) is the weakest and cannot be the sole procedure for any material control. **Observation** (watching the control operate) is stronger but limited to the observed instance. **Inspection** (examining evidence — signed documents, system logs, workflow-tool approval records) is the workhorse procedure. **Re-performance** (independently re-executing the control) is the strongest and is required for certain high-risk automated controls and reconciliations.

**Interim and year-end testing.** Testing runs on a rolling basis with an interim phase (Q2–Q3) covering the majority of controls and a year-end roll-forward extending coverage through the balance of the period with smaller supplemental samples and inquiry about process changes since interim.

## Deficiency classification — deficiency, significant deficiency, material weakness

The most consequential judgement in the SOX programme is the classification of identified deficiencies. **PCAOB AS 2201** and parallel SEC guidance define three tiers.

- **Control deficiency.** The design or operation of a control does not allow management or employees, in the normal course, to prevent or detect misstatements on a timely basis. The base classification — every gap starts here.
- **Significant deficiency.** A deficiency (or combination) *less severe than a material weakness* but important enough to merit attention by those responsible for oversight of financial reporting. Reported to the audit committee (required by the 302 certification and by AS 1301 audit-committee communications) but *not* separately disclosed in the 10-K.
- **Material weakness.** A deficiency (or combination) such that there is a **reasonable possibility that a material misstatement of the annual or interim financial statements will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis**. Under AS 2201, "reasonable possibility" means more than remote (drawing from the ASC 450 contingency terminology). Produces an adverse ICFR opinion from management and (where 404(b) applies) from the auditor, and is disclosed in the 10-K under Item 308.

**The two-gate analysis.** (1) The **likelihood gate** — is there a reasonable possibility that the deficiency could result in a misstatement; (2) the **magnitude gate** — could the resulting misstatement be material. Fail either → control deficiency. Clear likelihood but not magnitude → significant deficiency. Clear both → material weakness. Magnitude considers the *maximum potential misstatement*, not the actual observed misstatement — a gap that produced no actual misstatement in the period tested can still be a material weakness if the maximum potential is material.

**Aggregation.** Multiple less-severe deficiencies affecting the same account, assertion, or process must be **aggregated**. A single design gap in revenue may be a significant deficiency; combined with an operating-effectiveness failure in the same process, the combination may be a material weakness. Aggregation is a management judgement subject to auditor challenge.

**Auditor-vs-management view.** Management performs the classification first and documents it in the assessment memo. The auditor (where 404(b) applies) performs an independent analysis and may disagree — an unresolved disagreement produces an adverse ICFR opinion regardless of management's view. The classification conversation with the auditor runs continuously through the year, not only at year-end, so disagreements surface before the reporting deadline.

## Remediation and re-testing

A deficiency identified during the year can be remediated before year-end so that ICFR is effective *as of* the assessment date, but the timing constraints are strict.

**The remediation window.** Remediation involves designing (or redesigning) the control, implementing it, training the owner, and beginning to operate it. To be effective as of the assessment date, the remediated control must have operated for **sufficient time** to demonstrate operating effectiveness — a facts-and-circumstances judgement, typically at least a quarter for a monthly control and several months for a quarterly control. A control implemented in December of a December-year-end company will not be operating effectively as of December 31.

**Re-testing.** After remediation, the control is re-tested with a fresh sample drawn from the post-remediation period, sufficient to conclude on operating effectiveness under the same standards as the original testing.

**Disclosure.** A material weakness identified during the year but remediated before the assessment date is not disclosed as a material weakness in the 10-K (ICFR is effective as of the date). However, the remediation is a material change in ICFR disclosed under the 302 certification and Item 308(c) of Reg S-K in the 10-Q for the quarter of remediation. An unremediated material weakness at the assessment date requires the adverse opinion and Item 308 10-K disclosure.

**Auditor coordination.** Where 404(b) applies, the auditor's testing schedule and remediation-verification approach is coordinated with management's testing; the auditor typically re-tests remediated controls independently as part of the 404(b) opinion.

## Common material-weakness patterns for first-year public companies

Recurring material-weakness patterns (tracked by Audit Analytics and PCAOB inspection reports) cluster around a specific set of technical-accounting and process areas where first-year IPO issuers concentrate remediation attention.

- **Revenue recognition (ASC 606).** Variable consideration, performance-obligation identification, standalone-selling-price allocation, and the manual-journal-entry layer on top of the revenue subledger. Failures typically trace to (1) accounting judgements without a documented management-review control, (2) spreadsheet-driven calculations that fail the ITGC framework, or (3) adjustment journal entries that bypass workflow approval.
- **Stock-based compensation (ASC 718).** Grant-date fair-value estimation (Black-Scholes / lattice inputs), performance-condition assessment, modification accounting, and the Carta/Shareworks-to-payroll-to-GL interface.
- **Income-tax provision (ASC 740).** Calculated by a specialised group (often a Big Four tax-outsourcing team in year one). Deferred-tax-asset valuation, uncertain-tax-position analysis, and jurisdictional reporting are recurring weakness areas.
- **Complex-transaction accounting.** Business combinations (ASC 805), equity-method investments (ASC 323), derivatives (ASC 815), and leases (ASC 842) frequently rely on consultation with the auditor's national office — a workflow that generates material weaknesses if the consultation is not documented as a specific control activity.
- **User-access provisioning and SoD.** Excessive access, incompatible-duty combinations (posting and approving journal entries), missing quarterly access reviews, and untimely termination deprovisioning are the highest-frequency GITC-domain weaknesses.
- **Spreadsheet-based reporting.** Close-critical spreadsheets suffer version-control failures, formula errors, unauthorised changes, and lack of independent review. First-year programmes inventory every close-critical spreadsheet, apply end-user-computing controls (versioning, cell-locking, independent recalculation, distribution-list controls), and increasingly migrate high-risk spreadsheets to purpose-built close-management tools (BlackLine, FloQast).

## The interaction with the finance-function close discipline

SOX 404 assumes a finance function capable of executing controls within the reporting cadence. A public company with a **75-day 10-K** (accelerated filer) or 60-day (large accelerated) has roughly 30–45 days between quarter close and 10-Q filing to execute the close, run SOX controls, complete the auditor's interim review, produce the MD&A, and file. A function whose month-end close takes 15 business days cannot execute SOX controls in the remaining window — controls exist on paper but do not operate in time to be evidence. A **five-business-day close** (WD5) or better is the practitioner benchmark; mature public companies operate at WD3.

SOX design and close-discipline design are therefore the same project. A close-cycle bottleneck (a three-day manual reconciliation, a two-day spreadsheet consolidation, a manual revenue-adjustment process requiring per-entry controller sign-off) is a SOX bottleneck. The controller redesigning the close for WD5 must simultaneously redesign the SOX schedule so controls operate concurrently with the close rather than sequentially after. The close-discipline build sits under `startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum` mod-111 and is a prerequisite to this chapter's SOX programme.

## The programme construction workflow

A concrete workflow for standing up the SOX programme from a Series-C-through-pre-IPO baseline runs approximately as follows.

- **Hire the SOX programme lead.** Typically a former Big Four SOX senior manager, either as an internal FTE (director of SOX or internal controls) or retained through a SOX advisory firm (Protiviti, RSM, BDO, or a specialist boutique). Owns scoping, documentation, testing, deficiency management, and audit-committee reporting.
- **Coordinate scoping with the external auditor.** Significant accounts, in-scope processes, in-scope IT systems, and materiality thresholds are settled with auditor input so the auditor is not surprised at year-end. This coordination is required by the auditor's own AS 2201 risk-assessment work and is a reason to complete PCAOB-auditor selection (chapter 3) well before SOX construction begins.
- **Document process narratives and RACMs.** Interview process owners, walk through each process, document narratives and flowcharts, populate the RACM with risks, controls, and design assessments. Typically a 3–6-month initial build.
- **Test-of-design and remediate design gaps.** Walk-throughs establish TOD; design gaps are remediated (redesign, new control, process re-engineering) before TOE begins.
- **Run a full-cycle TOE dry run.** In the fiscal year *before* the first 404(a) assessment year, run the full TOE cycle end-to-end — every control, every sample, every deficiency, every remediation, every audit-committee reporting cycle. The dry run surfaces operational failure modes (missing evidence, unclear ownership, sample-size disagreements with the auditor) before results matter.
- **Coordinate with the auditor on the ICFR audit.** For 404(b)-applicable filers, coordinate the integrated audit under AS 2201 — timing, sample overlap, deficiency-classification alignment, remediation verification. The integrated-audit incremental fee typically runs **30–60% above the base financial-statement audit fee**; chapter 3 covers the negotiation mechanics.
- **Report quarterly to the audit committee.** Coverage: scoping, testing progress, deficiencies, remediation status, auditor coordination. Audit-committee-chair oversight of SOX is a governance obligation under SEC Rule 10A-3 (chapter 7).

## The Section 404(b) auditor attestation and the integrated audit

Where 404(b) applies, the auditor performs an **integrated audit** under PCAOB AS 2201 combining the financial-statement audit and the ICFR audit — same evidence used for both opinions where possible, either two separate reports or a single combined report. The ICFR opinion may be **unqualified** (ICFR effective), **adverse** (material weakness exists), or in narrow circumstances **disclaimed**. The first-year post-IPO 404(b) fee often runs above the standard 30–60% incremental band because the auditor is testing the programme against AS 2201 for the first time.

The **404(b) exemption trap** is that a company that has operated as an EGC and never faced auditor ICFR testing must transition at a specific moment — typically the loss of EGC status via the fifth-anniversary trigger, the annual-revenue trigger (currently **$1.235B**, adjusted periodically for inflation<!-- needs-research: verify current EGC revenue threshold post-most-recent-SEC-inflation-adjustment -->), the non-affiliate-common-equity trigger (**$700M**, coinciding with the large-accelerated-filer trigger), or the non-convertible-debt-issuance trigger ($1B over a three-year lookback). The transition is immediate in the first 10-K after the trigger — the same 10-K includes both the 404(a) assessment and the 404(b) opinion. The practitioner discipline is to run a **404(b)-quality programme throughout the EGC period** — documenting, testing, and remediating as though the auditor were opining every year — so the transition is not a scramble to build audit-ready evidence in the transition year. Chapter 5 covers EGC status and loss-of-status triggers in depth.

## Summary

The SOX 404 programme is the operational discipline that lets a first-year public company survive the quarterly reporting cadence: a **Section 404(a)** management ICFR assessment in the second-year 10-K, a **Section 404(b)** auditor attestation for accelerated and large accelerated filers (deferred for EGCs — chapter 5), and personal **Section 302** CEO / CFO certifications on every 10-K and 10-Q backed by **Section 906 (18 U.S.C. §1350)** criminal certification. It is scaffolded on the **COSO 2013 Internal Control — Integrated Framework** (five components, 17 principles), executed through top-down risk-based scoping under **SEC May 2007 Commission guidance** and **PCAOB AS 2201** — significant accounts → relevant assertions → significant processes → specific controls in a **RACM**. The control taxonomy drives the testing programme (walk-throughs for TOD; frequency-scaled samples — 25 daily, 5–10 monthly, 2–5 quarterly — with inquiry, observation, inspection, and re-performance for TOE). The IT layer sits on **GITCs** across access, change, operations, and system-development, with **AICPA SSAE 18 SOC 1 Type II** reports and **CUECs** from cloud and SaaS sub-service organisations. Deficiencies are classified under the two-gate test (reasonable possibility, material magnitude) into control deficiencies, significant deficiencies, and material weaknesses, with aggregation. Remediation is time-constrained by the "sufficient time" test. First-year weaknesses cluster around ASC 606, ASC 718, ASC 740, complex transactions, user access / SoD, and spreadsheets. The programme is stood up 18–24 months pre-IPO, dry-run in the year before the first 404(a) assessment, and reported quarterly to the audit committee under the **Rule 10A-3** governance framework (chapter 7).

Chapter 5 turns to the JOBS Act **emerging growth company** election — the specific EGC status thresholds, the scaled-disclosure and reduced-audit-attestation accommodations (including the 404(b) deferral referenced above), the loss-of-EGC-status triggers that force the transition to full accelerated-filer disclosure and 404(b) applicability, and the practitioner discipline of running EGC-period programmes at post-EGC quality so the eventual transition is not a scramble.
