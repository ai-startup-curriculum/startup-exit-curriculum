# NYSE / Nasdaq Listing-Standard Governance Graduation

## Why this matters

An SEC-effective S-1 registers securities under the Securities Act of 1933; a **Form 8-A** filing registers those securities under the Exchange Act of 1934 for listing on a national securities exchange (chapter 8). The Exchange Act registration triggers a *second regulator* — the exchange itself, acting as a self-regulatory organisation under Section 6 of the Exchange Act, with its own listing standards enforceable through the delisting mechanism. The **NYSE Listed Company Manual** and the **Nasdaq Rule 5000 series** are not SEC rules; they are exchange rules approved by the SEC under Section 19(b), and a company that misses a listing standard — a market-cap floor breach, a bid-price non-compliance, a corporate-governance shortfall — is not filing an amended 10-K, it is receiving a deficiency notice from **NYSE Regulation** or **Nasdaq Listing Qualifications** with a specific remediation window (typically 45 days for a compliance plan, up to 180 days for cure, extensions available for specific categories) that ends in delisting if unmet. Delisting triggers cross-default in credit agreements, removes the security from index inclusion (S&P, Russell, MSCI), pushes buy-side holders whose mandates prohibit non-listed securities to sell into a market with no liquidity, and pushes the security to the OTC pink sheets where analyst coverage and institutional ownership evaporate.

The corporate-governance listing standards operate on a second, quieter enforcement channel — the annual proxy voting recommendations of **Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS)** and **Glass Lewis**. Failure to maintain a fully-independent audit committee under **Nasdaq Rule 5605(c)** or **NYSE §303A.07**, failure to adopt a compliant clawback policy under **Rule 10D-1**, or failure to publicly disclose the insider-trading policy under the 2024 exchange amendments becomes a "vote-against" recommendation on the audit-committee chair's re-election, on Say-on-Pay, or on the entire slate. Institutional holders following ISS or Glass Lewis on autopilot can produce a 20-40% against-vote in a single proxy cycle — a governance-crisis event even if the director technically re-elects. The regime has two enforcement mechanisms — delisting power and proxy-advisor recommendations — and the CFO / GC / audit-committee-chair discipline is to satisfy both from the moment the security lists.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline for the listing-standard governance regime at effectiveness — the exchange selection, the initial-listing standards, the corporate-governance rules under **SEC Rule 10A-3** and the exchange rule sets, controlled-company exemptions, foreign-private-issuer accommodations, and the compliance policies (insider-trading, 10b5-1, clawback, whistleblower, cybersecurity) that must be in place on day one.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / accounting / tax advice — a live IPO's governance workstream engages qualified securities counsel, listing-exchange staff, PCAOB-registered auditor advisory input, and specialist corporate-governance counsel.

## The listing-exchange selection decision — NYSE vs. Nasdaq

Until roughly 2010, the exchange decision was a substantive market-structure and sector-preference decision. Nasdaq's electronic **market-maker** model (multiple registered market makers competing on quote) fit the technology sector — Microsoft, Cisco, Intel, Apple, Amazon all listed on Nasdaq — and the "Nasdaq 100" brand became the specific public-market signal of technology identity. NYSE's **Designated Market Maker (DMM)** model (successor to the specialist system — a single DMM with affirmative obligations to maintain fair and orderly markets in the assigned stock) fit industrials, financials, and consumer brands — GE, JPMorgan, Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble — and the trading-floor opening-bell ceremony became the specific institutional-blue-chip brand signal.

The split has narrowed materially since 2010. Nasdaq has recruited across sectors (financials, industrials, consumer) and NYSE has recruited across technology (Twitter, Spotify, Slack, Snap, Alibaba, and via the direct-listing accommodation a specific set of software companies). Both deliver essentially equivalent regulatory compliance and buy-side liquidity for a mature IPO candidate; the decision is more a *marketing and cultural choice* than a mechanics decision. The lead-left underwriter's IPO-execution team presents the exchange comparison at the org meeting; specific inputs are the branding fit against the company's identity narrative, the peer set the company wants to be listed alongside, the tiered initial-and-annual listing fee schedule (NYSE's initial fee for a large IPO is materially higher, with the largest listings capped at specific levels), the opening-ceremony optionality (NYSE trading-floor bell vs. Nasdaq MarketSite Times Square), and any specific relationships the CFO or board have with exchange leadership. Founders who spend disproportionate board time on this decision have usually mis-prioritised.

## Nasdaq initial-listing standards — Rule 5000 series

Nasdaq operates three tiers. The **Nasdaq Global Select Market** is the top tier and the standard destination for IPO candidates at the scale contemplated by this module; the **Nasdaq Global Market** is the middle tier; the **Nasdaq Capital Market** is the entry tier.

**Nasdaq Rule 5405 — Global Select Market initial listing.** Satisfaction of one of four financial-standards tests: (1) an *earnings standard* requiring aggregate pre-tax earnings of $11M+ over the prior three years with each of the two most recent years positive; (2) a *capitalisation-with-cash-flow standard* requiring $27.5M+ aggregate cash flow over three years plus $550M+ average market cap over 12 months and $110M+ revenue in the prior year; (3) a *capitalisation-with-revenue standard* requiring $850M+ average market cap over 12 months and $90M+ revenue in the prior year; or (4) an *assets-with-equity standard* requiring $160M+ total assets, $80M+ stockholders' equity, and $80M+ market cap. Plus the liquidity-and-distribution requirements: **1.25M+ publicly-held shares**, **$110M+ market value of publicly-held shares** (or $100M+ with specified stockholders' equity), **550+ round-lot holders** (or 2,200+ total holders), **$4.00+ minimum bid price**, and at least three registered and active market makers. **Rule 5505** (Global Market) and **Rule 5555** (Capital Market) apply materially lower thresholds for their respective tiers.

For a typical VC-backed technology IPO of the scale contemplated by chapter 1 (the ~$150M+ ARR SaaS candidate), the Global Select Market is the standard destination. The CFO runs the pro-forma listing-standards check against Rule 5405 at anticipated pricing, confirms each element clears with margin, and documents the compliance analysis in the underwriter's listing-application package.

## NYSE initial-listing standards — Listed Company Manual §102

**§102.01A — general equity-listing standards.** Distribution and size: **400+ round-lot holders**, **1.1M+ publicly-held shares**, **$40M+ aggregate market value of publicly-held shares** for IPOs (higher for other listings), and **$4.00+ per-share price** at listing. Financial-standards tests: (1) an *earnings test* requiring $10M+ aggregate pre-tax earnings over three years with each of the two most recent years positive and at least $2M in each of the two most recent years; (2) a *global market capitalisation test* requiring $200M+ market cap; (3) an *assets and equity test* requiring $75M+ revenues in the most recent year and $75M+ market cap; and (4) an *affiliated-company test* for spin-offs.

**§102.01B — IPO-specific variants**, including the **direct-listing accommodation** — an SEC-approved framework permitting a company to list without a traditional underwritten offering (registering existing shares for direct trading) with specific listing-standard adaptations. The specific SEC-approved *primary* direct-listing variant (permitting the company itself to sell newly-issued shares in the direct-listing opening auction) was approved in 2020 with subsequent amendments. The mechanics are outside this chapter (covered in mod-108), but the §102.01B accommodations are the exchange-side framework enabling Spotify, Slack, Palantir, Coinbase and subsequent direct listings.

## The corporate-governance rules — Rule 10A-3, Nasdaq 5600 series, NYSE §303A

**Majority-independent board — Nasdaq Rule 5605(b) and NYSE §303A.01.** More than half of the directors must satisfy the applicable independence definition. Under the specific transition rules — **Nasdaq Rule 5615(b)** for newly-listed issuers and the parallel accommodation under **NYSE §303A.00** — a newly-listed issuer is permitted a 12-month phase-in: at listing at least one independent director must serve; within 90 days a majority of the audit committee must be independent; within one year the full audit committee must be independent *and* the board must be majority-independent. The discipline discussed at the end of this chapter is to start independent-director recruitment 12-18 months before IPO to have the majority-independent board in place *at* effectiveness rather than *within a year of* effectiveness.

**SEC Rule 10A-3 — audit committee.** **Rule 10A-3** under the Exchange Act, promulgated under Section 10A(m) as added by Sarbanes-Oxley, imposes the substantive audit-committee requirements enforced through the listing standards. Every listed issuer must have an **audit committee composed entirely of independent directors** — with heightened independence that prohibits (1) acceptance of any consulting, advisory, or other compensatory fee from the issuer other than director compensation and (2) any *affiliation* with the issuer or a subsidiary (safe harbour for beneficial ownership below 10%). The audit committee must have a **written charter** covering appointment, compensation, retention, and oversight of the independent auditor (with the auditor reporting directly to the audit committee — a specific reversal of the pre-SOX regime), **pre-approval of audit and permitted non-audit services** (with *de minimis* exceptions), **procedures for anonymous complaints** on accounting or auditing matters, and **authority to engage independent counsel and advisors** with funding provided by the issuer.

**Nasdaq Rule 5605(c)** and **NYSE §303A.07** implement Rule 10A-3 with additional detail: at least three members; at least one must be an **audit committee financial expert** under **Item 407(d)(5) of Regulation S-K** (or the issuer must disclose why not); each member must be able to read and understand fundamental financial statements. The charter enumerates the Rule 10A-3 responsibilities plus additional exchange-level responsibilities (review of the annual audited financials with management and auditor, review of major issues regarding accounting principles and financial-statement presentation, review of earnings press releases and financial information provided to analysts, risk-assessment and risk-management oversight). Chapter 3 covers the PCAOB auditor engagement; chapter 4 covers the SOX 302/404 programme — both under audit-committee authority.

**Compensation committee — Nasdaq Rule 5605(d) and NYSE §303A.05.** All-independent, with heightened independence requiring consideration of *the source of compensation of the director* and *whether the director is affiliated with the issuer, a subsidiary, or an affiliate of a subsidiary*. Written charter covering CEO and other executive-officer compensation, incentive-compensation plans, and equity-based plans. The 2013 SEC-approved amendments implementing Section 952 of Dodd-Frank added the **compensation-adviser-independence assessment**: before engaging or receiving advice from any compensation consultant, counsel, or other adviser, the committee must consider six specific factors (services provided to the issuer, fees as a percentage of the adviser's total revenue, conflict-of-interest policies, business or personal relationships with committee members, ownership of issuer stock, business or personal relationships with executive officers). The consideration is annual and documented in committee minutes.

**Nominating / corporate governance committee — Nasdaq Rule 5605(e) and NYSE §303A.04.** Form differs, substance converges. **Nasdaq Rule 5605(e)** requires director nominations recommended either by an all-independent nominating committee or by a majority of the independent directors meeting in executive session — either structure. **NYSE §303A.04** requires an all-independent nominating and corporate governance committee with a written charter — no dispersed-independent-director alternative. Both require the function operate under a written framework covering director qualifications, independence assessments, board composition, board evaluations, and corporate-governance guidelines.

**Independent-director definition — Nasdaq Rule 5605(a)(2) and NYSE §303A.02.** Overlaps substantially with Rule 10A-3 but adds specific *three-year look-back* tests (with certain five-year look-backs under NYSE for specified relationships). A director is *not* independent if within the applicable look-back: the director was an employee of the issuer; the director received more than $120K in direct compensation excluding director fees, pension, and deferred compensation; a family member was an executive officer; the director was a partner or employee of the outside auditor working on the audit; the director was employed as an executive officer of another company where the issuer's executives served on the compensation committee. Additional tests cover material commercial relationships, charitable relationships, and specified other relationships. Independence is assessed annually by the board (typically the nominating committee) with each director completing an **annual D&O questionnaire** covering the relationship tests and any changes from the prior year.

**Code of business conduct and ethics — Nasdaq Rule 5610 and NYSE §303A.10.** Both require adoption and **public disclosure** of a code applicable to directors, officers, and employees, covering conflicts of interest, corporate opportunities, confidentiality, fair dealing, protection of company assets, compliance with laws (including insider-trading laws — the specific intersection with the insider-trading policy below), and reporting of illegal or unethical behaviour. Amendments and waivers for directors or executive officers must be publicly disclosed (Form 8-K Item 5.05 or on the company website with disclosure of the posting practice).

**Executive-session requirement — Nasdaq Rule 5605(b)(2) and NYSE §303A.03.** Regularly scheduled meetings of independent directors (Nasdaq) or non-management directors (NYSE) without management present. The NYSE formulation adds that where the non-management group includes non-independent directors, the independent directors must additionally meet in executive session at least once per year. The practical implementation is that the executive session occurs at the end of every regularly-scheduled board meeting, chaired by the **lead independent director** (or non-executive chair if the roles are separated) — a governance-structure choice with its own significance in the ISS / Glass Lewis frameworks.

## Controlled-company exemptions — Nasdaq Rule 5615(c) and NYSE §303A.00

**Nasdaq Rule 5615(c)** and **NYSE §303A.00** permit a **controlled company** — an issuer in which **more than 50% of the voting power for the election of directors is held by an individual, group, or another company** — to opt out of (1) the majority-independent board, (2) the fully-independent nominating committee, and (3) the fully-independent compensation committee requirements. The status must be disclosed in the annual proxy or annual report with specific disclosure of the requirements not complied with.

Critically, **Rule 10A-3's audit-committee independence is NOT waivable** — it is an SEC rule under Section 10A(m), not an exchange rule, and applies to every listed issuer regardless of control structure. A controlled company must still have a fully-independent audit committee with a Rule 10A-3 charter and authority.

The accommodation matters practically for two IPO structures. First, the **founder-controlled IPO** with a **dual-class share structure** (founder holds high-vote Class B shares — 10-vote-per-share or higher — and public investors receive low-vote Class A shares) that produces majority voting power in the founder at IPO. Meta, Alphabet, Snap, Palantir, and many recent tech IPOs listed as controlled companies on this basis. The founder retains the ability to nominate a friendly board without the independent-nominating constraint; ISS and Glass Lewis disfavour dual-class structures without sunset provisions and typically recommend against re-election of governance-committee chairs at such companies, so the accommodation trades off against subsequent proxy exposure. Second, the **VC-controlled IPO** where an investor group retains majority voting control post-IPO — less common (VC funds typically distribute shares to LPs on a schedule post-IPO) but occurs in specific structures.

The discipline is (1) confirm controlled-company status with counsel at IPO, (2) draft the proxy disclosure, (3) adopt a fully-independent audit committee under Rule 10A-3, (4) consider voluntary compliance with the majority-independent-board / independent-nominating / independent-compensation standards as a governance signal notwithstanding the exemption, and (5) track voting percentage over time to know when the exemption ceases (typically due to founder secondary sales, VC distributions, or dilutive issuances pushing voting power below 50%). When it ceases, the phase-in rules under **Nasdaq Rule 5615(c)(3)** and **NYSE §303A.00** provide a build-out window paralleling the IPO phase-in.

## Foreign private issuer accommodations

**NYSE §303A.00** and **Nasdaq Rule 5615(a)(3)** permit a **foreign private issuer** — defined under **Rule 3b-4** of the Exchange Act — to follow **home-country practice** for corporate governance in lieu of most listing-standard rules, subject to disclosure. The FPI must publicly disclose the specific US listing standards it is not following, and describe the corresponding home-country practice, in the annual report on **Form 20-F**. **Rule 10A-3 audit-committee independence still applies to FPIs**, subject to specific accommodations under **Rule 10A-3(b)(1)(iv)** including recognition that home-country statutory audit-board structures (the German *Aufsichtsrat*, the Japanese *kansayaku*) may satisfy the independence requirement notwithstanding the different structural form. A US-incorporated company IPO'ing on NYSE or Nasdaq is a domestic issuer and does not benefit from the FPI accommodation.

## Insider trading policy — the 2024 disclosure amendments

Every public company maintains an **insider-trading policy** covering purchases and sales by directors, officers, employees, and specified related parties. The substance is decades-old public-company practice; what changed materially in 2024 is the **public-disclosure requirement**. The **SEC's December 2022 amendments to Item 408 of Regulation S-K** (effective for fiscal years beginning on or after April 1, 2023) require the policy to be filed as an exhibit to Form 10-K (or 20-F) with specific disclosures in the annual report. **Nasdaq Rule 5250** and corresponding NYSE Listed Company Manual amendments in 2024 <!-- needs-research: confirm exact NYSE Listed Company Manual section for the 2024 insider-trading-policy amendment --> reinforce the requirement at the listing-standard level.

The typical policy covers (1) **quarterly trading windows** — trading opens after the earnings release (typically the second full trading day after) and closes at a specified point before quarter-end (typically two weeks before quarter-end, the "quarter-end blackout"); (2) **event-specific blackouts** — additional blackouts during material pending events imposed by the GC on notice; (3) **pre-clearance procedures** — insiders (directors, executive officers, specified other insiders under a schedule maintained by the GC) obtain written pre-clearance before every trade, documenting the requester's confirmation of no-MNPI; (4) **prohibited transactions** — hedging (put-and-call options, collars, prepaid variable forwards), pledging (margin loans or collateralised borrowing against company stock), and short-selling; (5) **hardship-exception procedures** — narrow processes for trades outside standard windows, requiring board or audit-committee approval; and (6) **10b5-1 plan procedures** covered next. Post-IPO administration sits with the general counsel's office (typically the **corporate secretary** or **deputy GC for securities**) with quarterly window-opening notices, pre-clearance workflow (form-based with 24-48-hour turnaround), Section 16 filing tracking, and annual compliance training. The board or audit committee reviews the policy annually.

## Rule 10b5-1 trading plans

**SEC Rule 10b5-1**, promulgated in 2000 and materially amended in December 2022 (effective February 27, 2023 with staggered compliance dates), provides an **affirmative defence** to Rule 10b-5 insider-trading liability for trades made pursuant to a plan adopted while the insider was *not aware* of MNPI. The **2022 amendments** materially tightened the framework in response to widespread evidence of abusive plan design: (1) a **cooling-off period** — directors and officers must wait the later of 90 days or two business days following disclosure of the financial results for the quarter of adoption, up to a maximum of 120 days, before the first trade; non-Section-16 employees have a 30-day cooling-off; (2) a **self-certification** by directors and officers at plan adoption of no-MNPI and good-faith adoption; (3) a **prohibition on overlapping plans** (with narrow exceptions for sell-to-cover and successor-plan transitions); (4) a **one-plan-per-12-months limit** on single-trade plans; (5) **quarterly disclosure** under **Item 408 of Reg S-K** on Form 10-Q and Form 10-K covering plan adoptions, modifications, and terminations by directors and officers; and (6) a **check-the-box** on Form 4 indicating whether the reported trade was made pursuant to a 10b5-1 plan.

The listing-governance implication is that the insider-trading policy incorporates the 10b5-1 procedures and the GC's office administers the plan pipeline and the quarterly Item 408 disclosure. The specific personal mechanics of designing a founder-CEO's own 10b5-1 plan post-IPO — brokerage administrator selection, trading formula (fixed-share, fixed-dollar, price-conditional, VWAP-based), interaction with the founder's overall diversification and tax-and-estate strategy — are covered in **mod-114 chapter 3**.

## The clawback policy — SEC Rule 10D-1 and Item 402(w)

**SEC Rule 10D-1**, adopted October 26, 2022 and effective for exchange-approved listing standards starting October 2, 2023, requires every listed issuer to adopt and enforce a **compensation-recovery policy** for the recovery of **erroneously awarded incentive-based compensation** received by current and former executive officers during the **three-year period preceding a required accounting restatement**. The rule implements Section 954 of Dodd-Frank enacted in 2010.

The policy applies to both **"Big R" restatements** (correcting an error that is material to previously issued financial statements) and **"little r" restatements** (correcting an error that would result in a material misstatement if corrected in the current period or left uncorrected). Little-r coverage substantially broadens scope beyond the pre-existing SOX Section 304 clawback (which required misconduct and covered only Big-R). Exchange implementation is under **Nasdaq Rule 5608** and **NYSE §303A.14**. Compliance requires (1) written policy meeting Rule 10D-1 substantive requirements, (2) filing as an exhibit to Form 10-K under **Item 402(w) of Regulation S-K**, (3) annual-report disclosure covering any restatement that triggered clawback analysis, amounts recovered or not recovered, and outstanding balances, and (4) a cover-page checkbox on Form 10-K indicating whether the financial statements reflect correction of an error to previously issued statements. Failure to adopt or enforce is a listing-standard violation. The "no indemnification" provision prohibits the issuer from indemnifying executives against clawback recovery or paying insurance premiums for insurance that would cover it.

The compensation-committee-owned discipline is (1) adopt at or before listing, (2) document adoption in board / compensation-committee minutes, (3) integrate clawback provisions into every executive compensation agreement (offer letters, equity award agreements, cash-bonus plans), and (4) run the clawback analysis whenever a restatement triggers the policy — a workstream that runs concurrently with the restatement disclosure and requires coordination with securities counsel, the auditor, and D&O insurance carriers.

## The whistleblower framework

The listing-standard whistleblower channel under **Rule 10A-3** operates in parallel with two broader statutory regimes. Rule 10A-3 requires the audit committee to establish **procedures for the receipt, retention, and treatment of complaints regarding accounting, internal accounting controls, or auditing matters, including confidential, anonymous submission by employees**. Typical implementation is a third-party hotline (EthicsPoint, NAVEX, Convercent) with an intake protocol routing complaints to the audit committee chair (and/or the general counsel with audit-committee visibility), a documented investigation protocol, and quarterly reporting to the audit committee.

**Sarbanes-Oxley Section 806** provides anti-retaliation protection for employees (and contractors, subcontractors, agents) reporting suspected violations of federal securities laws, SEC rules, or specified fraud statutes — reinstatement, back pay, damages — through a Department of Labor / OSHA process with de novo federal-district-court review after 180 days without a DOL final decision. **Dodd-Frank Section 922** created the **SEC whistleblower programme** — monetary awards of 10-30% of monetary sanctions above $1M for original information leading to a successful SEC enforcement action, plus stronger anti-retaliation with a direct federal-court private right of action.

GC / compliance-officer administration integrates the three regimes: the hotline is open to any reporter, complaints are triaged for accounting/auditing content (Rule 10A-3 audit-committee routing) versus other categories (compliance investigation), the retaliation framework is embedded in the code of conduct and employee handbook, and the SEC whistleblower programme is disclosed to employees in mandatory training. No employee is disciplined in connection with a whistleblower complaint without specific legal review documenting the non-retaliation basis.

## ESG and human-capital disclosure

**Human capital — Item 101(c) of Regulation S-K.** The SEC's August 2020 amendments added a principles-based requirement to disclose **human capital resources, including any human capital measures or objectives that the registrant focuses on in managing the business** to the extent material. The amendment moved away from a prescriptive checklist toward facts-and-circumstances disclosure with issuer discretion over specific metrics. Typical disclosure covers headcount, geographic distribution, demographic composition, turnover, training, health-and-safety, compensation and benefits.

**Climate — the 2024 SEC rule.** The SEC's March 2024 rule on **climate-related disclosures** (which would have added a new **Subpart 1500 to Regulation S-K** covering climate-related risks, greenhouse-gas emissions, and related governance) has been subject to significant litigation. **California SB 253 and SB 261.** The **Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act (SB 253)** and **Climate-Related Financial Risk Act (SB 261)**, both signed October 2023, impose greenhouse-gas emissions disclosure (SB 253 — companies with $1B+ revenue doing business in California — Scope 1 and 2 emissions initially, Scope 3 phased in) and climate-related financial risk disclosure (SB 261 — companies with $500M+ revenue doing business in California). ## Cybersecurity disclosure — Item 106 and Form 8-K Item 1.05

The SEC's July 2023 cybersecurity rule added **Item 106 to Regulation S-K** and **Item 1.05 to Form 8-K**.

**Item 106 — annual report disclosure.** Form 10-K must include disclosure of the registrant's processes for **assessing, identifying, and managing material risks from cybersecurity threats**, cybersecurity governance including the **board's oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats** and **management's role in assessing and managing material risks from cybersecurity threats**, and any material effects or reasonably likely material effects from cybersecurity threats or previous incidents.

**Form 8-K Item 1.05 — material incident disclosure.** A registrant must file within **four business days after determining that a cybersecurity incident is material** to disclose the nature, scope, and timing of the incident and its material impact or reasonably likely material impact. The four-business-day clock runs from the *materiality determination*, not incident detection — the materiality determination itself is a legal-cybersecurity-finance judgment that must be made without unreasonable delay after the incident becomes known. A narrow exception permits delayed disclosure where the US Attorney General determines disclosure would pose a substantial risk to national security or public safety, subject to specific documentation and periodic renewal.

The ownership boundary is that the underlying **security-engineering programme** — SOC 2 / ISO 27001 compliance, penetration testing, vulnerability management, incident-response playbooks, security-organisation staffing, board-level cybersecurity briefings — is owned by `security-learning`. This module covers only the *disclosure obligation* under Item 106 and Item 1.05 and the board-and-audit-committee governance overlay. Typical structure: the audit committee (or a dedicated cybersecurity / risk committee) receives regular CISO briefings, the incident-response playbook includes a specific "materiality determination" workflow involving the GC / CISO / CFO with audit-committee escalation, and the 8-K disclosure infrastructure includes pre-drafted templates and a rehearsed disclosure-committee workflow to execute the four-business-day filing.

## Anti-takeover provisions and defensive posture

The listing standards do not prescribe defensive charter and bylaw provisions, but the specific amendments typically adopted **immediately before or at IPO** shape the company's takeover-defence posture for its post-IPO life. The typical package: a **classified board** (staggered three-year terms so only one-third stand for election each year), a **supermajority vote requirement** for charter amendments and specified bylaw amendments (typically 66⅔% or 75% of outstanding shares), an **exclusive-forum bylaw** designating the Delaware Court of Chancery as the exclusive forum for internal-affairs claims (upheld in the 2013 *Boilermakers Local 154 v. Chevron* Delaware Chancery decision) and additionally designating federal district courts as the exclusive forum for 1933-Act claims (upheld in the Delaware Supreme Court's 2020 ***Salzberg v. Sciabacucchi***, subsequently facilitated by 2020 amendments to **Section 115 of the DGCL**), a **prohibition on stockholder action by written consent** (forcing all stockholder actions to a duly-called meeting), an **advance-notice bylaw** requiring stockholders to submit director nominations and proposals within a specified window in advance of the annual meeting, and often a **shareholder rights plan** ("poison pill") either adopted at IPO or held "on the shelf" for adoption in response to a specific accumulation event.

The trade-off is proxy-recommendation impact. ISS and Glass Lewis disfavour classified boards, supermajority-vote provisions, exclusive-forum-for-1933-Act-claims provisions, and shareholder-rights plans not put to shareholder vote within a year of adoption. Specific voting-recommendation outcomes vary by advisor policy year, company size, and specific facts, but the pattern is that a company adopting the full defensive package at IPO trades pre-IPO structural protection against subsequent proxy-season voting friction that shows up as against-votes on director elections and Say-on-Pay proposals starting in the first post-IPO proxy season. The full mechanics of activism defence and takeover response are outside this module's scope; refer to **mod-109** for the fiduciary-duty framework governing board response to unsolicited approaches, the *Revlon* / *Unocal* / *Blasius* doctrinal framework, and the substantive activism-defence playbook.

## The independent-director build-out timeline

The practical constraint on satisfying the majority-independent-board requirement at effectiveness is that **recruiting an independent director takes 6-12 months per seat** when done to public-company quality standards. The specific candidates most in demand — former public-company CFOs, former CEOs of similar-vintage public companies, audit-committee financial experts under Item 407(d)(5), directors bringing specific industry or geographic diversity — are running their own selection processes on the seats they accept, and the process from initial approach through reference-checking through offer through onboarding compounds across multiple candidates in a search.

The discipline is to start recruitment **12-18 months before the anticipated IPO** to have the majority-independent board in place *at* effectiveness rather than relying on the 12-month post-IPO phase-in. The typical search runs (1) a nominating-committee-authorised search-firm engagement (Heidrick & Struggles, Spencer Stuart, Russell Reynolds, and Egon Zehnder run the majority of public-company director searches; specific firm choice depends on prior relationships and role profile), (2) a target profile covering the specific committee assignments (audit committee financial expert, compensation committee independence, nominating), (3) a candidate slate of 20-40 names narrowed to a working shortlist of 5-8, (4) chair or CEO first meetings and full-board interviews of the leading 2-3 candidates, and (5) offer, D&O-insurance underwriting, D&O-questionnaire completion, board-materials onboarding, and initial committee assignments. The 6-12-month per-seat duration is the aggregate of these stages under best-case timing; multiple concurrent searches for multiple concurrent seats is the standard pattern for a pre-IPO company building the independent-director bench from scratch. Chapter 1's readiness memo flags this as a specific readiness signal — a board without the majority-independent composition in place at the readiness assessment is a board that requires 12-18 months of parallel search work before the S-1 filing decision, not a board that can rely on the post-IPO phase-in without governance and proxy-recommendation cost.

## Summary

The NYSE and Nasdaq listing standards operate as a second regulatory regime alongside the SEC — with the delisting process as the exchange's enforcement mechanism and the ISS / Glass Lewis proxy-recommendation channel as the practical enforcement of governance shortfalls at proxy season. The exchange-selection decision is now primarily a marketing and cultural choice, with either exchange delivering equivalent buy-side liquidity and regulatory compliance. The initial-listing standards under **Nasdaq Rule 5405 / 5505 / 5555** and **NYSE Listed Company Manual §102** are typically satisfied with margin by the mature IPO candidate; the substantive graduation is to the corporate-governance regime under **SEC Rule 10A-3**, the **Nasdaq Rule 5600 series**, and the **NYSE §303A series** — majority-independent board, fully-independent Rule 10A-3 audit committee with financial expert under Item 407(d)(5), all-independent compensation committee with compensation-adviser-independence assessment, independent nominating structure, code of business conduct and ethics under Nasdaq Rule 5610 / NYSE §303A.10, and regularly-scheduled executive sessions. The **controlled-company exemption** under **Nasdaq Rule 5615(c)** and **NYSE §303A.00** permits opt-out from the majority-independent-board and independent-nominating / independent-compensation requirements for issuers where more than 50% of voting power is held by an individual or group — a specific accommodation for dual-class founder-controlled and VC-controlled IPO structures — but **Rule 10A-3 audit-committee independence is not waivable**. The **foreign-private-issuer** accommodation permits home-country practice with disclosure. Compliance policies in place on day one include the insider-trading policy (publicly filed under 2022 SEC and 2024 exchange amendments), the 10b5-1 framework with 2022-amended cooling-off, self-certification, and disclosure requirements, the **Rule 10D-1 clawback policy** implemented under **Nasdaq Rule 5608** and **NYSE §303A.14** with 3-year look-back and Item 402(w) disclosure, the whistleblower framework under Rule 10A-3 with SOX Section 806 and Dodd-Frank Section 922 anti-retaliation protection, human-capital disclosure under Item 101(c), cybersecurity disclosure under Item 106 and Form 8-K Item 1.05 (with the underlying security programme owned by `security-learning`), and the anti-takeover charter-and-bylaw package trading off against ISS / Glass Lewis proxy-recommendation exposure (fiduciary duty and activism defence covered in **mod-109**). The independent-director recruitment discipline of starting 12-18 months before the anticipated IPO is the practitioner path to having the majority-independent board in place at effectiveness rather than relying on the 12-month post-IPO phase-in.

Chapter 8 turns to the EDGAR filing choreography — the DRS submission mechanics, the S-1 and S-1/A amendment cycle, the Form 8-A Exchange Act registration that formally lists the security, the Section 16 reporting infrastructure for insiders, the Form 10-Q and Form 10-K periodic-reporting infrastructure, and the EDGAR filer-management workflow that runs every filing under specific SEC-imposed formatting and submission discipline.
