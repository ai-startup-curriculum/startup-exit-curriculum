# Dual-Track M&A + IPO Governance

## Why this matters

A company mature enough to file an S-1 is by definition a company whose intrinsic value is legible enough to be underwritten and whose management team is credible enough to be marketed — which is precisely the profile a strategic acquirer or large financial sponsor finds attractive. The organisation that is running the IPO readiness workstream (chapter 1's memo, chapter 2's S-1, chapter 3's PCAOB reaudit, chapter 4's SOX programme, chapter 5's confidential DRS) is almost always simultaneously receiving inbound strategic interest and considering whether to formalise a parallel M&A process. The **dual-track** — the deliberate, board-authorised structure that runs an IPO preparation and a sell-side M&A process in parallel until one path is chosen — is the practitioner discipline for handling that reality. Practitioner-firm publications from Latham & Watkins, Skadden, Wachtell, and Sullivan & Cromwell consistently observe that dual-track processes tend to produce the best per-share outcome across the two paths, because the mere existence of a credible IPO alternative lifts the bid a strategic must pay to secure the asset, and the mere existence of a credible strategic bid tightens the pricing range an underwriter can market. The competitive tension between the two paths is the mechanism.

The dual track is not free — workstream expense roughly doubles, deal-team fatigue is real, the banker-conflict analysis is delicate, the confidentiality choreography is intricate, and the employee-communications posture becomes internally contradictory (an IPO track that wants an exciting, publicly-facing narrative running alongside an M&A track that wants a quiet, need-to-know posture). It also engages a specific Delaware fiduciary posture the moment a sale of control becomes reasonably probable: **Revlon** duties attach, the board's duty shifts from long-term-value stewardship to obtaining the best transaction reasonably available, and the process discipline that produces a defensible record becomes material. The S-1 itself must disclose the M&A alternatives under Item 105 (risk factors) and Item 303 (MD&A) once discussions cross the **Basic v. Levinson** materiality threshold. This chapter installs the specific dual-track governance posture — when to run it, when not to, how the board is structured to oversee it, how banker conflicts are managed, how the go / no-go decision sequences across S-1 milestones, and how the flip is executed if forced.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / tax / accounting / investment advice. A live dual-track engages qualified securities counsel, M&A counsel (frequently a separate firm), separate financial advisors for each track, a PCAOB-registered auditor, and a documented board and committee record.

Full mechanics of special-committee construction, Delaware fiduciary case-law depth, and banker-conflict remediation are covered in mod-109 chapters 1-4. Sell-side M&A process execution is mod-101 through mod-105. Transaction-communications choreography is mod-110. Consideration mix and tax structuring are mod-103. This chapter installs the dual-track *governance posture* — the seat-level decisions the founder-CEO, CFO, GC, board chair, and lead independent director must make to run the parallel process competently.

## The strategic case for the dual track

The dual-track pattern is not "run the IPO and see if a buyer shows up." It is a deliberate, board-authorised, calendar-anchored process where two paths are actively advanced so the market for the company is tested against both structures. The case rests on three mechanisms.

**Price discovery.** An IPO produces a price signal from the buy-side institutional book — the aggregated forward-multiple view of long-only mutual funds, hedge-fund crossover pods, and sovereign-wealth allocators. An M&A process produces a price signal from the strategic and financial-sponsor bidder set — the aggregated view of acquirers for whom the asset is uniquely valuable (a strategic with a product-portfolio gap, a sponsor with a consolidation thesis). The two signals reflect fundamentally different valuation frames — the IPO buyer pays for future public-market trading value; the strategic pays for synergies (revenue, cost, defensive, product-portfolio) and for the option value of consolidating the market. When strategic-synergy value exceeds standalone public-company value, the M&A path wins on price; when it does not, the IPO wins. Running both is the only way to know which regime the company is in *at the specific window*.

**Negotiating leverage.** A sell-side process where the seller has no credible IPO alternative faces a compressed dynamic — the bidder knows the seller must transact. A sell-side process with a documented, board-approved, banker-engaged, S-1-drafted IPO alternative faces a materially different dynamic — the bidder must bid above the IPO valuation range, and a low-ball bid produces a walk rather than a counter. Documented IPO readiness — 2-year audit complete, PCAOB auditor engaged, S-1 drafted, EGC-election memo drafted, SOX programme mature — produces a pricing lift that is the difference between negotiating with an alternative and negotiating without one.

**Optionality preservation in an uncertain window.** The IPO window is a moving variable (chapter 1's Meritech, Bessemer, Renaissance, PitchBook toolkit); the M&A window is a different moving variable driven by strategic-acquirer appetite, sponsor dry powder, and antitrust posture. The two do not close at the same time. A company running both paths can pivot to whichever is open at the moment the decision must be made.

The specific practitioner statistic — that dual-track processes historically resolve into a sale approximately 60–70% of the time when both paths are viable — is a rough number consistently cited in practitioner-firm publications, but the precise percentage varies by cohort, window, and sector. <!-- needs-research: verify the 60–70% dual-track-to-sale conversion statistic against a named practitioner-firm publication with a specific study cohort and date -->

## Full board vs. special committee

A stand-alone IPO is a **full-board** matter. The board authorises the org meeting, underwriter selection, S-1 filing, pricing range, and final pricing; audit / comp / nom-gov committees own their standard remits. The overall transaction is a full-board decision because a stand-alone IPO does not implicate the Delaware change-of-control doctrine that heightens duties under **Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes** (Del. 1986).

A dual-track process may or may not preserve that default. The question is whether the process crosses into **special committee** territory — the structure in which a subset of independent, disinterested directors is delegated exclusive negotiating authority, insulated from any conflicted party. The specific triggers:

**Controller involvement.** A cap table where a VC fund or founder holds majority voting power (a dual-class structure concentrating control, a VC that acquired majority in a down round, a founder with retained supermajority rights) creates a controller-conflict analysis under **Kahn v. M&F Worldwide** (Del. 2014) and its **MFW** progeny. Where the controller's interest diverges from the minority — a preference for an all-cash sale that liquidates the fund's position over an IPO that requires further multi-year holding, or vice versa — the process discipline is a fully-empowered special committee negotiating at arm's length plus a **majority-of-minority stockholder vote**.

**Insider participation.** A founder participating in the IPO through a founder-secondary sleeve (mod-108 chapter 4) or receiving a management-participation stake in an LBO has an interest that diverges from non-participating stockholders. The classic **In re Trados Inc. Shareholder Litigation** (Del. Ch. 2013) fact pattern — common-holder economics compromised by preferred-stack liquidation preference and management-participating consideration — is the canonical illustration.

**Insider bidder.** A take-private bid from a party affiliated with the company (a director's PE firm, a controlling stockholder's affiliated sponsor, a management-led buyout consortium) is per se conflicted and triggers special-committee territory almost automatically.

Absent these triggers, most dual-tracks are run under full-board oversight with a **transaction committee** or **IPO readiness committee** (see chapter 1) that handles execution between full-board meetings without exclusive negotiating authority. The distinction between a transaction committee (advisory, execution-focused) and a special committee (exclusive negotiating authority, insulated from conflicts) is a legal-structural distinction that GC and outside counsel own. Full special-committee construction — charter drafting, independence testing, advisor retention, negotiating authority, meeting cadence, minute-taking discipline — is covered in mod-109 chapter 4.

## The Revlon threshold and sale-becoming-reasonably-probable

**Revlon v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings** (506 A.2d 173, Del. 1986) established that when a sale of control becomes inevitable — or, per **Paramount Communications v. QVC Network** (637 A.2d 34, Del. 1994), when the board has initiated an active bidding process or a change of corporate control is otherwise reasonably probable — the board's duty shifts to obtaining the best transaction reasonably available. Once Revlon applies, the board must run a defensible process — a market check, independent advisor retention, arm's-length negotiation, documented deliberation — supporting the conclusion that the transaction is the best reasonably available.

The dual-track implication is that Revlon can be triggered mid-process in ways the board did not anticipate. A process that begins as "we are preparing the IPO and will entertain preliminary inbound conversations" can cross into Revlon territory the moment a specific bid materialises at a specific price with specific terms and the board begins to seriously entertain it. The transition from "preliminary conversations" to "reasonably probable sale of control" is a facts-and-circumstances judgment that GC and outside counsel monitor. The practical consequence is that process discipline — meeting frequency, minute detail, advisor-input documentation, specificity of price and terms explored — must be Revlon-defensible from a point earlier than the board's internal narrative may suggest.

**C&J Energy Services v. City of Miami General Employees' & Sanitation Employees' Retirement Trust** (107 A.3d 1049, Del. 2014) clarified that Revlon does not require a specific auction structure — a board that runs a single-bidder negotiation with a documented market check can satisfy Revlon if the record supports the conclusion that the process obtained the best transaction reasonably available. The dual-track relevance is that the IPO track itself functions as a market check — the S-1 valuation range, informed by TTW conversations with QIBs and by underwriter pitch materials, provides a documented alternative against which the M&A bid is measured. <!-- needs-research: verify exact citation for C&J Energy Services v. City of Miami General Employees' & Sanitation Employees' Retirement Trust -->

**Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings** (125 A.3d 304, Del. 2015) provides the stockholder-ratification cleansing doctrine — a fully-informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders on a non-controller transaction cleanses fiduciary claims (other than waste) and shifts the standard to the business judgment rule. A stockholder vote on the M&A transaction (a merger vote under DGCL §251) can — with proper disclosure — cleanse subsequent challenges. Disclosure quality in the proxy is therefore the cleansing mechanism. Full Corwin / MFW / Revlon depth is in mod-109 chapter 1; this chapter installs the point that the dual-track process runs against these doctrines in real time.

## Banker-conflict analysis across the two roles

The most-litigated dual-track governance issue is the **banker conflict** when the same investment bank is engaged both as IPO underwriter (or lead-left candidate) and as sell-side M&A advisor. The conflict is structural — as underwriter, the bank's incentive is to see the IPO price and produce underwriter fees plus aftermarket-support economics; as sell-side advisor, the incentive is to see the sale close and produce M&A fees. The two incentives push in opposite directions on the go / no-go decision at each milestone.

Delaware courts have scrutinised banker conflicts intensively. **In re Rural Metro Corp. Stockholders Litigation** (88 A.3d 54, Del. Ch. 2014) held a financial advisor secondarily liable for aiding and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty where the advisor had undisclosed staple-financing conflicts and steered the process in ways favouring its own economic interests. **In re Del Monte Foods Co. Shareholders Litigation** (25 A.3d 813, Del. Ch. 2011) enjoined a transaction where the sell-side advisor had run buy-side outreach for a specific bidder without disclosing the arrangement to the board and where staple-financing compromised its arm's-length posture. **In re El Paso Corp. Shareholder Litigation** (41 A.3d 432, Del. Ch. 2012) criticised a sell-side advisor whose senior banker held personal ownership in the acquirer, illustrating that individual-banker conflicts as well as institutional conflicts drive the analysis. <!-- needs-research: verify exact reporter citations for In re Rural Metro, In re Del Monte, and In re El Paso -->

The discipline that emerges is a **bright-line separation** of the underwriter role and the sell-side advisor role. The two are staffed by separate firms wherever possible — a bank runs the IPO track (lead-left plus co-managers), and a separate bank runs the M&A track (a bulge-bracket firm as sell-side advisor or a boutique specialist like Qatalyst, Centerview, LionTree, Allen & Company, or Moelis). Where a single firm is engaged in both roles — occasionally unavoidable in relationship-driven or sector-specialist scenarios — the firm must maintain a documented **information wall** between the two deal teams, staff the roles with separate senior bankers, and disclose the dual-role posture to the board with specific conflict-waiver documentation. Boards that authorise the dual role typically require independent-advisor input (a second banker for a fairness opinion, a separate advisor to validate the process). Full banker-conflict remediation is in mod-109 chapter 3. The rule of thumb: use separate firms unless there is an overriding reason not to, and where a single firm is used, over-document on the assumption that the transaction will be litigated.

## Price-signalling risk

A public S-1 sets a **pricing anchor** a strategic can use in negotiation. Once the S-1 is publicly filed, the market can compute an implied valuation from the disclosed financials, use-of-proceeds paragraph, post-offering share count, and sector comparables. Even before the specific pricing-range amendment lands (typically 15 days before roadshow, per JOBS Act Section 6(e) for EGCs — see mod-108 chapter 2), the market can triangulate. A strategic buyer negotiating against a live public S-1 knows the seller's floor is somewhere near the IPO range and bids accordingly. The public S-1 has *reduced* the seller's range from "any price the strategic can be pushed to" to "any price above the IPO range plus a strategic premium." This is why many dual-track processes make the pivot decision *before* the public flip, so the M&A process is not negotiating against a public anchor.

The **anti-signal risk** runs in the opposite direction. An IPO that files publicly and is withdrawn — the S-1 pulled via **SEC Rule 477** — sends a negative signal to the buy-side and to the strategic set. The buy-side reads withdrawal as a market-window or company-specific problem; strategics read it as a distressed-seller signal and adjust subsequent bids downward. The pattern of "we withdrew the IPO because we received a superior strategic offer we are now negotiating" can be a positive signal *if* the subsequent deal closes promptly; the pattern of "we withdrew because the window closed and we are now looking for strategic alternatives" is materially negative and persists through subsequent windows. The timing-management discipline is to make the pivot decision, wherever possible, at the confidential-DRS stage — the DRS can be withdrawn or paused without the market observing.

A related risk is that the existence of a public S-1 alters M&A dynamics — strategics not previously engaged may reach out post-filing (a **topping-bid** dynamic). This is often net positive for price discovery but adds coordination complexity.

## The go / no-go decision points

The dual-track decision is a sequence of go / no-go decisions made at specific milestones as information develops.

**(a) At the org meeting.** The org meeting is the board-authorised kick-off of the S-1 workstream (chapter 2). The board formally decides whether to commit to the dual-track structure — engaging separate M&A advisors alongside the underwriter slate, authorising a specific sell-side outreach list or leaving the M&A track reactive-inbound-only, and setting the transaction- or special-committee structure. The alternative single-track decisions are (i) IPO-only (respond to inbound with a "not for sale" posture) or (ii) M&A-only (pause the IPO in favour of a formal sell-side process, potentially with IPO fallback). The dual-track decision commits the company to materially higher run-rate cost across the 4–9 months to pricing.

**(b) At the initial DRS submission.** The confidential DRS (chapter 5) starts the SEC-review clock. By this point, the M&A process has typically run through initial outreach and produced non-binding **indications of interest** (IOIs) from the bidder set — preliminary price ranges based on the CIM and initial management-presentation contact. The board reviews IOIs against the anticipated IPO range and decides — proceed with both, pivot to M&A (potentially pausing the DRS clock), or narrow the bidder list while the IPO track proceeds.

**(c) At the SEC first-comment response.** The first SEC comment letter typically arrives 25–30 days after the DRS submission (chapters 2 and 5). Simultaneously, the M&A process has moved from IOIs to **letters of intent** (LOIs) with high-conviction bidders, with confirmatory diligence underway. The board decides whether to keep both tracks live given specific comment-letter density and LOI terms.

**(d) At the public S-1 flip.** The public S-1 flip — the confidential-DRS conversion to a public S-1, which under JOBS Act Section 6(e) must occur at least 15 days before roadshow — is the pricing-anchor moment. The board typically makes the primary path decision by this milestone — commit to IPO (with M&A wound down or held in reserve for a topping bid) or commit to M&A (with IPO paused, public S-1 either not filed or filed as a signal that a strategic bid must beat). The pattern where the public S-1 is filed with the intent of soliciting a topping bid is well-established but delicate and requires careful board and counsel choreography.

**(e) At the pricing meeting.** The pricing meeting (mod-108 chapter 3) is the final decision if both tracks are still viable at the eleventh hour — the IPO book has built to a specific range and coverage ratio, a strategic bid is on the table at a specific price with specific terms, and the board makes the final go decision on one path. This pattern is rare but not unknown; the choreography requires that the M&A counter-party has provided a firm bid with executed transaction documents ready for signing, so the switch from "price the IPO" to "sign the merger" can happen in hours rather than weeks.

## Using IPO readiness as leverage in a sell-side negotiation

The mechanism by which the dual track produces the M&A-side pricing lift is the documented IPO alternative. In the pre-marketing phase of the sell-side process, the seller's banker prepares a CIM and management presentation referencing — explicitly or implicitly — the specific IPO readiness achieved: 2-year audit complete (chapter 3), PCAOB auditor engaged with interim SAS 100 reviews complete, S-1 draft in stable form (chapter 2), EGC-election memo drafted (chapter 5), SOX programme mature (chapter 4), independent-director recruitment complete for the Rule 10A-3 audit committee (chapter 7), and — in some processes — the org meeting held and the underwriter slate publicly known.

The signal is that the seller has a real alternative and can walk from a low-ball bid. A strategic reading a CIM that references specific IPO readiness understands the board has authorised a specific alternative process producing a specific alternative valuation. The bidder must therefore bid above the IPO range plus a strategic premium — commonly quoted in the 15–30% range depending on sector, competition, and synergy value. The signal must be *real* to be credible — a CIM that references "the company is prepared to pursue an IPO" without underlying artefacts will not survive strategic-buyer diligence, and the lift will be lost. The dual-track discipline therefore requires the IPO workstream to be genuinely advanced rather than nominally advanced.

## Confidentiality management across the two workstreams

The confidential DRS is confidential to the SEC and to the deal team; the CIM and management presentation are confidential to a specific bidder list. Running both simultaneously creates a challenge — the same underlying financial statements, business narrative, and management team are shopped in two different informational packages to two audiences with different confidentiality expectations. A leak from either compromises both.

The practitioner discipline is a **deal-team clean-list** — a small, named group of executives, board members, and outside advisors read into both workstreams. Every other person is read into at most one (typically the IPO workstream, which has broader internal exposure through the audit, SOX, and finance organisation) or into neither. The clean-list is documented in a memorandum maintained by GC, with additions requiring specific board or transaction-committee approval and NDA execution. The discipline extends to outside advisors — IPO underwriter and M&A advisor deal teams should not overlap in personnel, and where they must (single-firm-both-roles), a documented information wall is required.

The operational challenge is that the finance function must produce financial packages for both workstreams — audited financials, interim SAS 100 reviews, segment-level detail, customer-concentration analysis, deferred-revenue rollforward — without alerting the broader finance organisation that a parallel M&A process is running. Typically the CFO and a small number of senior finance leaders own M&A data-room population, staffed from the IPO workstream's materials with redaction and reformatting for the M&A audience. The M&A data-room is hosted on a separate virtual-data-room platform (Intralinks, Datasite, Firmex) with access controls; the IPO workstream uses its own document-management systems.

## The disclosure obligation in the S-1

Item 105 of Reg S-K requires the S-1 to include a risk-factor section discussing material risks. Item 303 requires the MD&A to discuss known trends, events, and uncertainties reasonably likely to have a material impact. The dual-track question is when material M&A discussions cross into the disclosure obligation.

The materiality standard is the **Basic v. Levinson** (485 U.S. 224, 1988) probability-magnitude test — a fact is material if there is a substantial likelihood a reasonable investor would consider it important, weighing the probability the event will occur against the anticipated magnitude. Preliminary conversations without specific price, terms, or probability of consummation are typically not material; a specific bid at a specific price under active negotiation typically is. The transition is a facts-and-circumstances judgment that GC and outside counsel monitor.

The practitioner discipline is to include a risk factor covering the possibility of an acquisition proposal rather than remain silent. A typical formulation: "We may from time to time be the target of unsolicited acquisition proposals from strategic or financial parties. If we receive and pursue such a proposal, the timing or completion of this offering may be delayed, modified, or terminated, and the terms on which we transact with such a party may materially differ from the terms of this offering. Any such transaction would be subject to review and approval by our board of directors and, if required, by our stockholders." The wording is drafted by securities counsel with M&A counsel input and calibrated to the specific dual-track posture without disclosing bidder identities or pricing terms that would compromise the M&A process. Where a transaction becomes probable during the SEC-review cycle, the S-1 is amended. The MD&A obligation under Item 303 typically references the risk-factor discussion by cross-reference. Full Item 105 / Item 303 discipline is in chapter 2. The dual-track point is that silence is not a defensible posture; the risk factor exists as inoculation against subsequent Section 11 liability.

## Tax and structuring interactions

An IPO is a capital-raise-and-liquidity event — the company issues new primary shares (optionally with existing secondary), proceeds go to the company and to selling holders, and the company continues as an independent listed entity. Generally not a taxable event to the company; a taxable event to secondary sellers subject to holding-period and QSBS analysis.

A sale to a strategic for cash is a taxable exit — stockholders recognise capital gain on the exchange. A sale for mixed cash-and-stock is a structuring exercise — a reorganisation qualifying under **IRC §368** (typically §368(a)(1)(A) reverse triangular merger or §368(a)(2)(E) structures) can be tax-deferred to the extent of stock consideration, with cash taxable as boot. A sale to a financial sponsor is typically all-cash and fully taxable. Full tax-structuring analysis is mod-103; the dual-track relevance is that the IPO does not offer the tax-deferral option a stock-for-stock strategic sale offers, which is a reason certain founder and long-term-holder economics may prefer the strategic path.

**IRC §1202** qualified small business stock is a founder-economics consideration. Founders holding qualifying QSBS may exclude specified per-taxpayer amounts of capital gain from federal income tax on QSBS held more than five years, subject to per-issuer caps and qualifying-company requirements. An IPO of QSBS-eligible stock typically preserves §1202 treatment on subsequent post-IPO sales; a sale of the whole company can also preserve treatment for founders whose holding period is complete. Full §1202 mechanics are mod-114.

## Employee-communications discipline

The IPO track requires an exciting, publicly-facing employee-communications posture — the go-public narrative is a specific employee-recruiting and retention asset, the IPO event is celebrated internally, and milestone communications (org meeting, DRS submission, public flip, roadshow launch, pricing, first-day trade) are calibrated to build organisational momentum. The M&A track requires a quiet, need-to-know posture — the process is confidential, bidder identities are highly sensitive, and premature employee awareness can produce retention risk (executives leaving for less-uncertain opportunities, sales pipeline stalling on customer uncertainty, product-organisation attention diverting to integration anxiety).

The dual-track discipline is that the two postures must be reconciled — the organisation cannot simultaneously be told "we are going public, let's celebrate" and "we are also selling the company, but you cannot know about that." The typical resolution is that IPO-track communications proceed publicly to the broad organisation while the M&A track is held tightly on the clean-list, with employee communications shifting only when a specific path decision is made. Where the M&A path is chosen, the transition communication (merger announcement, change-of-control, retention-programme rollout) runs under the mod-110 transaction-communications discipline. Where the IPO path is chosen, the M&A track is quietly wound down without broader organisational awareness that it existed. Where a leak from the M&A track reaches the organisation before the board's path decision, GC and comms execute a pre-drafted holding statement that neither confirms nor denies.

## The flip from IPO to M&A (or vice versa)

When the dual-track resolves into a single path, the abandoned path is unwound. An S-1 publicly filed can be withdrawn under **SEC Rule 477** — the issuer files a withdrawal application on EDGAR, the SEC staff typically consents within days, and the offering is terminated. The application must state the reasons; the typical formulation is "the Registrant has determined not to pursue the offering at this time in light of market conditions and its evaluation of strategic alternatives." A confidential DRS not publicly filed can be withdrawn or paused without a public filing. Disclosure on withdrawal is limited — the application itself is public, but no further disclosure is required unless the issuer has other public reporting obligations or the withdrawal accompanies a transaction announcement.

The aftermarket-reputation consequence depends on the circumstances — a withdrawal accompanied by a promptly-closed strategic sale is a positive-outcome narrative; a withdrawal in a closed IPO window followed by an extended standalone period is negative and persists into subsequent windows. The practical fact is that most dual-track flips resolve by choosing one path before the other is fully unwound — the M&A track is quietly wound down before the IPO prices, or the IPO track is paused before the public flip and the M&A track proceeds to signing. Definitive-agreement execution, DGCL §251 proxy filing, HSR notification, and closing mechanics are in mod-101 through mod-105.

## When NOT to run a dual track

The dual track is not always right. Cases where a single track is preferable:

**Clear IPO-window advantage** — a sector where the window is decisively open with compressed timing pressure (a hot AI-infrastructure moment, a comp-set print opening a short window). The window may close before the M&A process could produce a competing bid; parallel-workstream cost is wasted.

**Existing strategic-buyer exclusivity** — a signed LOI with a no-shop, active confirmatory diligence, definitive-agreement negotiation underway. The dual track is contractually inconsistent with exclusivity.

**Board-fiduciary posture requiring a single path** — a controller demand, a fiduciary conclusion that one path is clearly superior. The record supporting the single-track decision is documented.

**Cost-benefit failure** — the workstream expense (two banker slates, two counsel slates, coordinated auditor, two management-presentation packages, extended deal-team commitment) can exceed the benefit for a smaller company where the expected pricing lift is modest.

**Deal-team fatigue** — a management team that has recently completed a major transaction may not have residual capacity for two parallel workstreams. Realistic executive-bandwidth assessment is an input into the authorisation decision.

## Board choreography for the dual-track decision

The choreography that authorises the dual track is a specific sequence.

**The authorising meeting.** A board meeting authorises the dual-track structure. The record includes a CEO / CFO presentation covering the readiness memo (chapter 1), the IPO valuation range under discussion, inbound or anticipated strategic interest, the banker and counsel slate proposed for each track, the committee structure, and expected timeline and cost. The meeting produces a resolution authorising the structure, committee construction, and advisor engagements.

**The committee structure.** The typical dual-track structure is a **transaction committee** (or expanded IPO readiness committee) with a charter covering both workstreams. Where a conflict trigger requires it, a fully-empowered special committee is constructed with specific independent-director membership, independent-advisor authority, and negotiating authority. Some structures use two separate committees — an IPO readiness committee overseeing the IPO track and an M&A transaction committee overseeing the M&A track — with coordination protocols. The structure is determined by the specific conflict analysis and board composition.

**The advisor slate.** Engagements are documented — separate banker for each track (subject to bright-line separation), separate counsel for each track (typically the same securities counsel drives the IPO S-1 workstream and a separate M&A counsel firm drives the M&A track, with coordination on joint diligence and disclosure), coordinated auditor (the PCAOB-registered auditor supports both tracks with the audit and interim-review products required), and specialist advisors as needed (tax, antitrust, executive-compensation).

**The cadence.** The transaction committee typically meets weekly or bi-weekly through the active phase. The full board typically meets monthly with dual-track updates and holds decision meetings at each go / no-go milestone. Minutes are drafted carefully — every decision documented, every advisor input referenced, every alternative considered noted. The minute-taking discipline is the record on which any subsequent fiduciary-duty challenge will turn.

## Summary

The dual-track M&A + IPO governance posture runs the S-1 workstream and a sell-side M&A process in parallel with the specific intent of producing competitive tension between the two paths and the best per-share outcome across them. Governance defaults to full-board oversight with a transaction or IPO readiness committee but shifts to a fully-empowered special committee when a conflict trigger appears — controller involvement, insider participation, or an insider bidder. **Revlon** duties can attach mid-process once a sale of control becomes reasonably probable, requiring a defensible process; **Corwin** provides a stockholder-ratification cleansing path if disclosure is fully informed; **Paramount v. QVC** and **C&J Energy Services** define the change-of-control triggering perimeter and the acceptable single-bidder-with-market-check structure. Banker conflicts across the underwriter and sell-side advisor roles are managed by bright-line separation into different firms or, where a single firm is unavoidable, documented information walls plus independent-advisor validation — the discipline emerges from **In re Rural Metro**, **In re Del Monte**, and **In re El Paso**. The go / no-go decision sequences across the org meeting, DRS submission, SEC first-comment response, public S-1 flip, and pricing meeting, with the primary path typically chosen by the public flip to preserve pricing-anchor optionality. IPO readiness serves as documented leverage in the sell-side negotiation when the readiness artefacts are real. Confidentiality is preserved through a documented clean-list, separate data rooms, and staffing discipline. The S-1 discloses the M&A alternative through an Item 105 risk factor calibrated against the **Basic v. Levinson** materiality threshold rather than remaining silent. Employee communications reconcile the exciting-public IPO posture with the quiet-need-to-know M&A posture through the clean-list. The flip is executed under **SEC Rule 477** for a public S-1 withdrawal, with the practical fact that most flips resolve by choosing one path before the other is fully unwound. The dual track is not always right — a clear IPO window, an existing exclusivity commitment, a fiduciary single-path conclusion, a cost-benefit failure, or executive-bandwidth exhaustion may argue for a single track. The choreography produces a specific record — an authorising resolution, documented committee structure, documented advisor slate, and specific meeting cadence with careful minute-taking against the anticipated litigation record.
