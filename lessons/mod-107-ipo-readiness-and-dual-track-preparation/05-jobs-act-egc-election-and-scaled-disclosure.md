# JOBS Act EGC Election and Scaled Disclosure

## Why this matters

The Jumpstart Our Business Startups Act of 2012 — the **JOBS Act** — was Congress's response to a fifteen-year decline in the US IPO population and to the widely held practitioner view that the cumulative post-Sarbanes-Oxley disclosure and internal-control burden had priced smaller and earlier-stage issuers out of the public markets. Title I of the Act created a new statutory category, the **emerging growth company** (EGC), and gave issuers that qualify a specific menu of accommodations: a shorter audited-financial history in the S-1, deferral of the SOX 404(b) auditor attestation, reduced executive-compensation disclosure, deferral of new-standard adoption under Section 107(b), a testing-the-waters privilege with QIBs and institutional accredited investors, deferral of say-on-pay, and — added by the FAST Act of 2015 — the ability to omit interim financial statements the issuer reasonably believes will not be required at effectiveness. The 2015 amendments and the SEC's 2017 policy expansion carried some of the same relief (notably confidential DRS filing) beyond EGCs to all issuers, but the core scaled-disclosure regime is unique to EGC status. The dollar-and-workload savings are meaningful: SOX 404(b) auditor attestation alone typically runs $500k–$2M per year for a mid-sized issuer; one fewer year of PCAOB-audited financials in the S-1 saves a year of audit cost, audit-committee-oversight cost, and reaudit-preparation effort (chapter 3); reduced Item 402 disclosure trims executive-comp-consultant fees and reduces the surface area on which the plaintiff-firm ecosystem builds Section 11 theories.

The trade-off is real. A sophisticated buy-side analyst reviewing an EGC S-1 knows exactly which disclosures are missing relative to the non-EGC comparable set, and either estimates the missing content from public sources or discounts the security to reflect the reduced transparency. A CFO who elects every available scaled-disclosure accommodation signals "we are taking every relief the statute offers," which lands differently on a buy-side desk than a CFO who elects selectively to preserve comparability signalling. This chapter installs the specific "elect all vs. elect selectively" debate the audit committee runs at the S-1 drafting stage, the five-year on-ramp clock that shapes EGC-exit planning, the irrevocable Section 107(b) deferral election made once in the first S-1, and the specific termination triggers every CFO tracking a public-company trajectory must model against.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / accounting / tax advice. Every specific EGC election is authored and cleared by qualified securities counsel and coordinated with the PCAOB-registered auditor; the accounting-standard deferral election under Section 107(b) is discussed with the auditor and the audit committee before the S-1 is filed, and is *irrevocable* once made.

The core seat this chapter addresses is the CFO / GC / audit-committee chair running the specific EGC-election workstream during the S-1 drafting phase (which chapter 2 covers) and the specific EGC-exit workstream that begins the day the S-1 is declared effective. The chapter assumes the reader has internalised the S-1 disclosure architecture from chapter 2, the PCAOB reaudit programme from chapter 3, and the SOX 404 mechanics from chapter 4; it focuses on the specific EGC-status overlay that reshapes each of those workstreams.

## The EGC definition — Section 2(a)(19) of the Securities Act

The JOBS Act added **Section 2(a)(19)** to the Securities Act of 1933, defining an emerging growth company as "an issuer that had total annual gross revenues of less than $1,000,000,000 (as such amount is indexed for inflation every 5 years by the Commission to reflect the change in the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers) during its most recently completed fiscal year." The SEC updates the threshold on the five-year cadence — the most recent adjustment raised the threshold to **$1.235B** in the 2022 rulemaking. <!-- needs-research: confirm the current 2025/2026 inflation-adjusted EGC revenue threshold and the specific SEC release citation for the most recent adjustment --> The test looks at *total annual gross revenues* — top-line reported revenue under US GAAP (or IFRS-as-issued-by-the-IASB for an FPI) — measured against the most recently completed fiscal year.

The statute couples the revenue test with a *timing* trigger: the company must not have had its "first sale of common equity securities pursuant to an effective registration statement" on or before December 8, 2011. Any company whose IPO effective date is after December 8, 2011 and whose most-recent-fiscal-year revenue is below the inflation-adjusted threshold qualifies as an EGC. The revenue test is applied at the initial IPO S-1 filing and re-tested at each subsequent fiscal-year-end as one of the exit triggers described below.

**Foreign private issuers.** FPIs are eligible for EGC status on the same terms — Section 2(a)(19) does not distinguish between domestic and foreign issuers. An FPI files on **Form F-1** for the initial registration and reports on **Form 20-F** annually rather than the domestic S-1 / 10-K pair; the EGC accommodations flow through to the FPI regime, subject to FPI-specific adjustments (an FPI's Form 20-F already has scaled executive-compensation disclosure relative to a domestic 10-K, so the specific EGC benefit is different in scope). FPI-specific mechanics are outside the core scope of this chapter but are noted here because a US-headquartered issuer contemplating a Cayman-topco / FPI restructure as part of pre-IPO planning should model the EGC-status implications specifically.

## The 5-year on-ramp and the early-termination triggers

An issuer that qualifies as an EGC at the time of its IPO retains EGC status until the earliest of four specific triggers set out in **Section 2(a)(19)(A) through (D)** of the Securities Act. The trigger set is the specific planning artefact every CFO tracks against, because EGC-status termination is the moment at which every previously-deferred obligation — SOX 404(b) attestation, full Item 402 disclosure, say-on-pay, new-standard adoption on the public-company timeline — turns on simultaneously.

**Trigger (A) — the revenue trigger.** EGC status terminates on the last day of the fiscal year in which the issuer's total annual gross revenues equal or exceed the inflation-adjusted threshold (currently **$1.235B**). A company that IPOs at $600M in revenue and grows through the threshold in year three exits EGC status at that fiscal year-end, three years into the on-ramp.

**Trigger (B) — the five-year clock.** EGC status terminates on the last day of the fiscal year following the fifth anniversary of the first sale of common equity securities pursuant to an effective registration statement. A company that IPOs on March 15, 2026 with a December fiscal year-end exits EGC status at the end of the fiscal year following March 15, 2031 — i.e., December 31, 2031. The five-year clock is the default outer bound of the on-ramp; every EGC that does not hit an earlier trigger exits at this point.

**Trigger (C) — the $1B non-convertible debt trigger.** EGC status terminates on the date on which the issuer has, during the previous three-year period, issued more than $1B in non-convertible debt securities. The trigger picks up companies that access the investment-grade or high-yield debt markets at scale — a specific pattern for a post-IPO company using bond issuance to fund M&A or capital-return programmes. Convertible debt does not count against the $1B threshold; the trigger is drafted specifically around straight non-convertible debt. <!-- needs-research: confirm any SEC interpretive guidance on the $1B non-convertible debt calculation including whether refinancings and 144A private placements count -->

**Trigger (D) — the large accelerated filer trigger.** EGC status terminates on the date the issuer becomes a **large accelerated filer** under Rule 12b-2 of the Exchange Act. A large accelerated filer is generally an issuer with an aggregate worldwide market value of common equity held by non-affiliates (public float) of **$700M or more** measured as of the last business day of the most recently completed second fiscal quarter, plus specific prerequisites (subject-to-reporting for at least 12 months, at least one annual report filed). A high-growth issuer that IPOs with a $2B market cap will typically cross the $700M public-float threshold at its first second-quarter measurement date and exit EGC status at that point regardless of the other triggers. This is the trigger that terminates EGC status for the most commercially successful IPOs — the very issuers whose IPOs the market rewards fastest lose EGC status earliest.

The four-trigger set means the practical duration of EGC status varies dramatically across the IPO cohort. A slow-growth, small-cap issuer may enjoy the full five-year on-ramp; a hot growth-name IPO may exit within 12–18 months on the large-accelerated-filer trigger. The CFO's discipline is to model termination against each trigger on a rolling basis and align the 404(b) programme, the executive-comp-disclosure programme, and the accounting-standard adoption schedule with the earliest projected termination date rather than the default five-year outer bound.

## Confidential DRS submission — Section 6(e) and the 15-day rule

The JOBS Act added **Section 6(e)** to the Securities Act, permitting an EGC to submit an initial S-1 (or Form F-1 for an FPI) on a *confidential* basis for staff review before public filing. The EDGAR submission type is **DRS** ("draft registration statement") for the initial submission and **DRS/A** for each amendment (chapter 8 covers the mechanics). The confidential-DRS regime lets the working group complete drafting, the auditor complete the audit, and the SEC staff run its first-round comment letter without public visibility — a competitive-intelligence and market-conditioning protection.

The **2017 SEC policy expansion** by the Division of Corporation Finance extended confidential submission to all issuers, not just EGCs, for initial registration statements and for follow-ons filed within 12 months of an IPO. Both EGCs and non-EGCs now file their initial S-1s confidentially; the DRS submission is the standard first step across the current IPO cohort. The EGC-specific benefit is narrower than in 2012, but the confidential-DRS pattern remains the default.

**The 15-day public-filing rule.** Section 6(e) requires that the issuer publicly file the S-1 (and all previously submitted DRS amendments — the entire submission history is exposed at the flip) **at least 15 days before the road show**. The SEC clarification places the road show as the first road show meeting with prospective investors. In practical scheduling, the public filing typically happens 3–4 weeks before pricing, placing the road-show start roughly two weeks before pricing.

**Staff-comment cadence.** SEC staff's typical first-round comment turnaround on an initial DRS submission is approximately **30 days**; subsequent amendment rounds run faster (14–21 days is a typical second-round cadence). A first-time IPO issuer running through 3–5 amendment cycles will therefore run 4–8 months of staff review from initial DRS submission through public filing, with the public flip triggering the 15-day roadshow clock.

## Testing the waters — Section 5(d) at high level

The JOBS Act's **Section 5(d)** privilege permits an EGC to communicate with qualified institutional buyers (QIBs, under Rule 144A) and institutional accredited investors (IAIs, under Rule 501(a)) before or after filing to gauge interest in the offering. The SEC's **2019 Rule 163B** extended the privilege to all issuers, not just EGCs; the specific mechanics — audience limits, content limits, S-1 consistency requirements, paper-trail discipline, cooling-off patterns — are the subject of mod-108 chapter 2, which covers the gun-jumping perimeter in depth. This chapter references TTW only to note that the privilege is a specific EGC accommodation whose scope was expanded post-2012 to become an industry-wide practice, and to flag that the specific TTW discipline is authored and cleared by underwriter counsel with a documented paper trail.

## The scaled-disclosure menu — the specific elections

The EGC's scaled-disclosure menu is a specific set of accommodations, each elected or declined at the S-1 drafting stage and reflected in the disclosure the issuer publishes both at the S-1 and in the ongoing 10-K / 10-Q / proxy statement filings during the EGC on-ramp.

**Two years of audited financial statements instead of three.** Under **Section 7(a)(2)(A)** of the Securities Act, an EGC's S-1 may include two years of audited financial statements rather than the three years otherwise required by Regulation S-X. The election reduces the reaudit workload (chapter 3) and shortens the S-1 disclosure footprint. It carries into the subsequent 10-Ks filed during EGC status: the audited-financial-statement footprint is two years for EGC filers rather than three. The MD&A comparability discussion tightens around the two-year set; the selected-financial-data disclosure under Item 301 was substantially eliminated in the SEC's 2020 disclosure-modernisation rulemaking, so that specific interaction is less material than it was.

**Reduced Item 402 executive-compensation disclosure.** The EGC regime under Item 402 requires compensation disclosure for only the CEO and the next two most highly compensated executive officers, rather than the top five ("named executive officers" or NEOs) required of non-EGCs. The EGC is exempt from the **Compensation Discussion and Analysis (CD&A)** required under Item 402(b), the **pay-versus-performance** disclosure introduced under Item 402(v) in the 2022 SEC rulemaking implementing Dodd-Frank Section 953(a), and the **pay-ratio** disclosure required under Item 402(u) implementing Dodd-Frank Section 953(b). <!-- needs-research: confirm the current pay-versus-performance disclosure applicability to EGCs including any SEC amendment scope --> The EGC compensation section is materially shorter and less analytically detailed than the non-EGC comparable. This is the single most-noticed scaled-disclosure gap by buy-side analysts and by governance investors (ISS, Glass Lewis, and the largest passive holders' stewardship teams), and is the gap most commonly voluntarily filled by EGCs preserving comparability signalling.

**No SOX 404(b) auditor attestation.** Section 103 of the JOBS Act amended **Section 404(b) of Sarbanes-Oxley** to exempt EGCs from the requirement to obtain an independent auditor's attestation on ICFR effectiveness. Chapter 4 covers the SOX 404 mechanics; the EGC-relevant point is that only 404(b) is deferred — the **Section 404(a) management assessment** still applies starting with the second annual report (the first post-IPO 10-K carries no 404(a) assessment). The 404(b) attestation turns on the day EGC status terminates. The practitioner discipline is to build the 404(b) programme in years 3–4 of the on-ramp so it is operational at termination; a company hitting the large-accelerated-filer trigger at its first Q2 measurement with no programme built is running an ICFR audit in flight. Dollar savings from 404(b) deferral is the single largest EGC cost saving — typically **$500k–$2M annually** depending on issuer complexity and auditor scoping.

**Deferred adoption of new accounting standards — Section 107(b).** **Section 107(b)** of the JOBS Act permits an EGC to elect to defer adoption of any new or revised financial-accounting standard to the compliance date otherwise applicable to non-public entities. The specific mechanics: FASB standards typically have two adoption dates — an earlier "public business entity" (PBE) date and a later "non-PBE" or private-company date, typically one year later. An EGC that elects Section 107(b) deferral applies each new standard on the non-PBE date, not the PBE date. The election is made in the first S-1 and, **if the issuer elects deferral, the election is irrevocable** — the issuer may not later opt out of deferral. Conversely, if the issuer opts *out* of deferral in the first S-1 (electing to adopt on the PBE timeline), that opt-out is also irrevocable — the issuer may not later opt in.

The historical examples clarify what the election means in practice. **ASC 842 (Leases)** had a PBE effective date of fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2018 and a non-PBE effective date of fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2021 (with the 2020 COVID-related deferral); an EGC electing Section 107(b) deferral applied ASC 842 three years later than a non-EGC public company, with material effects on the balance sheet's right-of-use asset and lease liability line items. **ASC 326 (Credit Losses, "CECL")** had a PBE effective date of fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2019 (large accelerated filers) and a non-PBE effective date of fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022; an EGC electing deferral applied CECL on the later date. The practitioner conversation with the auditor at first-S-1 drafting is: *which standards are in the near-term FASB pipeline, what is the specific deferral benefit, and — given the irrevocable nature of the election — should the issuer elect in or out?* The common default is to elect in, because deferral offers optionality: the issuer can *voluntarily* adopt any specific standard on the PBE date without losing the deferral for other standards, but cannot later opt out of the deferral entirely. Some issuers emphasising comparability with a non-EGC peer set elect out. The election is disclosed in the S-1 (typically in the significant accounting policies footnote and the summary of the offering).

**Reduced analyst quiet periods.** The JOBS Act reduced the post-pricing analyst quiet period for underwriter research on EGC IPOs. The specific current quiet-period day count is governed by **FINRA Rule 2242** (the successor to NASD Rule 2711 and NYSE Rule 472) as amended to reflect the JOBS Act relief. <!-- needs-research: confirm the current post-pricing analyst quiet period days for EGC IPOs under FINRA Rule 2242 including any post-2015 amendments --> The specific practitioner effect is that underwriter research from the deal's participating banks can initiate coverage earlier in the aftermarket than in the pre-JOBS-Act regime, which supports a specific aftermarket-launch signalling pattern (mod-108 chapter 5 covers the aftermarket-launch choreography).

**Deferred say-on-pay and say-on-frequency.** Under the JOBS Act's amendments to the Dodd-Frank say-on-pay provisions, an EGC is exempt from the say-on-pay advisory vote required under Exchange Act Section 14A(a)(1) and from the say-on-frequency advisory vote required under Section 14A(a)(2) for the duration of EGC status. The exemption picks up at EGC-status termination; the specific issuer must hold a say-on-pay vote at its first post-EGC annual meeting and must hold a say-on-frequency vote at that same meeting.

## The comparability trade-off — "elect all" vs. "elect selectively"

The practitioner debate at the S-1 drafting stage is whether to elect every scaled-disclosure accommodation or to elect selectively to preserve comparability signalling. The "elect all" default is administratively simplest and captures maximum cost saving; the "elect selectively" pattern is more common among issuers whose buy-side book is comparability-sensitive — enterprise-software issuers whose institutional-holder base cross-holds non-EGC public comparables, consumer issuers whose ISS / Glass Lewis governance signalling matters at the annual-meeting cadence, financial issuers whose disclosure-transparency signal is priced by the market.

The accommodations most commonly *voluntarily declined* by comparability-sensitive issuers are: (1) full **Item 402 disclosure** with top-five NEOs, CD&A, and pay-ratio — complied with to align with ISS and Glass Lewis stewardship frames; (2) **three years of audited financials** — complied with when the auditor has already completed the third-year audit (chapter 3); (3) **SOX 404(b) attestation** on a voluntary basis — rare, because the cost is high and the signalling benefit is limited. Almost never voluntarily declined: the **Section 107(b) deferral** (irrevocable, so a voluntary "elect out" is a permanent commitment) and the **reduced analyst quiet period** (a structural accommodation built into the aftermarket launch, not something the issuer can meaningfully "voluntarily comply" against).

The audit-committee conversation at S-1 drafting is a specific artefact: a written CFO memo to the audit committee laying out each election, the cost saving, the comparability signal, and a recommendation. The audit committee reviews the memo, the CFO discusses with the auditor and underwriter counsel, and elections are documented in audit-committee minutes and reflected in the S-1. The documentation discipline matters because the elections drive several years of subsequent disclosure and — for Section 107(b) — an irrevocable one-time decision.

## The FAST Act 2015 expansion — omitting interim financials

The **Fixing America's Surface Transportation Act (FAST Act) of 2015** added several EGC accommodations on top of the JOBS Act baseline. The most operationally significant is the ability of an EGC to omit from the initial DRS submission (and subsequent amendments and the initial public filing) any interim financial statements the issuer reasonably believes will not be required at the time of effectiveness. This matters for calendar-year-end issuers filing near a quarter boundary — a December-fiscal-year issuer filing an initial DRS in October 2026 including third-quarter interim financials would, absent the relief, be forced to update the interims to include the fourth quarter as the audit completes. Under FAST Act relief, the issuer may omit the interims from the initial submission if it reasonably believes they will not be required at effectiveness. The 2015 SEC Compliance and Disclosure Interpretations clarify the "reasonably believes" standard. <!-- needs-research: confirm the current SEC C&DI on FAST Act interim financial omission including any post-2020 amendments -->

The FAST Act also confirmed and expanded the confidential-submission regime (before the SEC's 2017 non-EGC expansion), permitted grandfathered-EGC treatment for issuers that lost EGC status shortly before filing under specific transitional rules, and made technical adjustments to the JOBS Act provisions. The 2015 amendments together with the 2017 expansion produced the current practitioner pattern: confidential DRS submission and 15-day-before-roadshow public filing is the default for essentially every issuer, with the EGC-only accommodations (Section 107(b) deferral, Item 402 reduction, SOX 404(b) deferral, say-on-pay deferral, reduced quiet period) layered on top for Section 2(a)(19) qualifiers.

## The Regulation A+ / Regulation Crowdfunding boundary

The JOBS Act's Title IV created **Regulation A+** (updated Regulation A with expanded offering caps) and Title III created **Regulation Crowdfunding** (Reg CF). These are alternative capital-formation regimes designed for issuers not pursuing a traditional registered IPO — smaller offerings, community-of-interest raises, and specific issuer profiles for whom the full Section 5 registration regime is disproportionate. Reg A+ and Reg CF are the subject of mod-108 chapter 7 (IPO alternatives) and are outside the scope of this chapter, which focuses specifically on the EGC-in-a-registered-IPO regime. The practitioner note here is only that the JOBS Act is a multi-title statute with several distinct capital-formation regimes; a reader whose company is considering a non-traditional capital raise should read this chapter for the EGC-registered-IPO mechanics and mod-108 chapter 7 for the alternative regimes.

## The EGC-exit workstream — planning against the five-year clock

The specific project-planning discipline that begins the day the S-1 is declared effective is the **EGC-exit workstream** — the coordinated build of every previously-deferred obligation so that the obligation is operational at the moment EGC status terminates. The workstream has a specific structure.

**Year 1.** The company operates under the scaled-disclosure regime, files its first 10-K with two-year audited financials and reduced Item 402 disclosure, files its first proxy without a say-on-pay vote, and completes the first-year integration of the public-company reporting cadence. The SOX 404(a) management assessment does not apply to the first 10-K.

**Year 2.** The company files the second 10-K with the first Section 404(a) management assessment (the 404(b) attestation remains deferred). The CFO begins the 404(b) programme design — scoping the ICFR programme against the auditor's expected 404(b) audit scope, building control documentation and testing infrastructure, and rehearsing the 404(a) assessment against a "shadow 404(b)" auditor read.

**Year 3.** The 404(b) programme runs at full operational scale (control documentation, quarterly walkthroughs, deficiency-remediation cycles, auditor-alignment sessions), aiming to be 404(b)-ready by year-end. The CFO models termination triggers against the current forecast: large-accelerated-filer $700M float at year-3 Q2, revenue trigger at year-end, or the five-year outer bound.

**Year 4.** The 404(b) programme runs in shadow mode with the auditor performing a dry-run 404(b) audit; deficiencies are remediated. The CFO drafts the full Item 402 disclosure package (CD&A, pay-versus-performance, pay-ratio) in preparation for the first post-EGC proxy. The audit committee reviews the say-on-pay programme design.

**Year 5 — EGC-status termination.** At the end of the fiscal year following the fifth anniversary of the IPO (assuming no earlier trigger fires), EGC status terminates. The next 10-K includes the first full-scope 404(b) attestation, the next proxy includes the first say-on-pay vote, and — for issuers that elected Section 107(b) deferral — any deferred-standard adoption not previously voluntarily accelerated becomes due on the PBE timeline for new standards issued from that point.

The planning discipline is to align the 404(b) build with the *earliest* projected termination trigger, not the five-year outer bound. A company projecting a large-accelerated-filer trigger at year-3 Q2 needs a 404(b) programme operational at year-3 Q2, not year 5. A CFO who plans against the outer bound and hits the accelerated trigger is running an ICFR audit in flight — the specific worst-case scenario the discipline is designed to prevent.

## Summary

The JOBS Act's Section 2(a)(19) emerging-growth-company regime is a statutory accommodation for issuers with pre-IPO annual revenues below the inflation-adjusted $1.235B threshold, offering a five-year on-ramp of scaled disclosure subject to earlier termination on the revenue trigger, the five-year clock, the $1B non-convertible-debt trigger, or the $700M-public-float large-accelerated-filer trigger. The menu covers two years of audited financials under Section 7(a)(2)(A), reduced Item 402 disclosure (no CD&A, no pay-versus-performance, no pay-ratio, top-three NEOs), deferral of the SOX 404(b) auditor attestation (with continuing 404(a) management-assessment applicability from the second 10-K), the irrevocable Section 107(b) election on new-standard adoption timing (with ASC 842 and ASC 326 as the practitioner-familiar examples), reduced FINRA Rule 2242 analyst quiet periods, and deferral of say-on-pay and say-on-frequency. The Section 6(e) confidential DRS submission — expanded to all issuers in the SEC's 2017 policy update — is the standard first-round filing mechanism, with the 15-day-before-roadshow public-filing rule anchoring pre-marketing timing. Section 5(d) testing-the-waters is covered in mod-108 chapter 2. The practitioner debate at S-1 drafting is "elect all vs. elect selectively" — cost savings (SOX 404(b) at $500k–$2M annually, one fewer year of PCAOB audit, reduced compensation-disclosure surface) against comparability signalling to a buy-side book cross-holding non-EGC comparables. The EGC-exit workstream begins the day the S-1 is effective and aligns the 404(b) build, the full Item 402 package, the say-on-pay programme, and any deferred-standard adoption schedule against the *earliest* projected termination trigger rather than the five-year outer bound.

Chapter 6 turns to the dual-track structure — the coordinated preparation of the S-1 filing (leveraging the confidential-DRS regime this chapter covered) with a parallel sell-side M&A process, the specific competitive-tension mechanics that produce the best per-share outcome, and the fiduciary-duty and disclosure choreography that lets the board run both paths credibly to a decision point.
