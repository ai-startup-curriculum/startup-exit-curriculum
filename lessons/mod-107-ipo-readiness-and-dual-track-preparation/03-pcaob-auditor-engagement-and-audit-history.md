# PCAOB Auditor Engagement and the 2-Year Audit History

## Why this matters

The audited financial statements bound into the S-1 are not a compliance artefact — they are the single most heavily-litigated portion of the prospectus and the anchor of the underwriter's Section 11 due-diligence defence. **Section 11** of the Securities Act imposes strict liability on the issuer and near-strict liability on officers, directors, and underwriters for material misstatements or omissions as of the effective date. One portion of the S-1 escapes: information "expertised" by a named expert. Under **Section 11(a)(4)** and the associated **expertised-portion doctrine**, an underwriter with no reasonable ground to believe the auditor's report is materially misstated may invoke a due-diligence defence on the audited financials without conducting an independent audit-quality investigation. The PCAOB-registered auditor's report is the specific mechanism by which the underwriter's litigation exposure on multiple fiscal years of financials is contained. Absent a clean PCAOB audit, the underwriter is *not* protected on the expertised portion and will not price the deal.

The audit is also the buy-side's aftermarket-quality signal. A long-only portfolio manager who buys the deal is buying a stream of 10-Ks and 10-Qs whose reliability is enforced by the same auditor across the cycles that follow. A change of auditor mid-cycle, a Part I.A finding in the engagement partner's most-recent PCAOB inspection, or a restatement out of the reaudit workstream materially alters the aftermarket buyer's willingness to hold. Auditor choice is a multi-year commitment with consequences long after the IPO closes.

This chapter installs the practitioner discipline: firm-tier selection, PCAOB registration and Section 104 inspections, the SEC and PCAOB independence rules, the 2-year (EGC) versus 3-year (non-EGC) audit-history footprint, the reaudit workstream that converts AICPA audits to PCAOB audits, the audit-committee choreography, and the deliverables (audit reports with Critical Audit Matters, interim reviews, comfort letters, preferability letters) produced at each milestone.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal / accounting / tax advice. Every auditor engagement is negotiated by qualified counsel, cleared by the audit committee under a documented pre-approval regime, and executed under the standards in force on the specific engagement dates.

## Firm-tier selection — Big Four vs. next-tier

The universe of PCAOB-registered firms with meaningful IPO practices is dominated by the **Big Four** — PricewaterhouseCoopers, Deloitte, Ernst & Young, and KPMG — followed by a **next tier** of Grant Thornton, BDO USA, RSM US, Baker Tilly, and Crowe LLP, plus a small handful of specialist regional firms. The choice is a pricing-meeting and aftermarket-signalling decision, not solely a fee-and-quality decision.

A **Big Four** audit buys three specific goods. First, **buy-side comfort in the pricing meeting** — the long-only community reads the brand as a proxy for engagement-team depth, national-office consultation capacity, and coverage bench that survives partner turnover. Second, **comparable-issuer signalling** — the sector's public cohort is almost uniformly Big Four audited, so a next-tier auditor becomes a specific line item in sell-side initiation reports. Third, **technical capacity** on sector-complex accounting — national offices maintain deep benches on **ASC 606** (revenue), **ASC 842** (leases), **ASC 718** (stock-based compensation), **ASC 805** (business combinations), and **ASC 740** (income taxes and deferred-tax-asset valuation allowances) that mid-market firms match case-by-case but not consistently. The cost is fees in the low-single-digit millions annually for a recurring IPO-scale audit (with reaudit and IPO-year incremental adding materially), partner attention diluted across a dozen-plus public clients, and national-office consultation cycles measured in weeks.

A **next-tier** firm buys **lower fees** (often 40–60% of Big Four for comparable scope), **partner attention** (2–4 public clients per partner rather than 12+), and **sector fit** where a firm has built bench (BDO in life sciences, Grant Thornton in mid-market technology, RSM in mid-market financial services). The cost is the pricing-meeting and comparable-issuer signal above. The common pattern is to run a next-tier firm through the private-company growth years and transition to a Big Four firm 18–36 months before filing, timing the switch so any Item 304 auditor-change disclosure sits cleanly inside or outside the two-year window.

The board conversation is owned by the audit-committee chair with the CFO, documented in a board resolution and, post-IPO, put to shareholders under the annual "ratification of auditor" vote disclosed in the proxy under Item 9 of Schedule 14A.

## PCAOB registration — Sarbanes-Oxley Section 102

Under **Section 102(a)** of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, any accounting firm that "prepares or issues, or that participates in the preparation or issuance of, any audit report with respect to any issuer" must be **registered with the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board**. Registration is applied for on **PCAOB Form 1**, maintained through annual **Form 2** reporting, and updated on **Form 3** for specified events. Status is publicly searchable on the PCAOB website; the CFO's first competence check on any prospective auditor is confirming registration and the offices from which the engagement team will work.

Registration covers the whole firm, not individual partners. Foreign-headquartered auditors of US issuers must also be PCAOB-registered and are inspected country-by-country subject to cross-border access arrangements. The historical friction with China-based auditors of US-listed issuers is a specific example of the regime's teeth; resolution has run through the **Holding Foreign Companies Accountable Act (HFCAA)** and subsequent PCAOB access agreements. <!-- needs-research: current status of PCAOB access to China-based audit workpapers and any HFCAA enforcement developments through 2025 --> An AICPA-standards audit report from a non-registered firm is not acceptable for the S-1's audited financials.

## PCAOB inspections — Section 104 and Part I.A / I.B / II

**Section 104** of Sarbanes-Oxley authorises the PCAOB to inspect registered firms. Firms auditing **more than 100 issuers** are inspected **annually**; firms auditing **100 or fewer** are inspected **triennially**. The Big Four are annual inspectees; next-tier firms' cycles depend on issuer count each year. Each inspection samples prior-year engagements and reviews the firm's quality-control system.

The report has three portions. **Part I.A findings** are audit deficiencies material enough that the inspection team concluded the firm did not obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to support its opinion on one or more engagements — published on the PCAOB website and tracked by audit committees, buy-side investors, and comparable-issuer benchmarking. Firm-level aggregate Part I.A deficiency rates are a persistent focus of PCAOB commentary. <!-- needs-research: most-recent PCAOB annual inspection deficiency-rate statistics by firm --> **Part I.B findings** are compliance instances that did not necessarily undermine the opinion (documentation shortfalls, procedural non-compliance) — public, but with lower signalling weight. **Part II findings** address the firm's quality-control system and are **confidential**; they become public only if the firm fails to remediate to the PCAOB's satisfaction within **12 months**. Audit-committee diligence is a documented review of the most-recent inspection report, a discussion with the engagement partner on any Part I.A findings against the partner's prior engagements, and a discussion of the firm's Part II remediation programme — minuted.

## SEC auditor independence — Regulation S-X Rule 2-01

An auditor of an SEC issuer must be **independent** under **Rule 2-01 of Regulation S-X**, together with the more-restrictive-if-applicable AICPA and PCAOB independence rules. SEC independence is more restrictive than the AICPA rules governing private-company audits, which is why a private-company engagement often requires independence rescoping at the transition to public-company work.

**Non-audit-service prohibitions — Rule 2-01(c)(4).** The auditor may not provide bookkeeping or accounting-record services; financial information systems design and implementation; appraisal, valuation, or fairness-opinion services; actuarial services; internal-audit outsourcing; management functions or HR; broker-dealer, investment-adviser, or investment-banking services; legal services; or expert services unrelated to the audit. Tax compliance, planning, and advice are **permitted** subject to audit-committee pre-approval, with carve-outs for aggressive tax-shelter transactions, personal tax services to persons in FRO roles, and contingent-fee arrangements. Tax-services and IT-consulting scoping at engagement start are specific negotiations.

**Business relationships — Rule 2-01(c)(3).** Direct business relationships between auditor and client (other than as an ordinary-course consumer) impair independence — the auditor cannot be a material customer, joint-venture partner, or co-investor.

**Employment relationships — Rule 2-01(c)(2).** Partners, principals, and professional employees cannot serve as officers, directors, or in financial-reporting-oversight roles at the client during the audit or professional-engagement period. A **1-year cooling-off period** under **Rule 2-01(c)(2)(iii)** applies to former audit-team members joining the client in a **financial-reporting-oversight role (FROR)** — CFO, controller, chief accounting officer, or any role reviewing or supervising financial reporting. This constrains the common pattern of hiring a former engagement senior manager into the controller seat.

**Partner rotation — Rule 2-01(c)(6).** The **lead audit partner** rotates after **5 consecutive years** with a **5-year time-out**; the **concurring (engagement quality review) partner** rotates after **5 years** with a **3-year time-out**; other audit partners after **7 years** with a **2-year time-out**. Rotation is an engagement-planning constraint: an audit committee selecting an auditor three years before an anticipated IPO needs to know the lead partner will be the specific person through the IPO and the two following public audit cycles, and that the clock is already running.

**Pre-approval — Sarbanes-Oxley Section 202.** All audit and non-audit services must be **pre-approved by the audit committee**, either specifically or under general categories with fee ceilings, subject to a de minimis exception. The regime is documented in the audit-committee charter and each meeting's minutes. At engagement start and annually, the auditor delivers a written independence-confirmation letter under **PCAOB Rule 3526** disclosing any relationships that could reasonably be thought to bear on independence; the committee reviews it in private session.

## Auditor changes — Item 304 of Reg S-K and Form 8-K Item 4.01

Where the issuer has changed auditors in the two years preceding filing, **Item 304 of Regulation S-K** requires S-1 disclosure of: the former auditor's identity and the date of resignation, dismissal, or non-reappointment; whether reports on the past two years contained an adverse opinion, disclaimer, qualification, or modification; whether the audit committee or board recommended or approved the change; and — critically — whether any **disagreements** on accounting principles, disclosure, or auditing scope, or any **reportable event** under Item 304(a)(1)(v), occurred. Where a disagreement or reportable event occurred, details are disclosed and the former auditor is given the opportunity to file a letter under **Item 601(b)(16)** confirming or contesting the description. A clean change (no adverse opinions, no disagreements, no reportable events, audit-committee approval documented) is routine; a change with a disagreement draws specific staff attention. **Post-IPO auditor changes** are reported on **Form 8-K Item 4.01** within **4 business days**, with the same Item 304 content and Item 601(b)(16) letter. That cadence is why post-IPO changes are rare and handled with materially more choreography than private-company changes.

## The 2-year vs. 3-year audit-history footprint

The audited-financials footprint depends on issuer status. **Non-EGC issuers** must include, under **Regulation S-X Rules 3-01 and 3-02**, **three years** of audited statements of comprehensive income, cash flows, and stockholders' equity, and **two years** of audited balance sheets, plus interim unaudited statements for the current stub and comparable prior-year period, reviewed by the auditor under AS 4105. Selected financial data under **Item 301** historically required a five-year table; the requirement was substantially deprecated by SEC amendments in 2020. <!-- needs-research: current status of Item 301 selected financial data requirement post-2020 SEC amendments -->

**EGC issuers.** Under **JOBS Act Section 7(a)(2)(A)**, an emerging growth company may include only **two years** of audited financials rather than three. Chapter 5 covers EGC scaled disclosure in depth; the point here is that the auditor's reaudit workload, engagement fees, and calendar risk are materially smaller for an EGC. ## PCAOB Auditing Standards — the AS series

Audits of SEC issuers are executed under **PCAOB Auditing Standards**, codified in the AS series (from the pre-2015 AU numbering). The standards most relevant to an IPO engagement include **AS 1101** (Audit Risk — the foundational risk-based framework), **AS 1201** (Supervision), **AS 1301** (Communications with Audit Committees — below), **AS 2201** (An Audit of ICFR Integrated with an Audit of Financial Statements — applicable when the auditor issues an integrated opinion under Sarbanes-Oxley Section 404(b); chapter 4 covers the ICFR programme), **AS 2410** (Related Parties — below), **AS 3101** (The Auditor's Report When the Auditor Expresses an Unqualified Opinion — including the Critical Audit Matters requirement), **AS 4105** (Reviews of Interim Financial Information — historically **AU 722** / **SAS 100**), and **AS 6101** (Communications with Predecessor Auditors — on any auditor change). The series runs from **AS 1000** through **AS 6115** with periodic amendment.

## AS 3101 and Critical Audit Matters

**AS 3101** requires the auditor's report to include a **Critical Audit Matters (CAMs)** section describing matters communicated to the audit committee that relate to accounts or disclosures material to the financial statements and that involved **especially challenging, subjective, or complex auditor judgement**. Each CAM is described by the account or disclosure it relates to, the reasons the auditor determined it to be a CAM, and how it was addressed in the audit.

CAMs phased in through 2019–2020: **large accelerated filers** were subject for fiscal years ending on or after **June 30, 2019**; **all other filers** required to file auditor reports for fiscal years ending on or after **December 15, 2020**. **EGCs are exempt** for the duration of EGC status; the first CAMs-inclusive report typically appears in the 10-K for the first fiscal year in which EGC status is lost. CAMs give the buy-side and the plaintiff bar a specific window into where the auditor's judgement fragilities live — deferred-tax-asset valuation, revenue recognition on a particular contract type, goodwill-impairment analysis. Drafting is a specific negotiation between auditor and CFO / controller in the pre-filing weeks.

## AS 4105 — interim reviews

Under **AS 4105** (historically **AU 722** / **SAS 100**), the auditor conducts **interim reviews** of quarterly financial information, providing **negative assurance** — "nothing came to the auditor's attention causing the auditor to believe" the interim statements are not in conformity with GAAP — rather than the **positive assurance** of an audit opinion. Interim procedures consist principally of inquiries and analytical procedures. During the IPO, the auditor reviews the stub-period financials in the S-1 at initial filing and each amendment; post-IPO, each 10-Q. The review is not signed as an opinion but is confirmed in a written communication to the audit committee. Failure to complete an interim review before an S-1 amendment or a 10-Q filing is a filing-blocker.

## The comfort letter — AU 634 / SAS 72

The **comfort letter** is an auditor deliverable to the underwriters (not the issuer) at pricing, providing negative assurance on unaudited financial information in the S-1 to support the underwriters' Section 11 due-diligence defence on the non-expertised portions. It is governed by **AU 634** (historically **SAS 72**). A typical comfort letter confirms auditor independence; that the audited financials comply as to form with the Securities Act and Reg S-X; gives negative assurance on specified unaudited information (interim data, subsequent-events data, capitalisation tables, selected-financial-data reconciliations, ratios computed from audited numbers); and confirms that specific **circled figures** in the S-1 tie to the auditor's workpapers or accounting records.

The **circle-up** workflow is the practitioner mechanism: underwriter counsel marks every number in the prospectus draft it wants covered; the items are transmitted to the auditor, which agrees to cover, pushes back where a specific representation cannot be given, or proposes alternative coverage. Circle-up iterates over the two-to-four weeks before pricing and finalises in a pricing-date comfort letter and a **bring-down comfort letter** at closing (typically three business days later). The comfort letter runs to the underwriters' benefit, not the issuer's or the buy-side's.

## AS 1301 — audit-committee communications and choreography

**AS 1301** specifies the auditor's required communications with the audit committee: audit scope, significant accounting policies, significant unusual transactions, critical accounting estimates, disagreements with management, material weaknesses and significant deficiencies in ICFR, uncorrected misstatements, and other specified topics. Communications occur at engagement start, planning-phase before fieldwork, and completion before the report issues.

The audit committee meets with the auditor at each communication point, quarterly on interim reviews, and ad-hoc on arising issues. Each meeting includes a **private session** from which management is excluded, in which the auditor speaks directly about management cooperation, tone-at-the-top on accounting judgement, and previously unraised concerns. Consistent use of the private session is a specific audit-quality indicator. The audit committee also owns **engagement-letter** negotiation, **fee approval**, and — under **Sarbanes-Oxley Section 202** — the pre-approval regime described above. Where counsel supports it, the committee may request the substance of the auditor's confidential **PCAOB Part II** quality-control criticisms and the firm's remediation approach as part of selection or retention diligence.

## Reaudit and re-issuance

Most first-time IPO issuers face the circumstance that historical financials were audited under **AICPA Statements on Auditing Standards** by a firm engaged for private-company work but must now be presented in the S-1 under **PCAOB Auditing Standards**. Either the historical auditor **re-issues** under PCAOB standards, or the incoming PCAOB-registered auditor **reaudits** and issues a new opinion.

**Re-issuance** is available where the historical firm is PCAOB-registered on the reissuance date and its workpapers support PCAOB-standard depth. The firm re-reads its workpapers, performs the additional PCAOB-specific procedures the AICPA engagement did not require, updates its **subsequent-events review** through the S-1 filing date, and issues a re-issued opinion. It is materially faster and cheaper than reaudit but requires the historical firm's cooperation.

**Reaudit** is required where the historical firm is not PCAOB-registered, will not re-issue, or where the workpapers do not support reissuance. The incoming auditor performs full substantive testing, third-party confirmations, physical-inventory-observation-equivalent procedures for closed fiscal years, and full documentation. The typical reaudit runs **3–9 months** and adds **2–3x the annual audit fee** per reaudited fiscal year. This is a primary reason auditor selection must occur 12–24 months before filing; a reaudit under a compressed calendar becomes a filing-blocker. **AS 6101** governs the incoming auditor's required predecessor-firm communication before accepting the engagement.

## Preferability letters — Item 601(b)(18)

Where the issuer voluntarily changes an accounting principle (a revenue-recognition method not compelled by a new standard, FIFO to LIFO or vice versa, a depreciation method), the auditor must issue a **preferability letter** stating that the change is to a **preferable** principle. The letter is filed as an S-1 exhibit under **Item 601(b)(18) of Regulation S-K**. Preferability is an accounting-standards judgement, not management preference; the auditor must be able to defend it on the specific facts, often after national-office consultation.

## AS 2410 — related-party transactions

Related-party transactions receive specific auditor focus under **AS 2410**. The auditor identifies related parties through management inquiry, board and audit-committee minutes review, D&O questionnaires, and specific procedures; tests the specific transactions (terms, pricing, business purpose, disclosure); and evaluates disclosure. The **Item 404 of Regulation S-K** related-person-transactions disclosure in the S-1 (transactions above the $120,000 threshold plus specific director and officer relationships) draws directly from the auditor's AS 2410 workpapers. The audit committee approves related-party transactions under a board-adopted policy (a standard governance artefact adopted at or before IPO), and AS 2410 procedures verify approval-discipline compliance. A related-party transaction that surfaces without prior board approval is a specific finding triggering audit-committee, disclosure-committee, and — where material — restatement analysis.

## Pushdown accounting — ASC 805-50

Where the issuer was acquired by a parent (a PE-portfolio holding, or a corporate parent spinning out a subsidiary), the parent's acquisition-date basis may be **pushed down** to the issuer's financials under **ASC 805-50**. Pushdown is an election, generally irrevocable, made at the acquisition date; it affects carrying values and ongoing depreciation, amortisation, and impairment cadence. IPO relevance is specific: an acquired subsidiary being spun out presents on either a **push-down** basis or a **carve-out** basis (historical cost with parent-cost allocations), and the choice drives materially different reported earnings profiles. The choice is disclosed and the auditor opines on the presentation.

## Sector-specific audit considerations

Audit workload and technical complexity vary materially by sector. **SaaS — ASC 606** drives complexity around performance-obligation identification (subscription vs. professional services vs. implementation vs. training, often bundled), transaction-price allocation, variable consideration (usage-based, tiered pricing, discounts, rebates), and timing (over-time vs. point-in-time). Multi-year contracts with variable ramps trigger contract-modification accounting; reseller and channel arrangements trigger principal-vs.-agent analysis. **Stock-based compensation — ASC 718.** Grant-date fair value uses Black-Scholes, Monte Carlo, or lattice models depending on award features; fair-value inputs (expected volatility, term, risk-free rate, dividend yield) are estimates the auditor tests. Pre-IPO common-stock values set by periodic **409A valuations** face specific scrutiny; **cheap stock** — pre-IPO grants at values materially below the eventual IPO price — is an SEC comment-letter frequent flyer and drives the retrospective valuation-adjustment workstream. **Leases — ASC 842.** Right-of-use assets and lease liabilities on the balance sheet, discount-rate estimation, and portfolio disclosure become material workload for issuers with substantial real-estate footprints. **Deferred-tax-asset valuation — ASC 740.** The realisability analysis assesses whether it is **more likely than not** that a DTA (from NOLs, credits, temporary differences) will be realised; pre-profitability issuers typically carry a **full valuation allowance**. Documentation is a persistent auditor focus, and the eventual release of the allowance at sustained profitability is a specific S-1 disclosure and consultation event.

## Summary

The PCAOB-registered auditor engagement supports the underwriter's Section 11 expertised-portion due-diligence defence, the buy-side's aftermarket-quality signal, and the audit committee's ongoing governance discipline. Big Four selection buys pricing-meeting comfort and comparable-issuer signal; next-tier firms buy fee, partner attention, and vertical fit. **Sarbanes-Oxley Section 102** registration is threshold; **Section 104** inspections produce public **Part I.A** / **I.B** and confidential **Part II** findings that inform audit-committee diligence. **Reg S-X Rule 2-01** independence imposes non-audit-service prohibitions ((c)(4)), business- and employment-relationship rules and the 1-year FROR cooling-off ((c)(3), (c)(2), (c)(2)(iii)), and the 5-year lead-partner / 5-year concurring-partner / 7-year other-partner rotation regime ((c)(6)), all enforced by **Section 202** pre-approval and confirmed under **PCAOB Rule 3526**. Auditor changes are disclosed under **Item 304 of Reg S-K** in the S-1 and **Form 8-K Item 4.01** post-IPO, with an **Item 601(b)(16)** former-auditor letter. The audit-history footprint is **2 years** for EGCs under **JOBS Act Section 7(a)(2)(A)** and **3 years P&L / 2 years balance sheet** for non-EGCs under **Reg S-X Rules 3-01 and 3-02**. Audits run under PCAOB Auditing Standards, with **AS 3101** governing the auditor's report and CAMs (EGCs exempt), **AS 4105** governing interim reviews (negative assurance), **AS 1301** governing audit-committee communications and the private session, **AS 2201** governing the Section 404(b) integrated ICFR audit, **AS 2410** governing related-party procedures feeding the Item 404 disclosure, and **AS 6101** governing predecessor communications. **AU 634 / SAS 72** governs the underwriter comfort letter and its circle-up workflow. **Item 601(b)(18)** preferability letters accompany voluntary accounting changes. Reaudit and re-issuance convert AICPA-era audits to PCAOB opinions with 3–9 month timelines and 2–3x annual audit fees per period. **ASC 805-50** pushdown accounting shapes carve-out and spinout presentations. Sector complexity in **ASC 606**, **ASC 718** (409A cheap-stock scrutiny), **ASC 842**, and **ASC 740** valuation allowances shapes engagement scope and CAMs.
