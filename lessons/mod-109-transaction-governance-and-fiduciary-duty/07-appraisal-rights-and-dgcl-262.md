# Appraisal Rights and DGCL §262 Exposure

## Why this matters

**Appraisal rights** are the statutory remedy that gives dissenting stockholders in specified merger and consolidation transactions the right to have the Delaware Court of Chancery determine the "fair value" of their shares — as of the effective date of the merger, exclusive of any element of value arising from the accomplishment or expectation of the merger — and be paid that amount in cash (with interest at the statutory rate). The right is a specific statutory carve-out from the ordinary majority-rule that binds all stockholders to a merger approved by the requisite vote. It exists to protect minority stockholders from being cashed out at a price they consider unfair.

For the transaction-side board and its advisors, appraisal-rights exposure is a specific *deal-economics* consideration distinct from the fiduciary-duty analysis of chapters 1–6. A transaction that satisfies Revlon, that satisfies MFW (or Corwin), that has a clean fairness opinion, and that gets clean stockholder approval can *still* face substantial appraisal-rights exposure from a specific class of dissenting stockholders — often specialist funds ("appraisal arbitrage") that acquired positions post-announcement specifically to file appraisal claims. The Delaware Chancery Court's appraisal caselaw over 2016–2019 (Aruba / DFC Global / Dell) materially repositioned deal price as the presumptive fair value in an arms-length sale process, but appraisal exposure remains a material consideration in specific transaction types.

This chapter walks the DGCL §262 statutory framework, the market-out exception that eliminates appraisal for many public-company mergers, the Aruba / DFC Global / Dell arc that repositioned the doctrine, the appraisal-arbitrage economics, and the drag-along / voting-agreement mechanics that pre-empt individual dissents at private-company scale.

## The DGCL §262 statutory framework

**Delaware General Corporation Law §262** confers appraisal rights on stockholders of a Delaware corporation that is a constituent corporation in a merger, consolidation, or specified other transaction, subject to the specific eligibility, procedural, and exception provisions of the statute.

**Eligibility.** Appraisal rights are available to stockholders of the constituent corporation who: (a) held the shares of record on the date the demand for appraisal is made and continuously through the effective date, (b) did not vote in favour of the merger, (c) delivered a written demand for appraisal to the corporation before the vote, and (d) neither withdraw the demand nor otherwise take actions that terminate appraisal rights.

**Class of stockholders.** Appraisal rights typically apply to all classes of stock — common and preferred — that receive consideration in the merger, unless the specific class is exempted under the market-out exception (below) or by charter provisions.

**Procedural requirements.** The stockholder must:

1. Deliver a written demand for appraisal to the corporation before the merger vote.
2. Not vote in favour of the merger (a stockholder who votes yes waives appraisal).
3. Continuously hold the shares from the demand through the effective date.
4. Perfect the demand by continuing to comply with procedural requirements.

The corporation must:

1. Provide notice of appraisal rights to eligible stockholders in the merger proxy (or specified pre-merger notice).
2. After the merger effective date, deliver a specific notice to stockholders who perfected appraisal rights, providing information about the appraisal process.
3. Either negotiate a fair-value settlement with the appraisal claimants or defend the appraisal action in Chancery.

**Fair-value determination.** Chancery determines fair value through a valuation proceeding — evidentiary hearings, expert witness testimony (typically financial-valuation experts for both sides), and a written opinion determining fair value. The court's determination is binding.

**Interest.** The statutory interest rate is set at 5% above the Federal Reserve discount rate compounded quarterly, accruing from the effective date through the payment date. The interest rate has historically been substantially above the plaintiff's alternative-investment yield, which is a specific driver of the appraisal-arbitrage economics discussed below.

The **2016 §262 amendment** added specific provisions to reduce speculative appraisal filings — including a *de minimis* exception (permitting the corporation to bypass appraisal for stockholder-groups holding less than the lesser of 1% of the merger class or $1M of merger consideration) and permitting the corporation to prepay a specified amount to reduce interest accrual.

## The market-out exception — DGCL §262(b)(1) and (b)(2)

The **market-out exception** eliminates appraisal rights for many public-company mergers, but not all. The specific rule:

**§262(b)(1) — the "public market" exclusion.** Appraisal rights are not available for shares of any class or series that were, on the record date fixed for determining stockholders entitled to receive notice of the meeting to vote on the merger, either (a) listed on a national securities exchange or (b) held of record by more than 2,000 holders.

**§262(b)(2) — the "cash / consideration" exception to the exclusion.** The §262(b)(1) exclusion does *not* apply — meaning appraisal rights *do* attach — if the merger consideration is *anything other than* (a) shares of stock of the surviving corporation, (b) shares of stock of another publicly-traded corporation listed on a national securities exchange or held of record by more than 2,000 holders, (c) cash in lieu of fractional shares, or (d) a combination of (a), (b), and (c).

**The practical implication:** Appraisal rights attach to any all-cash public-company merger. Appraisal rights do *not* attach to a stock-for-stock merger where both companies remain publicly traded post-close.

**Practical examples:**

- **All-cash sale of a NYSE-listed target to a strategic acquirer.** Appraisal rights attach (market-out §262(b)(1) is applicable to the exclusion, but §262(b)(2) restores the rights because consideration is cash).
- **Stock-for-stock merger of two NYSE-listed public companies.** Appraisal rights do not attach (market-out §262(b)(1) applies and §262(b)(2) does not restore because consideration is publicly-listed stock).
- **Controller take-private of a NYSE-listed target for cash.** Appraisal rights attach (same as all-cash sale).
- **All-cash sale of a private Delaware corporation.** §262(b)(1) does not apply (the shares are not publicly traded), so appraisal rights attach without needing §262(b)(2) analysis. This is the case for private-company sales.
- **Mixed cash-and-stock consideration in a public-company merger.** Appraisal rights typically attach because the cash component breaks the §262(b)(2) exception.

The market-out exception is the reason appraisal-rights exposure is a specific consideration in cash-consideration public-company deals but not in stock-for-stock mergers.

## The Aruba / DFC Global / Dell arc — deal price as presumptive fair value

Between 2016 and 2019, the Delaware Supreme Court issued a series of appraisal decisions that materially repositioned the doctrine. Prior to this arc, Chancery appraisal decisions had frequently determined fair value through independent DCF analyses, sometimes yielding "fair values" materially above (or below) the deal price. This created a specific arbitrage opportunity for appraisal-focused funds.

**DFC Global Corp. v. Muirfield Value Partners, L.P., 172 A.3d 346 (Del. 2017).** The Delaware Supreme Court reversed a Chancery decision that had found fair value at 15% above deal price. The court held that where the sale process was "robust" — arm's-length, market-checked, competitive — the deal price should carry substantial weight in the fair-value determination, and specifically criticised the lower court's DCF-based fair-value calculation. The court identified specific process indicators supporting deal-price weight: broad market check, multiple bidders, arm's-length negotiation, no controller conflict, informed disinterested-stockholder approval.

**Dell Inc. v. Magnetar Global Event Driven Master Fund Ltd., 177 A.3d 1 (Del. 2017).** The Delaware Supreme Court reversed a Chancery decision that had found fair value at 22% above deal price in the Dell go-private transaction. The court emphasised that the deal price in a "meaningful" sale process should be given substantial weight, and rejected specific criticisms of the process. Even though the Dell deal was a controller take-private (Michael Dell was on the buy-side), the court found the process — with an active go-shop and multiple bidders — sufficiently market-testing to support deal-price weight.

**Verition Partners Master Fund Ltd. v. Aruba Networks, Inc., 210 A.3d 128 (Del. 2019).** The Delaware Supreme Court reversed a Chancery decision that had found fair value *below* the deal price — specifically at the pre-announcement trading price. In a peculiar procedural posture, the Chancellor had used unaffected market price as the presumptive fair value, holding that synergies from the merger should be deducted. The Supreme Court reversed the specific mechanics of the deduction but reaffirmed the broader principle that deal price (properly adjusted) is the presumptive fair value where the process is robust.

The **combined effect** of DFC Global / Dell / Aruba:

- Deal price is the presumptive fair value where the sale process is "robust" — arms-length, market-checked, competitive.
- DCF analyses can support or refine the fair-value determination but are not the presumptive anchor where deal price is available.
- Where the sale process is *not* robust — controller conflict without adequate mitigation, absence of market check, structural coercion — DCF and other analyses can drive a fair value materially above deal price.
- Appraisal arbitrage becomes much less attractive economically in robust-process transactions.

The post-Aruba practical framework: a robust process significantly reduces appraisal exposure. A weak process — controller take-private without MFW, single-bidder without market check, absence of fairness opinion or with a conflicted opinion — preserves appraisal exposure.

## Appraisal-arbitrage economics

The **appraisal-arbitrage** trade is a specific strategy pursued by specialist funds (historically including Merion Capital, Verition Partners, Magnetar Capital, and others):

1. A merger is announced with cash consideration at a specific per-share price.
2. The fund acquires shares in the market at the deal price (or slightly below, given deal-risk discount).
3. The fund perfects appraisal rights.
4. The fund pursues a Chancery appraisal action seeking a determination of fair value above the deal price.
5. The fund earns (a) the difference between fair value and deal price (if any), and (b) the statutory interest rate on the appraisal claim from effective date through payment.

The economics work when:

- The statutory interest rate exceeds the fund's cost of capital (historically true, given the Fed-discount-rate-plus-5% formulation).
- The fair value determination is expected to exceed the deal price.
- The transaction is large enough to justify the litigation cost.

The post-Aruba framework has substantially reduced the "fair value exceeds deal price" prospect for robust-process deals, and the 2016 §262 amendments (de minimis exception, prepayment option) have reduced interest-arbitrage economics. Appraisal arbitrage remains active but has narrowed to specific transaction profiles — controller take-privates with weak process, going-privates with limited market check, transactions with material process-defect indicators.

## Interest-rate arbitrage and the 2016 amendments

The pre-2016 statutory interest rate — Fed discount rate + 5%, compounded quarterly — was substantially above appraisal claimants' alternative-investment yields, creating a specific incentive to file appraisal claims even where the fair-value determination was expected to be near deal price. A claim that resulted in no fair-value premium still generated substantial interest earnings.

The 2016 amendments to §262 addressed this in two ways:

1. **De minimis exception.** The corporation may treat as ineligible for appraisal any stockholder-group holding less than the lesser of 1% of the merger class or $1M of merger consideration. This eliminates small speculative filings.
2. **Prepayment option.** The corporation may prepay a specified amount to appraisal claimants after the effective date. Interest on the prepaid amount stops accruing. This lets the corporation eliminate interest exposure on any specific portion of the claim it is confident will be included in the fair-value determination.

The prepayment option is a specific transaction-defence tool. The corporation typically prepays an amount equal to the deal price × shares in appraisal, eliminating interest exposure on that portion and shifting the appraisal focus to the *incremental* fair value above deal price.

## The proxy disclosure regime for appraisal rights

The corporation must provide **notice of appraisal rights** in the merger proxy. The specific notice requirements:

- A statement that appraisal rights are available and the specific class(es) of stockholders eligible.
- The specific procedural requirements for perfecting appraisal — written demand before the vote, no vote in favour, continuous holding through effective date.
- The specific deadlines for making the demand.
- The full text of §262 (typically attached as an appendix to the proxy).
- Post-effective-date notice requirements the corporation will comply with.

The notice must be delivered to eligible stockholders no less than 20 days before the vote (with specific timing variations for tender-offer structures).

Failure to provide adequate notice is a specific procedural defect that can extend appraisal rights (i.e., a stockholder who did not receive proper notice may retain the right to seek appraisal notwithstanding the specific procedural requirements). The proxy is drafted by corporation's counsel with specific care to the §262 notice requirements.

## Common transaction structures that shape appraisal exposure

**Tender offer with back-end short-form merger (§253) or medium-form merger (§251(h)).** In a tender offer where the acquirer achieves 90%+ ownership, the back-end merger can be effected as a "short-form" merger under §253 without a stockholder vote. In a §251(h) medium-form merger, an acquirer achieving the applicable percentage (typically 50%+ under the specific §251(h) framework) can effect the back-end merger without a stockholder vote. Stockholders who did not tender have appraisal rights in the back-end merger. This structure is common in cash tender offers and specifically manages the vote-solicitation timing.

**One-step merger with stockholder vote.** The transaction is a single-step merger requiring a stockholder vote. Appraisal rights attach for cash-consideration deals.

**Reverse triangular merger.** The acquirer forms a merger sub, the merger sub merges into the target with the target as the surviving corporation, target stockholders receive cash from the merger sub, and target becomes a wholly-owned subsidiary of the acquirer. Appraisal rights attach for cash consideration.

**Asset sale followed by dissolution.** An asset sale does not itself trigger appraisal rights under §262 (which is limited to specified merger, consolidation, and other transactions). Some asset sale structures effectively achieve the equivalent of a merger without triggering appraisal — a specific structural consideration in transactions where appraisal exposure is a specific concern.

**Reincorporation from Delaware to a non-Delaware jurisdiction.** Occasionally used as a structural manoeuvre; can trigger appraisal rights depending on specifics and generally raises specific fiduciary-duty questions.

## Private-company appraisal — the drag-along interaction

At private-company scale, most transactions with a controller or majority-VC-backed cap table use the **NVCA model voting agreement** or a similar contractual mechanism that includes a **drag-along** provision. The drag-along binds all covered stockholders to a sale approved by a specified vote (typically majority of preferred, sometimes majority of common, sometimes a combination), including the specific obligation to (a) vote in favour, (b) waive appraisal rights, (c) sign transaction documents, (d) provide reps and warranties, and (e) accept the same consideration structure as other stockholders.

The **drag-along waiver of appraisal** is a specific contractual pre-emption of appraisal rights. When properly drafted and properly invoked, the drag-along means that stockholders who might otherwise dissent and seek appraisal are contractually bound to vote yes (which waives appraisal) and to waive appraisal even if they somehow could vote no.

The drag-along is generally enforceable under Delaware contract law, though there are specific limitations:

- The drag-along must have been agreed to by the specific stockholders being dragged, either at the time of investment (if the drag was in the original financing documents) or through a subsequent amendment they consented to.
- The drag-along must be triggered under the specific terms of the agreement (correct voting threshold, correct procedural steps, correct notice).
- The drag-along cannot be used to procure a transaction that violates fiduciary duties (e.g., a fraudulent transaction, a transaction with a controller conflict that lacks proper cleansing).
- Specific stockholders (typically holders who did not sign the voting agreement, or holders who acquired shares outside the voting-agreement structure) may not be subject to the drag.

The practitioner discipline at private-company transaction stage:

1. Review the voting agreement and the drag-along to identify which stockholders are covered.
2. Identify any stockholders *not* covered by the drag-along — these are the appraisal-exposure stockholders.
3. Consider whether individual consent, individual settlement, or specific structural mitigations (e.g., a tender-offer alternative) can eliminate the residual exposure.
4. For any residual appraisal exposure, model the specific §262 mechanics and the specific fair-value analysis to size the exposure.

## Appraisal-defence practitioner discipline

The corporation's appraisal-defence practitioner discipline over the transaction and post-close:

**Pre-signing.** Structure the process to be as "robust" as possible in the DFC Global / Dell / Aruba sense — arms-length negotiation, market check, competitive tension, informed and uncoerced stockholder vote, clean fairness opinion, no material process defects. A robust process is the primary appraisal defence.

**Proxy disclosure.** Deliver clean §262 notice. Fully disclose the process, the fairness opinion, and any conflicts.

**Vote outcome.** A strong disinterested-stockholder-majority vote (well above the 50% threshold) is a specific process-quality indicator that supports deal-price weight in appraisal.

**Post-effective-date.** Deliver the required post-effective-date notices to appraisal claimants. Track specific claimants and specific share amounts.

**Prepayment.** Consider the prepayment option to eliminate interest exposure on the deal-price portion of any appraisal claim.

**Settlement.** Evaluate settlement of appraisal claims. Many appraisal actions are settled at or near deal price given the post-Aruba framework; specific claims involving process-defect indicators may settle at a modest premium.

**Litigation.** Where settlement is not achievable, defend the appraisal action in Chancery. Retain specific appraisal-experienced counsel (Richards Layton & Finger, Morris Nichols, Potter Anderson, Young Conaway, plus non-Delaware firms with Delaware benches).

## The interaction between appraisal and fiduciary-duty litigation

Appraisal and fiduciary-duty litigation are separate remedies with separate frameworks:

- **Appraisal** is a statutory remedy for individual dissenting stockholders. The remedy is a fair-value payment. The corporation's directors are not personally liable in the appraisal action; the appraisal is a valuation proceeding.
- **Fiduciary-duty litigation** is a class-action-or-derivative remedy for stockholders alleging director breach. The remedy is damages or injunctive relief. Directors (and aiders-and-abettors) can be personally liable.

A specific transaction can face both simultaneously — an appraisal action from specific dissenters and a fiduciary-duty class action from other stockholders. The doctrines interact in specific ways:

- A Corwin-cleansed transaction generally has fiduciary-duty exposure dismissed, but appraisal remains available for dissenters.
- A MFW-cleansed controller transaction similarly has fiduciary exposure dismissed while appraisal remains available.
- A fair-value determination in appraisal materially above deal price is evidence of process-defect and can support a subsequent (or parallel) fiduciary-duty claim.
- A fair-value determination at deal price supports the corporation's fiduciary-duty defence.

The corporation's counsel manages both workstreams in parallel and coordinates defence strategy across them.

## Summary

DGCL §262 provides appraisal rights to dissenting stockholders in specified merger and consolidation transactions, subject to eligibility, procedural, and market-out exception provisions. The market-out exception (§262(b)(1) / (b)(2)) eliminates appraisal for stock-for-stock mergers of publicly-listed companies but preserves it for cash-consideration deals. The DFC Global / Dell / Aruba arc (2017–2019) repositioned deal price as the presumptive fair value in robust-process transactions, materially reducing appraisal-arbitrage economics. The 2016 §262 amendments (de minimis exception, prepayment option) further reduced speculative filings. The proxy must provide §262 notice with specific procedural requirements. Common transaction structures — tender offer with §253/§251(h) back-end merger, one-step merger, reverse triangular merger — shape appraisal exposure in specific ways. At private-company scale, the NVCA voting-agreement drag-along contractually pre-empts appraisal for covered stockholders, subject to specific enforceability limits. Appraisal-defence practitioner discipline: structure a robust process, deliver clean §262 notice, achieve strong disinterested-vote support, consider prepayment, evaluate settlement, retain appraisal-experienced counsel for any litigation. Appraisal and fiduciary-duty litigation are separate remedies that can proceed in parallel and interact in specific ways.
