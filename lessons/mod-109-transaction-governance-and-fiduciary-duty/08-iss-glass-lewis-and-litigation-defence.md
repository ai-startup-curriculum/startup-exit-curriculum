# ISS, Glass Lewis, and Stockholder-Litigation Defence

## Why this matters

Two adjacent workstreams close out the transaction-governance module. The first is **proxy-advisor engagement** — the specific pre-engagement discipline with Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) and Glass Lewis whose recommendations move institutional-holder votes on M&A transactions and IPO-governance-graduation matters. A negative ISS or Glass Lewis recommendation on a large M&A vote can shift 15–25% of the institutional-holder vote and, in a close vote, sink the deal. Engaging the proxy advisors early, understanding their published methodology, delivering the transaction rationale in the specific formats they consume, and (if a negative recommendation issues) authoring the response-to-recommendation letter is a distinct workstream that runs in parallel to the Corwin / MFW / fairness-opinion machinery.

The second is **stockholder-litigation defence** — the specific mechanisms the corporation puts in place to structure, limit, and manage the class-action, derivative, and Section 11 IPO litigation that follows nearly every announced sale and every going-public transaction. The specific mechanisms include exclusive-forum bylaws (choosing Delaware Court of Chancery as the venue for internal-affairs disputes), federal-forum bylaws (specifying federal district courts for Section 11 claims), D&O tail-policy purchase (typically a 6-year run-off for post-close claims), R&W-insurance interaction, and the plaintiff-firm engagement pattern that follows every sale (WeissLaw, Halper Sadeh, Brodsky Smith, Kaskela, Monteverde, and dozens of others).

This chapter walks the ISS and Glass Lewis methodologies for transaction and IPO-governance votes, the pre-engagement and response-to-recommendation discipline, the forum-selection-bylaw framework post-*Salzberg v. Sciabacucchi*, the D&O tail-policy structure, the R&W-insurance interaction, and the plaintiff-firm engagement pattern that closes out every deal announcement.

## Proxy advisors — who they are and why they matter

**Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS)** and **Glass, Lewis & Co.** are the two dominant proxy advisory firms in the US. Together they influence a substantial share of institutional-holder proxy voting through published recommendations, custom-policy engagement, and proxy-vote-execution services.

**ISS.** Founded 1985, owned by Deutsche Börse (through Deutsche Börse's 2020 acquisition, replacing the earlier Genstar-Vestar ownership). ISS publishes policy guidelines annually — the **U.S. Proxy Voting Guidelines** — and specific supplementary policies for specific transaction types (M&A, compensation, environmental / social / governance topics). ISS provides both "policy-based" recommendations (following the published guidelines) and "custom" recommendations (following an institutional client's specific policy). The ISS analytics arm — ISS Analytics — publishes company-specific analytical reports on transactions. The ISS **Governance QualityScore** rates companies on specific governance dimensions and is a widely-consumed reference.

**Glass Lewis.** Founded 2003, owned by Peloton Capital Management and Stephen Smith (from 2021, replacing the earlier Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan Board ownership). Glass Lewis publishes its **Policy Guidelines** annually and provides voting recommendations for M&A, compensation, and governance matters. Glass Lewis's methodology is somewhat more principles-based than ISS's, and Glass Lewis is often perceived as more issuer-favourable on close-call M&A votes (though this is a pattern-observation rather than a hard rule).

Institutional holder voting practice varies:

- Many long-only institutional holders (index funds, large mutual-fund complexes) follow ISS or Glass Lewis recommendations as a default, deviating only for specific holdings where the fund has an independent view.
- Larger active-management complexes (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Wellington, Capital Group) have internal stewardship teams that develop independent voting positions, though they often reference ISS / Glass Lewis as inputs.
- Passive-management complexes (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) have internal stewardship teams that increasingly develop independent voting positions on high-profile matters, but often follow ISS / Glass Lewis for routine and mid-scale matters.
- The specific "float" of the vote that follows ISS / Glass Lewis is transaction-specific and holder-mix-specific; for a mid-cap public-company M&A vote with substantial index-fund and long-only ownership, an ISS "against" recommendation can shift 15–25% of the vote or more.

## ISS M&A methodology — Insight-M&A

ISS's M&A vote recommendations follow specific methodology anchored in the U.S. Proxy Voting Guidelines. The specific analytical inputs:

**Valuation.** ISS analyses whether the offered consideration is fair, benchmarked against premia to unaffected trading price, comparable-transaction premia, and (increasingly) discounted-cash-flow and other valuation methodologies. The ISS-published deal analysis typically references specific benchmarks — 1-day premium, 30-day premium, 52-week high, deal-multiple, transaction-multiple comparables.

**Process.** ISS evaluates the sale process for indicia of robustness — market check breadth, competitive tension, negotiation quality, deal-protection terms (termination fee, matching rights, no-shop, fiduciary-out), any specific conflicts (controller, management, banker), and the special-committee construction (if applicable). A weak process is a specific ISS-negative factor.

**Deal terms.** ISS reads the specific terms — the definitive agreement's specific provisions, the closing conditions, the specific representations and warranties, the specific indemnification structure, and any specific side-agreements (management retention, rollover equity, transition arrangements).

**Strategic rationale.** ISS evaluates the specific strategic rationale — is the sale timing appropriate given the company's business trajectory, is the buyer a strategic or financial acquirer, what is the specific value-creation thesis.

**Board independence and conflicts.** ISS reads the board composition and any specific conflicts (management-buyer arrangements, controller interests, banker conflicts, director-personal interests).

**Governance overlay.** ISS applies specific governance considerations — Corwin cleansing indicators, MFW compliance indicators, fairness-opinion methodology and independence.

**ISS Insight-M&A** is the specific analytical product ISS produces for major M&A transactions — a detailed written analysis of the transaction with the specific ISS voting recommendation.

## Glass Lewis M&A methodology

Glass Lewis's methodology is documented in its **Policy Guidelines** and specific M&A supplementary guidance. The specific analytical elements overlap substantially with ISS's — valuation, process, deal terms, strategic rationale, governance — but Glass Lewis's presentation and specific analytical emphasis differs. Glass Lewis is typically:

- More heavily weight on process-robustness and market-check breadth.
- Less DCF-focused; more comparable-transaction-focused.
- More willing to defer to board judgement on strategic rationale where governance and process are strong.
- Occasionally more favourable than ISS on close-call transactions.

Both firms publish their M&A recommendation in a specific written report circulated to institutional-holder clients ahead of the vote.

## The pre-engagement discipline

The corporation's proactive engagement with ISS and Glass Lewis is a specific workstream:

**Timing.** Engagement typically begins 2–4 weeks after transaction announcement. Both firms need time to complete their analysis (typically 3–6 weeks) and publish their recommendation approximately 3 weeks before the vote.

**Team.** Engagement is typically led by the general counsel, investor relations lead, and specific transaction-team members (financial advisor and possibly outside counsel). Pre-meeting preparation is intensive.

**Materials.** The corporation prepares a proxy-advisor engagement deck — typically 20–40 slides covering the transaction rationale, process history, valuation analysis, deal terms, governance, and specific points ISS or Glass Lewis is expected to focus on. The materials should anticipate and address the specific analytical concerns each firm typically raises.

**Meetings.** Typically 60–90 minute meetings with each firm's specific analyst covering the transaction. The corporation walks the analyst through the deck, answers questions, and (importantly) commits to any follow-up materials the analyst requests.

**Follow-up.** The specific analyst may request additional information — specific financial-model outputs, specific board-meeting minutes, specific fairness-opinion presentation excerpts. Prompt response is a specific relationship-quality indicator.

**Non-engagement policy at some funds.** Some institutional-holder complexes and some proxy advisors have specific rules restricting corporate communication during the review period. The corporation should understand the specific rules before initiating engagement.

## The response-to-recommendation letter

When ISS or Glass Lewis publishes a recommendation, the corporation may (particularly for a negative recommendation) publish a **response-to-recommendation letter** to correct factual errors, address analytical concerns, and present specific counter-arguments. The letter is typically:

- Published as a supplement to the proxy or as an 8-K exhibit.
- 5–15 pages of specific factual and analytical response.
- Distributed to institutional holders directly (via investor-relations lists) and through the transaction advisor's investor-facing sales channel.
- Reviewed by outside counsel for SEC-disclosure compliance.
- Focused on facts and analysis, not on attacking the proxy advisor's motives or methodology.

Effective response letters can shift a subset of institutional holders — those with internal stewardship teams that read the response — but rarely change the ISS / Glass Lewis published recommendation itself.

## The IPO-governance-graduation vote

The pre-IPO charter amendment (that eliminates preferred-stock special voting rights, converts preferred to common, and installs the IPO-target charter) typically requires stockholder consent. This is a pre-IPO vote and is often obtained via written consent under DGCL §228 rather than a proxy vote — so ISS / Glass Lewis are not directly engaged.

The specific IPO-governance-graduation matters that *do* engage ISS / Glass Lewis appear post-IPO:

**Multi-class stock structures.** IPOs with founder-favouring dual-class structures (Facebook / Meta, Google / Alphabet, Snap, Palantir, Airbnb, Coupang, and many others) draw specific ISS and Glass Lewis attention. Both firms have policies disfavouring multi-class structures without adequate sunset provisions and against directors who fail to address the multi-class structure over time. The first post-IPO annual meeting draws ISS / Glass Lewis review of the specific governance structure — the specific voting-rights allocation, sunset provisions, protective provisions, and specific director-election consequences.

**Board classification.** Classified boards (staggered election of directors) draw ISS / Glass Lewis specific attention. Post-IPO ISS / Glass Lewis review typically includes a "declassify the board" recommendation over time.

**Anti-takeover provisions.** Poison pills adopted at IPO (or shortly after), specific bylaw provisions restricting stockholder action by written consent, restrictions on stockholder ability to call special meetings — all draw specific ISS / Glass Lewis attention.

**Say-on-pay and executive compensation.** Every US-listed public company is subject to annual say-on-pay votes under Dodd-Frank §14A. ISS and Glass Lewis compensation-methodology (ISS's Insight-Compensation module, Glass Lewis's compensation-quality assessment) drive substantial vote shift on close say-on-pay decisions.

The IPO-governance-graduation transition is not a single-vote event but an ongoing dialogue with the institutional-holder community that plays out over multiple annual meetings. The pre-IPO governance structure choices set the stage; the post-IPO ISS / Glass Lewis engagement discipline manages the ongoing dialogue.

## Exclusive-forum bylaws — the Delaware venue anchor

**Exclusive-forum bylaws** are corporate-bylaw provisions that specify Delaware (typically the Court of Chancery) as the exclusive forum for specified categories of corporate litigation — typically internal-affairs disputes, derivative actions, fiduciary-duty claims, and DGCL-based claims. Their purpose is to consolidate corporate litigation in Delaware (the corporate-law-expert forum) rather than dispersing across multiple state and federal courts.

**Delaware bylaw enforceability.** *Boilermakers Local 154 Retirement Fund v. Chevron Corp.*, 73 A.3d 934 (Del. Ch. 2013) upheld the enforceability of a board-adopted exclusive-forum bylaw for internal-affairs claims. Delaware itself is comfortable enforcing exclusive-forum bylaws in its own courts.

**Non-Delaware enforceability.** Non-Delaware courts have generally enforced Delaware exclusive-forum bylaws under the specific choice-of-forum framework, though specific state and federal court decisions have varied.

**Federal-forum bylaws for Section 11 claims.** *Salzberg v. Sciabacucchi*, 227 A.3d 102 (Del. 2020) upheld the enforceability of a Delaware corporation's bylaw specifying federal district courts as the exclusive forum for Securities Act Section 11 claims. The bylaw addresses the specific problem of Section 11 IPO-litigation being filed in state courts (particularly California state court after the U.S. Supreme Court's *Cyan v. Beaver County Employees Retirement Fund*, 138 S. Ct. 1061 (2018) decision confirmed that state courts retain concurrent jurisdiction over Section 11 claims). The **federal-forum-bylaw** consolidates Section 11 litigation in federal court, reducing exposure to multi-forum litigation and specific state-court procedural disadvantages.

The typical bylaws package for a going-public Delaware corporation now includes:

1. **Delaware Court of Chancery exclusive forum** for internal-affairs disputes, derivative actions, fiduciary-duty claims, and DGCL-based claims.
2. **Federal district court exclusive forum** for Section 11 claims (post-*Salzberg*).
3. Specific carve-outs for specific case types (e.g., securities-fraud claims under Exchange Act §10(b) that must be filed in federal court under specific federal statutes).

The bylaws are adopted by the board (typically before IPO pricing) and disclosed in the S-1.

## D&O tail-policy purchase — the 6-year run-off

The corporation's ordinary-course **Directors and Officers (D&O) liability insurance** covers directors and officers for claims arising from their service, subject to specific policy terms. The **D&O tail policy** — also called a "run-off" policy or "extended reporting period" — is a specific policy purchased at the time of a transaction (typically an M&A close, going-private, or IPO) that provides coverage for claims filed after the transaction based on pre-transaction acts.

The transaction-context D&O tail is typically:

- **6-year term.** Covers claims filed within 6 years of the transaction close based on pre-close acts. The 6-year term is market convention; longer or shorter terms are possible but 6 is the modal choice, corresponding to the effective statute-of-limitations period for many Delaware and federal claims.
- **Same or higher policy limits as the ordinary-course policy.** The tail typically maintains the same aggregate limit structure (often $100M–$500M+ depending on company size) as the ordinary-course policy. Additional limits or specific excess-tail arrangements are sometimes purchased.
- **Same or broader coverage terms.** The tail typically preserves the ordinary-course policy's terms and may broaden specific provisions (e.g., extending Side A coverage — the non-indemnifiable-loss coverage that protects directors from personal financial exposure).
- **Non-cancellable.** Once purchased, the tail cannot be cancelled by the corporation or (typically) by the insurer, providing a stable insurance backstop for the run-off period.
- **Pre-paid premium.** The premium is paid at transaction close (typically 200–350% of the annual ordinary-course premium for a 6-year tail). The premium is typically a specific transaction-close expense.

Purchase of the tail:

- In M&A close, the tail is a specific merger-agreement covenant — the target's board typically negotiates that a specific D&O tail (at a specific limit and term) will be purchased at close, funded by the buyer or from the transaction consideration.
- In going-private, the tail is typically funded from transaction proceeds and negotiated similarly.
- In IPO context, the tail is not typically purchased at IPO itself (the corporation continues its ordinary-course D&O coverage post-IPO); tails may be purchased later in specific circumstances (a subsequent acquisition, a specific management transition).

**Selection of the tail carrier.** The tail is often placed with the same carrier as the ordinary-course policy for continuity. Specific brokers (Marsh, Aon, Willis Towers Watson, Lockton, and specific D&O specialists like Woodruff Sawyer) manage the placement.

## R&W insurance interaction

**Representations & Warranties (R&W) insurance** is a specific insurance product that protects the buyer (or, less commonly, the seller) against losses arising from breaches of the target's representations and warranties in the merger or acquisition agreement. It is now standard on mid-market and larger private-company M&A transactions and increasingly common in public-company M&A.

The R&W insurance interaction with the D&O and fiduciary-duty framework:

- R&W insurance is a *buyer-side* product covering financial losses from rep breaches. It does not cover fiduciary-duty claims from stockholders.
- R&W insurance can *replace* traditional indemnification escrow / holdback in the merger agreement, freeing up transaction consideration for stockholders.
- R&W insurance's specific policy exclusions (typically fraud, known-issue exclusions, specific exclusions negotiated during underwriting) can leave residual buyer exposure that is addressed through negotiated escrow or specific indemnification.
- The R&W underwriting process (typically 3–6 weeks) is a specific transaction-timeline consideration.

R&W insurance and D&O tail insurance are complementary — R&W covers *buyer* losses from rep breaches; D&O tail covers *director / officer* claims from stockholder litigation. Both are typically purchased at close.

## The plaintiff-firm engagement pattern

Every announced sale of a US-listed public company draws immediate response from plaintiff firms:

**The strike-suit firms.** WeissLaw LLP, Halper Sadeh LLP, Brodsky & Smith LLC, Kaskela Law LLC, Monteverde & Associates PC, Rigrodsky Law PA, Bragar Eagel & Squire PC, and dozens of others. The firms specialise in filing rapid post-announcement complaints alleging deficient proxy disclosure or process defects.

**The typical timeline.** Within 24–72 hours of transaction announcement, plaintiff firms issue press releases inviting stockholders to "investigate" the transaction. Within 1–3 weeks, one or more complaints are filed — typically in federal court (post-*Trulia*, Delaware Chancery is less receptive to disclosure-only settlements), often in the Southern District of New York, District of Delaware, or specific California or state venues.

**The disclosure-only complaint pattern.** The complaint alleges that the proxy is materially misleading or incomplete in specific respects — often including boilerplate allegations about banker-conflict disclosure, fairness-opinion methodology detail, or specific process-history omissions. The typical remedy sought is enhanced disclosure and (in the plaintiff firm's economics) a mootness-fee award to plaintiff counsel.

**The mootness-fee pattern.** The corporation may issue supplemental disclosure (typically in an 8-K) that addresses the specific allegations. The complaint is then voluntarily dismissed as "moot" — the corporation is not liable, no injunctive relief issues, no stockholder recovery. Plaintiff counsel then seeks (and often receives) a specific "mootness fee" — typically $200K–$500K — for having caused the disclosure improvement. *In re Trulia, Inc. S'holder Litig.*, 129 A.3d 884 (Del. Ch. 2016) largely killed the Delaware Chancery disclosure-only-settlement / mootness-fee economics; the pattern has migrated to federal courts and specific state courts. Delaware Chancery's more recent posture on federal-court mootness fees (*In re Anthem-Cigna Merger Litigation*, and specific 2020–2024 opinions) continues to evolve.

**The substantive class-action complaint.** A smaller number of complaints allege substantive fiduciary-duty breaches — process defects, banker conflicts, deficient special-committee construction. These complaints seek damages or (rarely) injunctive relief blocking the transaction.

**Consolidation and lead-counsel appointment.** Multiple parallel complaints in the same court are typically consolidated. Plaintiff firms compete for the lead-counsel appointment (which drives fee awards); consolidation and lead-counsel motions are a specific procedural workstream.

## The corporation's litigation-defence discipline

The corporation's specific defence workstreams:

**Anticipate the strike-suit response.** Every announced transaction should be expected to draw strike-suit filings. The corporation should have retained specific defence counsel (typically a large-firm litigation practice with securities-class-action experience — Wilson Sonsini, Cooley, Fenwick, Latham, Sullivan & Cromwell, Davis Polk, Skadden, Wachtell, Simpson Thacher, and many others) before announcement.

**Author strong proxy disclosure.** The best defence against disclosure-only complaints is a well-drafted proxy that fully addresses the specific patterns strike-suit complaints target. The special-committee counsel, the corporation's counsel, and the SEC-comment counsel jointly own the disclosure quality.

**Prepare supplemental disclosure quickly if needed.** Where a disclosure-only complaint identifies a genuinely meaningful issue, prompt supplemental disclosure (via 8-K) can moot the claim without material litigation cost.

**Defend substantive complaints on process quality.** The special-committee construction (chapter 4), the fairness-opinion procurement (chapter 6), the banker-conflict discipline (chapter 5), and the fiduciary-framework compliance (chapters 1–3) are the substantive-litigation-defence backbone. A well-documented process defends itself.

**Manage the D&O and R&W insurance interaction.** Defence costs and any damages exposure interact with the D&O tail policy and (for buyer-side coverage) R&W insurance. The specific coverage and specific claims-handling procedures should be understood at close.

**Track the plaintiff-firm ecosystem.** Specific plaintiff firms have specific patterns — some focus on disclosure, some on substantive fiduciary claims, some on specific transaction types. Tracking the ecosystem informs defence strategy.

## Section 11 IPO-specific litigation

Section 11 of the Securities Act of 1933 creates a strict-liability regime for material misstatements or omissions in a registration statement (S-1) for a registered offering. Section 11 claims can be brought by any purchaser of the offered securities (traceable to the offering), with specific defences for directors (a due-diligence defence) and specific proportional-liability defences.

Section 11 litigation is a specific IPO-context exposure:

**Post-*Cyan*.** State courts retain concurrent jurisdiction over Section 11 claims. Post-2018 saw a surge in California state court Section 11 filings against California-headquartered issuers.

**Federal-forum bylaws.** As discussed above, post-*Salzberg* Delaware corporations can adopt federal-forum bylaws to consolidate Section 11 litigation in federal court.

**Due-diligence discipline.** The S-1 diligence process — coordinated by underwriting counsel, driven by the auditor's comfort letters, and documented in specific diligence files — is the primary Section 11 defence.

**D&O insurance.** IPO-context D&O insurance typically includes specific Section 11 coverage; premium and terms are negotiated at pricing.

**Bespeaks-caution / safe-harbour analysis.** Forward-looking statements in the S-1 are subject to specific safe-harbour provisions; the "bespeaks caution" doctrine and Section 27A of the Securities Act govern specific forward-looking-statement disclosures.

Section 11 defence is a specialised workstream typically led by IPO-experienced litigation counsel.

## Summary

Proxy-advisor engagement — ISS Insight-M&A and Glass Lewis M&A recommendations — is a distinct workstream running in parallel to the Corwin / MFW / fairness-opinion machinery. Both firms analyse valuation, process, deal terms, strategic rationale, and governance. Pre-engagement (typically 2–4 weeks post-announcement), meeting materials, follow-up commitment, and (if needed) response-to-recommendation letters are the specific practitioner disciplines. Post-IPO ISS / Glass Lewis engagement addresses multi-class stock structures, board classification, anti-takeover provisions, and say-on-pay. Exclusive-forum bylaws — Delaware Court of Chancery for internal-affairs claims (upheld in *Boilermakers*) and federal district courts for Section 11 claims (upheld post-*Salzberg*) — consolidate corporate litigation in expert forums. D&O tail-policy purchase at transaction close (typically 6-year run-off, 200–350% of annual ordinary-course premium, non-cancellable, funded from transaction proceeds) provides a stable insurance backstop for post-close claims. R&W insurance covers buyer losses from rep breaches and is complementary to D&O tail. The strike-suit firm ecosystem (WeissLaw, Halper Sadeh, Brodsky Smith, Kaskela, Monteverde, and dozens of others) issues immediate post-announcement complaints; the disclosure-only / mootness-fee pattern largely killed by *Trulia* in Delaware has migrated to federal courts. The corporation's defence discipline: anticipate the strike-suit response, author strong proxy disclosure, prepare supplemental disclosure quickly if needed, defend substantive complaints on process quality, manage D&O and R&W insurance interaction, and track the plaintiff-firm ecosystem. Section 11 IPO-specific litigation is a distinct workstream managed through federal-forum bylaws, due-diligence discipline, D&O insurance, and safe-harbour analysis.
