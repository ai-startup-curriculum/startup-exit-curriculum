# Special Committee Construction — Composition, Charter, Advisors, and Minutes

## Why this matters

The special committee is the *operating vehicle* through which most of the fiduciary framework from chapters 1–3 gets executed. The MFW cleansing (chapter 2) is impossible without a proper special committee. The Revlon process (chapter 1) in a controller-conflict transaction runs through the committee. The Blasius-safe deliberation on franchise-affecting decisions (chapter 3) is typically vested in the committee. The fairness opinion (chapter 6) is procured by the committee. The banker-conflict analysis (chapter 5) is the committee's responsibility. The proxy disclosure that must fully-inform the stockholder vote (Corwin, MFW) describes the committee's process.

A special committee that is *properly constituted, properly advised, and properly documented* earns the deferential standard-of-review the transaction is capable of earning. A special committee that has independence flaws, mandate-scope flaws, advisor-selection flaws, or minute-discipline flaws collapses the framework and drops the transaction back to entire fairness — or, in the MFW context, defeats the cleansing entirely.

Chancery Court opinions on special-committee performance are remarkably granular. The court will read the specific minute-book entries, the specific committee agendas, the specific committee-advisor engagement letters, the specific meeting-by-meeting cadence, the specific presence-and-absence of the controller's representatives at committee meetings, the specific negotiation history reflected in the minutes. A committee that survives Chancery scrutiny is one whose minute book reads like an *actual* deliberative body did *actual* work.

This chapter walks the operational construction — composition rules, mandate scoping, advisor selection, meeting cadence, minute discipline, and the specific integration with the Rural Metro / Del Monte / El Paso banker-conflict analysis. Chapter 5 goes deeper on the banker-conflict piece.

## When a special committee is required — and when it is not

Not every transaction requires a special committee. The specific triggering conditions:

- **Controller-conflict transactions.** Any transaction where a controlling stockholder is on the other side, or has a material differential interest, requires a special committee if MFW cleansing (chapter 2) is the goal. Entire-fairness burden-shifting under Lynch also requires a well-functioning special committee.
- **Interested-director transactions.** A transaction where a majority of the board has a material conflict of interest — e.g., a management buyout where the CEO / CFO / other key management are on the buy-side — requires a committee of the non-conflicted directors to negotiate and approve.
- **Certain arms-length transactions with process-optics complications.** Even in an arms-length transaction, a special committee is often constituted when the CEO or a majority of the board has a specific arrangement (rollover equity, post-close employment) that could create the appearance of a conflict. The committee is not strictly required but is often used to shore up the record.
- **Certain going-private transactions under SEC Rule 13e-3.** Rule 13e-3 imposes specific disclosure requirements on going-private transactions; a special committee is commonly constituted to satisfy the "fairness" disclosure requirements of Item 1014.

A transaction with no controller conflict, no interested-director conflict, no material CEO / management conflict, and no going-private character generally does not require a special committee — the full board of independent directors can act.

## The composition rules — independence, disinterestedness, and skill

The Delaware caselaw on special-committee composition has three overlapping tests.

**Independence.** Each committee member must be independent of the controller (or, in an interested-director context, of the interested directors). Delaware independence analysis is fact-specific and rigorous. Prior-employment relationships, ongoing consulting arrangements, family relationships, joint investments, board seats on controller-affiliate boards, material commercial dealings, and long-standing personal friendships have all been analysed. The independence analysis is done at the transaction level — a director may be "independent" for ordinary-course NYSE or Nasdaq listing purposes but non-independent for the specific transaction context.

The specific independence-analysis inputs the committee's counsel should collect:

- The director's D&O questionnaire (updated for the transaction).
- The director's professional and personal biography.
- Any prior or current business relationships with the controller (or interested directors).
- Any prior or current personal or family relationships.
- Any prior or current investment overlap.
- Any prior board or advisory service to controller-affiliates.
- Any material charitable or civic-organisation overlap where the controller is a significant sponsor.

The committee's independent counsel documents the analysis in a formal memorandum, kept in the minute book, referencing specific caselaw precedents.

**Disinterestedness.** Each committee member must have no material personal interest in the transaction. A director who will roll over equity, sign an employment agreement with the buyer, receive a specific transaction bonus, or otherwise personally benefit differentially from the transaction is *not* disinterested for that transaction and cannot serve on the committee. This is a narrower test than independence — it looks specifically at the director's personal-benefit posture in the specific transaction, not at broader relationship independence.

**Skill and expertise.** The committee does not require a specific credential set, but Delaware courts read favourably a committee with a mix of financial literacy, industry expertise, and prior transaction experience. A committee with one director who is a former investment banker, one who is a current sitting CEO of a comparable-scale company, and one who is an experienced M&A lawyer or corporate audit-committee chair reads as more capable than a committee of three career-CEO-experts with no financial or M&A background. The specific skill mix should be documented in the mandate resolution as a reason for the committee's composition.

**Size.** Special committees are typically 2–5 members. A one-member committee is *possible* but rare and reads sub-optimally — one director cannot deliberate; the deliberation is the point. Two-member committees exist but require a mechanism for resolving disagreement (typically committee counsel deferring to unanimous). Three-member committees are the modal size for major transactions. Five-member committees exist for very large or complex deals.

## The mandate resolution — the constituting document

The special committee is constituted by a board resolution — the **mandate resolution** — that identifies the committee members, defines the committee's authority, and delegates specific board-level powers to the committee. The mandate resolution is drafted by the corporation's outside counsel in coordination with the committee's independent counsel and is approved by the full board.

The specific mandate-resolution elements:

**Composition.** Names the specific directors constituting the committee. Names the chair. Specifies the quorum requirement (typically a majority of the members).

**Charge.** Describes the committee's specific charge — typically some variant of: "to evaluate and, if the committee determines appropriate, negotiate, approve, and recommend to the full Board and to the stockholders (or, if applicable, to determine to reject) the proposed transaction with [Counterparty]."

**Authority to select and engage advisors.** Grants the committee the authority to retain independent legal counsel, independent financial advisor(s), and any other advisors it deems necessary or appropriate, at the corporation's expense. This authority is unconditional — the committee does not require additional board approval to engage advisors.

**Authority to negotiate.** Grants the committee the authority to negotiate directly with the counterparty (or the counterparty's representatives). This includes the authority to conduct a market check, consider alternative transactions, and explore alternatives to the proposed transaction.

**Authority to reject.** Grants the committee the authority to reject the transaction definitively. The full board and the counterparty are bound by a rejection — the transaction cannot proceed if the committee rejects.

**Absence of contingent authority.** The mandate should *not* include language that allows the full board (or the controller) to override the committee's decisions or that makes the committee's authority conditional on specific outcomes. Contingent-authority language destroys the MFW empowerment condition.

**Compensation.** Specifies the committee members' compensation for their work. Typically a fixed fee (either a lump sum or a per-meeting fee), paid regardless of transaction outcome. Compensation contingent on transaction completion creates a conflict of interest that undermines the committee's independence.

**Advisors' expense.** Confirms that the committee's advisor engagements are at the corporation's expense.

**Reporting.** Establishes reporting to the full board — typically the committee reports at each regular board meeting and produces a written recommendation at the conclusion of its work.

**Confidentiality.** Establishes the committee's confidentiality obligations and the committee's authority to hold executive sessions.

A well-drafted mandate resolution is 2–4 pages and is attached to the board minutes as an exhibit. The mandate resolution is a document Chancery will read carefully in any subsequent litigation.

## Independent legal counsel — selection and role

The committee's **independent legal counsel** is separately retained from the corporation's regular outside counsel (unless there is no conflict, which is rare in transactions requiring a special committee). Independent counsel represents the committee alone, owes duties to the committee alone, and drives the committee's process discipline.

The specific independent-legal-counsel-selection criteria:

- **Independence from the controller and from the corporation's counsel.** The chosen firm should have no material representation of the controller (or, in an interested-director context, of the interested directors) and no shared-partnership with the corporation's outside counsel.
- **Delaware corporate-law expertise.** The firm should have a specific Delaware corporate-law practice with recent special-committee work. Firms with this specific expertise include Richards Layton & Finger, Morris Nichols Arsht & Tunnell, Potter Anderson & Corroon, Young Conaway Stargatt & Taylor, Prickett Jones & Elliott, and non-Delaware firms with Delaware benches — Wachtell, Cravath, Skadden, Latham, Cooley, Wilson Sonsini, Simpson Thacher, Davis Polk, Fried Frank, Weil, Kirkland, Paul Weiss, Gibson Dunn, Sullivan & Cromwell.
- **Independent-advisor track record.** Prior representation of special committees in comparable transactions with a track record of committee-favourable outcomes.
- **Availability and staffing.** The committee's work is time-intensive; the counsel should be able to dedicate a senior partner and appropriate associate support.

The committee's counsel:

- Drafts the mandate resolution (or reviews the corporation's counsel's draft) and negotiates any changes.
- Conducts the independence analysis of committee members.
- Advises the committee on the Delaware fiduciary framework applicable to the transaction (Revlon, MFW, entire-fairness, Blasius as appropriate).
- Advises on the committee's process discipline — meeting cadence, materials in advance, executive session, documented negotiation.
- Advises on the financial-advisor selection and reviews the engagement letter for banker-conflict issues (chapter 5).
- Attends every committee meeting.
- Drafts (or reviews) the committee minutes.
- Reviews the fairness-opinion presentation and the opinion itself for legal sufficiency.
- Drafts (or reviews) the committee's written recommendation to the full board.
- Reviews the proxy statement's committee-and-fairness-opinion disclosure for accuracy and legal sufficiency.
- Prepares the committee for the proxy-filing / SEC-comment / stockholder-vote workstream.

## Independent financial advisor — selection and role

The committee's **independent financial advisor** — typically an investment bank engaged specifically by the committee, distinct from any bank engaged by the corporation or by the controller — provides valuation analysis, market context, transaction structuring advice, and (usually) the fairness opinion.

The specific independent-financial-advisor selection criteria:

- **Independence.** The bank should have no material engagement with the controller (or, in an interested-director context, with the interested directors) that could compromise the analysis. Recent past representations, sell-side coverage of controller-affiliate companies, and any specific stapled-financing arrangements with the counterparty must be evaluated. Chapter 5 walks the banker-conflict analysis in depth.
- **Reputation and franchise.** A recognised bank with a specific franchise in the sector — bulge-bracket firms (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi, Barclays) or specialist boutiques (Evercore, Lazard, Moelis, Centerview, Guggenheim, Rothschild, Houlihan Lokey, PJT Partners) are common special-committee choices.
- **Sector expertise.** Specific analyst and investment-banking coverage of the target's sector.
- **Fee structure and conflict transparency.** The engagement letter should disclose all fees, all recent relationships with the counterparty, and any staples or contingent economics.

The financial advisor's role:

- Runs a comprehensive valuation analysis — DCF, precedent transactions, trading comparables, LBO analysis, sum-of-parts as appropriate. Chapter 6 covers the valuation methodology in depth.
- Provides market-context analysis — comparable-transaction premia, sector-multiple context, recent-deal environment.
- Advises on transaction structure, deal-protection terms, and negotiation strategy.
- Delivers the fairness opinion (chapter 6).
- Attends committee meetings as requested.
- Prepares valuation presentations for the committee, including detailed appendices supporting the analysis.

The financial advisor is *not* the committee — the committee makes the decisions, deliberates, and negotiates. The advisor supports the committee with analysis and expertise.

## Meeting cadence — how often and how long

The committee meets *as often as the transaction requires*. In a major transaction, meeting cadence is typically:

- **Kickoff / mandate meeting.** The committee convenes for the first time, receives the mandate resolution, discusses the counterparty and the transaction, and authorises the advisor-selection process.
- **Advisor-selection meeting(s).** The committee interviews legal-counsel and financial-advisor candidates, discusses selection criteria, and formally engages the chosen advisors.
- **Weekly or bi-weekly working meetings.** During active negotiation phases, the committee meets weekly (or more frequently). Each meeting includes the committee's advisors, discusses the status of negotiations, reviews specific offers or counter-offers, and provides direction on next steps.
- **Valuation meetings.** As the financial advisor develops the valuation analysis, dedicated meetings walk the committee through the methodology, assumptions, and results.
- **Fairness-opinion meeting.** A specific meeting at which the financial advisor delivers the fairness opinion, typically with an accompanying detailed valuation presentation. The committee formally receives the opinion and moves toward recommendation.
- **Recommendation meeting.** The committee approves its recommendation to the full board (or its decision to reject).
- **Post-signing meetings.** After the transaction is announced, the committee typically has ongoing responsibilities (e.g., considering interim events, superior proposals, disclosure amendments) and may meet as needed.

A large controller take-private typically has 10–20 committee meetings over 2–6 months. A smaller transaction may have 5–8 meetings over 4–8 weeks. The specific cadence should reflect the transaction's complexity and duration; a "too few meetings" pattern is a specific vulnerability.

## Meeting mechanics — the who-is-in-the-room discipline

The committee's meetings should follow a specific attendance discipline:

- **Committee members.** All committee members should attend (in person or by video/phone, with attendance recorded). Absent members should receive materials and minutes and confirm ratification of decisions taken in absentia.
- **Committee's legal counsel.** Present at every meeting.
- **Committee's financial advisor.** Present at meetings addressing financial-advisor scope, valuation, negotiation strategy, or fairness opinion.
- **Corporation's outside counsel (regular counsel).** *Not* present at committee-only sessions; may be invited to specific meetings addressing matters where the corporation's counsel has specific expertise (e.g., regulatory clearance strategy).
- **The corporation's CEO / CFO / management.** *Not* present at committee-only sessions. May be invited to specific meetings to provide business information or answer questions, then dismissed for committee-only deliberation.
- **The controller and the controller's representatives.** *Not* present at committee meetings.
- **The controller's advisors.** *Not* present at committee meetings.

The committee should routinely hold **executive sessions** — portions of meetings with only committee members and the committee's independent legal and financial advisors present. Executive sessions are the venue for candid discussion of counter-party posture, of committee-member concerns, of negotiation strategy. Executive sessions should be noted in the minutes.

## Minute discipline — the paper trail that survives Chancery

Special-committee minutes are the primary evidentiary record Chancery Court reviews in subsequent litigation. Good minutes reflect *actual deliberation*; bad minutes are checklist entries that make the committee look passive.

**What to include in the minutes:**

- Meeting date, time, location, attendance (with specific member absences noted and reasons if applicable).
- Advance materials distributed and the date of distribution.
- The specific business topics discussed.
- The substantive deliberation — key questions asked by committee members, key concerns raised, specific issues that were probed with advisors.
- Advisor presentations — a summary of the analysis presented, the key conclusions, the specific committee questions.
- Executive sessions — noted, with the specific topic discussed at a summary level.
- Specific decisions taken — with the specific rationale and the specific committee member positions if not unanimous.
- Action items and follow-ups.
- Adjournment.

**What to avoid in the minutes:**

- Checklist entries ("The committee reviewed the transaction and approved.").
- Ambiguous references ("The committee considered various matters.").
- Substantive characterisation of legal advice (attorney-client privilege issues; the minutes should reflect that legal advice was received without characterising its substance).
- Verbatim transcripts (minutes are a summary, not a transcript; specific quotations create hostage-to-fortune).

The minutes are typically drafted by the committee's independent legal counsel, circulated to committee members within a week of the meeting, and formally approved at the next committee meeting. Approved minutes become part of the corporate record and are producible in litigation.

**Contemporaneous notes.** Committee members should be aware that their contemporaneous notes may be discoverable. Personal notes taken during meetings can end up in litigation. The best discipline is (a) minimal personal notes (rely on the minutes) or (b) careful, professional notes that reflect only the substantive content and not personal opinions or characterisations.

**Materials retention.** All materials distributed to the committee — presentations, board books, valuation packages, draft agreements — should be retained in a dedicated committee file maintained by the committee's counsel. The corporation's secretary should maintain a parallel file. Deleted or lost materials are Chancery-hostile.

## Banker-conflict integration — the Rural Metro / Del Monte / El Paso overlay

The committee's financial-advisor selection is subject to the aiding-and-abetting-breach-of-fiduciary-duty exposure articulated in *In re Rural Metro Corp. Stockholders Litigation*, 88 A.3d 54 (Del. Ch. 2014), aff'd sub nom. *RBC Capital Markets, LLC v. Jervis*, 129 A.3d 816 (Del. 2015). The Rural Metro pattern is a bank that (a) is providing sell-side advice to the target committee, (b) is simultaneously seeking to provide buy-side financing to the sponsor bidder, and (c) allows the buy-side-financing interest to influence its sell-side advice to the target committee.

*In re Del Monte Foods Company Shareholders Litigation*, 25 A.3d 813 (Del. Ch. 2011) and *In re El Paso Corp. Shareholder Litigation*, 41 A.3d 432 (Del. Ch. 2012) preceded Rural Metro and established the pattern: sell-side bankers with buy-side conflicts or personal conflicts (an El Paso banker who was personally invested in the buyer) drew significant Chancery scrutiny even where the underlying transactions were not enjoined.

The committee's discipline on banker-conflict:

- **Full disclosure of all recent past and current relationships with the counterparty.** The financial-advisor's engagement letter should include a detailed schedule of all recent engagements with the counterparty (typically 2 years back) with fees, roles, and scope.
- **Prohibition on buy-side financing without committee consent.** The engagement letter should prohibit the bank from providing financing (or other services) to the counterparty during the transaction without the committee's express consent.
- **Prohibition on stapled financing.** The bank should not be simultaneously offering buy-side financing (a "staple" package) to the counterparty. If the bank has a specific staple offer, the committee should consider engaging a separate independent-financial-advisor for the fairness opinion.
- **Real-time conflict monitoring.** The engagement letter should require the bank to disclose any new engagement or relationship with the counterparty during the transaction, in real time.
- **Full disclosure in the proxy.** The proxy discloses the bank's fee structure, the bank's recent-past and current relationships with the counterparty, any specific conflicts, and the committee's analysis of the conflicts.

Chapter 5 covers the banker-conflict framework in depth, including the specific disclosures and the practitioner discipline.

## The committee's recommendation — form and substance

The committee's ultimate work product is a **written recommendation** to the full board (and, through the proxy, to the stockholders). The recommendation is typically 5–15 pages and includes:

- A summary of the committee's mandate and composition.
- A summary of the committee's process — number of meetings, advisor engagement, negotiation history.
- The committee's substantive analysis of the transaction — the price, the terms, the alternatives considered, the risks.
- The financial advisor's fairness opinion and a summary of the underlying valuation analysis.
- The committee's specific reasons for its recommendation (or rejection).
- The committee's formal recommendation (or rejection).

The recommendation is drafted by the committee's counsel with committee input, is formally approved at a committee meeting, and is delivered to the full board. It becomes part of the proxy disclosure.

## The proxy-disclosure integration

The committee's process and recommendation must be fully described in the proxy. The specific proxy items:

- The composition of the committee (with independence rationale).
- The mandate resolution (typically summarised or attached).
- The committee's process — meeting cadence, advisor engagement, negotiation history.
- The financial advisor's fairness opinion (with the SEC-required disclosure about the opinion's assumptions, methodology, and limitations — chapter 6 covers this in depth).
- The specific banker-conflict disclosures — fee structure, prior relationships, current relationships.
- The alternatives considered and the reasons for the chosen transaction.
- The committee's recommendation.
- The reasons for the recommendation.
- Any dissenting committee views.

Material omissions defeat Corwin cleansing (chapter 1) and MFW cleansing (chapter 2). The disclosure discipline is a joint responsibility of the committee's counsel, the corporation's counsel, and the SEC-comment counsel.

## Committee compensation — the conflict-of-interest lens

Committee members are typically compensated for their work — a fixed fee (per meeting, per month, or lump-sum) separate from ordinary board fees. The compensation:

- Should be *fixed*, not contingent on transaction outcome. Contingent compensation creates a conflict of interest.
- Should be *reasonable*. Excessive compensation reads as buying the committee's approval. Modest compensation reflects the additional workload.
- Should be *approved by the full board* (not by the committee itself, which would be self-dealing).
- Should be *disclosed in the proxy*.

Typical special-committee compensation for a major transaction: $50,000–$250,000 total per member for a 2–6 month engagement, depending on complexity and duration.

## Summary

The special committee is the operating vehicle for MFW cleansing, controller-conflict Revlon process, interested-director transactions, and Rule 13e-3 going-privates. Composition requires independence (of the controller and interested directors), disinterestedness (no material personal-benefit in the specific transaction), and appropriate skill and expertise. The mandate resolution — the constituting document — must expressly grant authority to select and engage advisors, to negotiate directly, and to reject the transaction, without contingent-authority language. Independent legal counsel (Delaware-experienced) and independent financial advisor (with a documented banker-conflict analysis under Rural Metro / Del Monte / El Paso — chapter 5) are separately retained. Meeting cadence should reflect transaction complexity (10–20 meetings over 2–6 months for a major transaction), with executive sessions of the committee and its advisors only. Minute discipline is the primary evidentiary record — actual deliberation reflected, checklist entries avoided, contemporaneous notes minimised. The committee's written recommendation and the underlying process must be fully disclosed in the proxy to satisfy Corwin (chapter 1) and MFW (chapter 2). Committee compensation is fixed (not contingent), reasonable, board-approved, and disclosed.

Chapter 5 turns to the banker-conflict analysis under Rural Metro, Del Monte, and El Paso — the aiding-and-abetting-breach-of-fiduciary-duty exposure that the financial advisor carries, and the operational discipline for engagement-letter drafting and proxy disclosure.
