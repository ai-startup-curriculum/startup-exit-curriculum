# Banker Conflicts under Rural Metro, Del Monte, and El Paso

## Why this matters

Chapter 4 laid out the special-committee construction and identified the financial advisor as one of the committee's two independent-advisor engagements. This chapter goes deeper on the specific conflict-of-interest doctrine that shapes the financial-advisor selection, the engagement-letter drafting, and the proxy disclosure.

The Delaware Court of Chancery over 2011–2015 issued a trilogy of opinions — *In re Del Monte Foods Company Shareholders Litigation*, 25 A.3d 813 (Del. Ch. 2011); *In re El Paso Corp. Shareholder Litigation*, 41 A.3d 432 (Del. Ch. 2012); and *In re Rural Metro Corp. Stockholders Litigation*, 88 A.3d 54 (Del. Ch. 2014) (affirmed *RBC Capital Markets, LLC v. Jervis*, 129 A.3d 816 (Del. 2015)) — that transformed the sell-side banker's conflict-of-interest posture from a compliance-desk item to a personal-liability exposure. Rural Metro in particular held RBC Capital Markets liable for aiding-and-abetting-breach-of-fiduciary-duty and imposed a $75.8 million damages award — roughly twice the bank's fee on the transaction — because the bank had allowed its buy-side-financing interest in the sponsor bidder to distort the sell-side advice it provided to the target board.

The trilogy is now the anchor for how special committees select bankers, how engagement letters are drafted, how proxy disclosures are authored, and how boards diligence the specific banker they engage. A committee that has not internalised the trilogy is a committee that walks into a *post-close* aiding-and-abetting suit that survives motion to dismiss and produces a substantial damages verdict.

This chapter walks the three opinions, the specific factual patterns each attacked, the current-practice engagement-letter and disclosure discipline, and the specific decision framework for choosing between the sell-side banker and a separate independent-fairness-opinion provider.

## The doctrinal frame — aiding-and-abetting breach of fiduciary duty

The financial advisor is not itself a fiduciary of the target's stockholders (with limited exceptions). The advisor's exposure is derivative — the advisor can be liable for **aiding and abetting** a director's breach of fiduciary duty if the advisor (a) had actual knowledge of the breach and (b) provided substantial assistance to the breach. The doctrine is anchored in Delaware caselaw including *Malpiede v. Townson*, 780 A.2d 1075 (Del. 2001).

The aiding-and-abetting exposure has three practical components:

1. **A predicate director breach.** Some director's fiduciary duty must have been breached — typically a Revlon-process failure, a disclosure failure, an entire-fairness failure. The advisor's aiding-and-abetting exposure is derivative of this predicate breach.
2. **Actual knowledge.** The advisor knew (not should have known) that the directors were breaching their duties. Knowledge can be inferred from specific factual patterns.
3. **Substantial assistance.** The advisor's conduct materially contributed to the breach — typically by providing skewed advice, withholding material information from the board, or actively structuring the transaction to conceal the conflict.

The Rural Metro / RBC-Capital-Markets damages were $75.8 million — the bank's compensatory-damages share of the "fair value" difference between what stockholders received and what the court found the transaction should have delivered. This is *personal* firm-level exposure; the bank pays out of its P&L. It is a category of exposure that dwarfs the ordinary fee economics of a sell-side engagement.

## *Del Monte* (2011) — the single-bank-both-sides pattern

The Del Monte transaction was a leveraged buyout of Del Monte Foods by a Kohlberg Kravis Roberts (KKR)-led private-equity consortium at $19/share. Barclays Capital served as sell-side financial advisor to Del Monte's board — and, without disclosing the arrangement to Del Monte's board, Barclays also arranged buy-side acquisition financing for KKR and steered specific PE consortium members into the transaction. Barclays effectively played both sides of the negotiation while representing to Del Monte's board that it was the target's advisor.

Vice Chancellor Laster's opinion:

- Barclays's dual-role conduct created a substantial risk that the sell-side advice to Del Monte was distorted by Barclays's interest in the buy-side financing fee.
- Barclays had not obtained Del Monte's board consent to the buy-side role.
- The board was consequently uninformed about a material conflict affecting the process.
- The court enjoined the stockholder vote for 20 days to allow additional disclosure and additional market-check, and permitted the transaction to proceed with modest additional deal-protection concessions.

The *Del Monte* immediate practitioner takeaways:

- **Dual-role engagements require board consent.** A single bank cannot represent both the target and provide buy-side financing to the acquirer without express, informed board consent.
- **Disclosure of the dual-role is essential.** The proxy must fully describe the bank's roles on both sides.
- **The banker's incentive skew must be evaluated in the process.** The board (through the special committee's counsel) must analyse how the bank's buy-side interest could have distorted the advice, and either add process safeguards or engage a separate advisor.

Del Monte alone did not produce a massive damages award, but it established the doctrinal frame that was extended in El Paso and Rural Metro.

## *El Paso* (2012) — the personal-conflict pattern

The El Paso Corporation transaction was a proposed sale of El Paso to Kinder Morgan Inc. at approximately $26/share. Goldman Sachs served as El Paso's sell-side financial advisor. Two specific conflicts emerged:

1. Goldman held approximately $4 billion in Kinder Morgan common stock across its investment funds — a substantial ownership interest in the acquirer.
2. The specific Goldman banker leading the El Paso engagement — Steven Daniel — personally owned approximately $340,000 in Kinder Morgan stock, an ownership interest he did not disclose to El Paso's board.

To manage the firm-level conflict, El Paso's board engaged Morgan Stanley as an independent second-opinion advisor. But Morgan Stanley's engagement was contingent on the Kinder Morgan transaction closing — if the transaction failed, Morgan Stanley received no fee. The court found this fee structure created an incentive for Morgan Stanley to support the transaction, undermining its "independent" status.

Chancellor Strine's opinion:

- The firm-level Goldman conflict was material.
- The personal Daniel conflict — undisclosed to El Paso's board — was extraordinary and disqualifying.
- The Morgan Stanley engagement's contingent-fee structure undermined its independence.
- The board's process was consequently compromised.
- The court declined to enjoin the transaction (stockholders had approved and the price was arguably reasonable) but wrote a scathing opinion that put the banker-conflict doctrine on notice.

The *El Paso* practitioner takeaways:

- **Firm-level bank-holdings of the acquirer's stock are material conflicts** and must be disclosed and analysed. Investment banks' proprietary trading desks and asset-management arms often have substantial holdings in transaction counterparties; the sell-side engagement should include a specific conflict analysis for material holdings.
- **Individual banker personal ownership of the acquirer is disqualifying.** The specific banker leading the engagement cannot have personal ownership in the acquirer.
- **"Independent second-opinion" advisors must have genuinely independent fee structures.** Contingent-fee arrangements that pay only on transaction close create incentive skew.
- **Committee-level advisor engagement is critical.** El Paso did not have a special committee independently engaging Morgan Stanley — the corporation engaged Morgan Stanley as a "supplemental" advisor. A special-committee-driven independent-advisor engagement, with a non-contingent fee, would have insulated the board better.

## *Rural Metro* (2014) — the stapled-financing pattern

Rural Metro was a private-equity buyout of Rural/Metro Corp. by Warburg Pincus at $17.25/share. RBC Capital Markets served as Rural Metro's sell-side financial advisor. RBC pursued (without disclosing to the Rural Metro board) two specific parallel objectives:

1. RBC sought to provide buy-side acquisition financing to Warburg Pincus — a "staple" financing package that would generate a substantial fee for RBC if Warburg won the auction.
2. RBC sought to use the Rural Metro engagement as a "loss leader" to secure future PE-consortium business.

RBC's sell-side conduct was accordingly skewed:

- RBC understated other bidder interest to the Rural Metro board.
- RBC accelerated the process to prevent alternative bidders from developing.
- RBC's valuation analysis included discount-rate assumptions that were skewed to support a lower price (favouring Warburg's bid).
- RBC did not disclose its parallel buy-side pitching to Warburg.

Vice Chancellor Laster's opinion (and the Delaware Supreme Court's affirmance):

- The Rural Metro directors breached their duty of care by relying on RBC's advice without probing the conflicts. The directors were consequently exposed to entire-fairness review.
- RBC had actual knowledge that its advice to the board was compromised and that the directors were consequently proceeding on an inadequately-informed basis.
- RBC's conduct constituted substantial assistance to the directors' breach.
- RBC was liable for aiding and abetting the breach.
- Damages: $75.8 million (RBC's proportional share of the "fair value" delta the court found stockholders were shortchanged).

The *Rural Metro* practitioner takeaways are the doctrinal capstone:

- **Stapled financing arrangements — where the sell-side bank simultaneously offers buy-side financing to the sponsor — are the archetypal conflict pattern.** They are not automatically disqualifying but they must be disclosed, analysed, and mitigated (often by engaging a separate independent-fairness-opinion provider).
- **Board reliance on skewed advice is a duty-of-care breach.** The board (and the special committee) must probe the bank's conflicts and cannot passively defer to bank-provided analysis where the bank has an undisclosed skew-generating interest.
- **The bank's conduct in accelerating or manipulating the process** — not just the disclosure failure — is aidng-and-abetting-actionable.
- **The valuation-methodology choices themselves** — discount rates, terminal-value assumptions, comparable-transaction selection — are examined for conflict-driven skew.

## The current-practice engagement-letter discipline

The special-committee's financial-advisor engagement letter — negotiated by the committee's independent legal counsel, in coordination with the bank's counsel — should include specific banker-conflict provisions:

**Conflicts disclosure at engagement.** The bank represents that it has disclosed all material engagements, relationships, and interests with the counterparty and its affiliates in the recent past (typically 2 years) and currently. The bank represents that no specific personnel on the engagement have personal ownership interests in the counterparty (with any exceptions specifically disclosed). The bank represents that no specific stapled-financing or other buy-side arrangements are in place with the counterparty at the time of engagement.

**No buy-side engagement without committee consent.** During the transaction, the bank commits not to provide financing, advisory services, or other engagement to the counterparty without the express prior written consent of the committee. Any new relationship that emerges during the transaction must be disclosed to the committee immediately.

**Real-time conflict updates.** The bank commits to update its conflict disclosures in real time as new relationships or engagements arise.

**Fairness-opinion fee de-linked from close.** If the bank will deliver the fairness opinion, the fairness-opinion fee should be a *non-contingent* fee — earned upon delivery of the opinion regardless of whether the transaction closes. The transaction-close fee (the "success fee") should be structured separately and disclosed transparently.

**Fee-cap or specific-fee structure.** The engagement letter specifies the fee (base retainer, milestone fees, success fee at close, and any specific incremental fees). Success fees materially larger than the fairness-opinion fee are common but should be sized in proportion to the transaction and disclosed.

**Absent-conflict-consent limits on future engagement.** The engagement letter may include specific "tail" provisions — the bank commits not to engage on the counterparty side of any related transaction for a specified period post-close without committee (or its successor) consent.

**Reservation of committee's right to disengage.** The committee retains the unconditional right to terminate the engagement if a conflict emerges, without penalty beyond payment for services rendered.

**Fee-disclosure obligation.** The bank consents to complete fee disclosure in the proxy.

## The proxy-disclosure discipline

The specific banker-conflict disclosures required in the proxy:

**Aggregate fees.** The bank's total fees for the engagement — including retainer, milestone fees, success fee, and expense reimbursement — with the specific components separated.

**Fee structure and conditions.** Whether the success fee is contingent on close (typical) and the specific milestone or achievement triggering each fee component.

**Recent-past relationships.** A specific list of the bank's engagements with the counterparty (and its affiliates) in the recent past (typically 2 years), with the specific fees, roles, and scopes.

**Current relationships.** All ongoing engagements with the counterparty (and its affiliates) at the time of the transaction.

**Firm-level holdings.** Material firm-level ownership interests in the counterparty (through investment-management arms, proprietary trading positions, or other channels).

**Individual banker interests.** Any personal ownership or specific interest of the individual bankers on the engagement.

**Stapled-financing or buy-side arrangements.** Any offers or engagements the bank has extended (or is considering extending) to the counterparty for buy-side financing, advisory services, or other engagement related to the transaction.

**Committee's conflict analysis.** A summary of the committee's analysis of the bank's conflicts and the specific mitigations or safeguards put in place.

**Reasons for retaining despite conflicts.** If material conflicts exist, the committee's specific reasons for retaining the bank notwithstanding the conflicts.

The proxy disclosure is drafted by the committee's counsel with financial-advisor input and reviewed by SEC-comment counsel and (as appropriate) SEC staff during the proxy-review process. Material omissions in the banker-conflict disclosure defeat Corwin cleansing (chapter 1) and MFW cleansing (chapter 2) and expose the transaction to enhanced scrutiny.

## The single-banker vs. separate-fairness-opinion decision

The special committee faces a specific decision when the primary sell-side bank has material conflicts: does the committee engage the same bank for both sell-side advisory *and* the fairness opinion, or does the committee engage a separate independent-fairness-opinion provider?

**Arguments for single-banker.** Cost efficiency (one engagement, one fee), engagement-continuity (the sell-side bank already knows the company deeply), market convention (this is the modal structure for many transactions).

**Arguments for separate fairness-opinion.** The fairness opinion is insulated from the sell-side bank's conflicts. The proxy disclosure is stronger. The committee's litigation posture is materially improved. The cost is a second engagement fee (typically $500K–$2M+ for a major transaction depending on complexity).

The specific circumstances that push toward separate fairness-opinion:

- The sell-side bank has stapled financing or is actively pitching buy-side financing to the counterparty.
- The sell-side bank has material firm-level holdings of the counterparty.
- The transaction is high-profile or contentious (a controller take-private, a strategic sale with rival-bidder litigation risk).
- The transaction has a specific characteristic (e.g., Rule 13e-3 going-private) that carries a specific "fairness" disclosure obligation.
- The special committee's independent counsel recommends a separate fairness opinion for the litigation-defence posture.

Specific firms with strong independent-fairness-opinion franchises include Duff & Phelps (now Kroll), Houlihan Lokey, Stout Risius Ross, Valuation Research Corp., Mesirow Financial Consulting, and the M&A-advisory boutiques (Evercore, Lazard, Moelis, Guggenheim, Centerview, PJT Partners, Perella Weinberg) when engaged specifically as second-opinion providers. Duff & Phelps / Kroll in particular has built a substantial franchise as the "clean-fairness-opinion" provider for controller take-privates and other conflict transactions.

## The board's discipline — reading the banker

The committee's discipline in reading banker advice through the conflict lens:

- **Probe the bank's analysis with independent perspective.** The committee's counsel should engage the committee in specific probing of the bank's valuation methodology — the discount rates, the terminal-value assumptions, the comparable-transaction selection, the sensitivity ranges. Ask why specific choices were made. Ask what alternative choices would produce.
- **Compare against independent references.** The committee's counsel can commission an independent valuation reference (an accounting firm's valuation practice, an independent-fairness firm) to test specific banker inputs.
- **Test the bank's characterisation of market interest.** In a Revlon process, the bank characterises the level of alternative-bidder interest. The committee should probe — with specific questions — what actual conversations occurred, what specific bidders were contacted, what specific responses were received.
- **Read the bank's incentive structure.** A bank with a substantial success fee will have specific incentives; the committee should read the bank's recommendations with the incentive lens active.
- **Ensure the paper trail.** The committee's counsel should document each meeting's substantive discussion with the bank — including specific committee questions and bank responses — so that any subsequent litigation shows the committee did the diligence.

## Aiding-and-abetting exposure for lawyers and other advisors

The Rural Metro doctrine principally attacks financial advisors, but the aiding-and-abetting framework applies to *any* advisor providing substantial assistance to a director breach with actual knowledge. Delaware caselaw has examined:

- **Legal counsel.** Legal counsel that provided advice inconsistent with fiduciary duty or that concealed conflicts can be exposed. The specific exposure is comparatively rare (legal privilege and the attorney-as-fiduciary-of-court framework provide additional insulation) but not absent.
- **Accountants and auditors.** Auditors that provided misleading opinions or facilitated fraud have specific exposures.
- **Consulting firms.** Strategic consultants that recommended transactions with undisclosed conflicts have been analysed.

The practitioner discipline: all advisors on a transaction should be subject to a conflict-analysis and disclosure regime commensurate with the specific advisor's role.

## Post-close litigation pattern — the plaintiff-firm response

Every announced sale draws an immediate strike-suit response from plaintiff firms (WeissLaw, Halper Sadeh, Brodsky Smith, Kaskela, Monteverde & Associates, Rigrodsky Law, Bragar Eagel & Squire, and others). The strike-suit pattern:

- **Immediate press release.** Within 24–72 hours of transaction announcement, plaintiff firms issue press releases inviting stockholders to contact the firm about "investigating" the transaction.
- **Section 14(a) disclosure complaint.** A complaint is filed alleging deficient proxy disclosure — usually including specific claims about banker-conflict disclosure, fairness-opinion methodology, and process disclosure.
- **Rapid settlement or dismissal.** The complaint typically settles for a "mootness fee" to plaintiff's counsel (a specific pattern the Delaware court largely killed with *In re Trulia S'holder Litig.*, 129 A.3d 884 (Del. Ch. 2016) — but which continues in federal courts and other state courts) or is dismissed following supplemental disclosure.
- **Post-close aiding-and-abetting suit.** In a smaller number of cases, plaintiff counsel pursues a substantive post-close suit alleging director breach and aiding-and-abetting by advisors. These are the Rural Metro / Del Monte-descendant cases that survive to trial in the material minority.

## Summary

The Delaware Chancery trilogy — *Del Monte* (2011), *El Paso* (2012), and *Rural Metro* (2014) — transformed sell-side banker conflict-of-interest from a compliance-desk issue into an aiding-and-abetting-breach-of-fiduciary-duty exposure with material damages potential. Del Monte attacked the undisclosed dual-role pattern (single bank both sides); El Paso attacked firm-level and individual banker personal-conflict patterns (undisclosed Kinder Morgan holdings) and contingent-fee "independent second-opinion" arrangements; Rural Metro attacked the stapled-financing pattern (RBC parallel-pitching Warburg while advising the target) and produced a $75.8M aiding-and-abetting damages award. Current practice: engagement letters include comprehensive conflict-disclosure representations, no-buy-side-engagement-without-consent commitments, real-time conflict updates, non-contingent fairness-opinion fees, and reservation of committee's right to disengage. Proxy disclosures include aggregate fees, fee structure, recent-past and current relationships, firm-level holdings, individual banker interests, stapled-financing arrangements, and the committee's conflict analysis. The single-banker vs. separate-fairness-opinion decision turns on conflict severity, transaction profile, and litigation-defence posture, with Duff & Phelps / Kroll, Houlihan Lokey, and specialised M&A boutiques the modal separate-fairness-opinion providers. The board's discipline in reading banker advice through the conflict lens — probing methodology, testing market-interest characterisations, and documenting the paper trail — is what earns the deferential litigation-defence posture.
