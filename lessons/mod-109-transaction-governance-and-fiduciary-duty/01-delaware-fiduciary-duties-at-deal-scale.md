# Delaware Fiduciary Duties at Deal-Scale — BJR, Van Gorkom, Revlon, Unocal, Weinberger, Corwin

## Why this matters

The Delaware Court of Chancery is the venue where roughly two-thirds of large-cap M&A deals get tested when a stockholder plaintiff or a strike-suit firm challenges them. The court reads the same deal three different ways depending on which **standard of review** applies. Under the **business-judgement rule** the court will not second-guess a board decision absent fraud, illegality, or self-dealing — the practical effect is dismissal on the pleadings. Under **enhanced scrutiny** (Revlon or Unocal) the court probes the process — was the board's decision reasonable in the specific market context, was the sale process reasonably designed to secure the best price, was the defensive tactic proportional to the threat. Under **entire fairness** the court audits both the process (fair dealing) and the price (fair price), with the burden on the *defendants* to prove fairness — and the practical effect is a trial, damages exposure, and a well-known appetite for finding process defects.

The distance between the three standards is the difference between "you win on a motion to dismiss and the case closes in nine months for $2M in defence costs" and "you lose at trial and the court orders a $250M damages payment three years later." The board that understands which standard applies to its transaction, and configures the process to earn the most deferential standard available, saves the deal and the company. The board that treats fiduciary duty as a legal-notice-on-the-agenda item ends up with a deal that gets challenged and a legal spend that eats the transaction premium.

This chapter walks the standards-of-review ladder and the specific case that anchors each rung. The subsequent chapters install the operational overlays — MFW (chapter 2), Blasius (chapter 3), special-committee construction (chapter 4), banker conflicts (chapter 5), fairness opinions (chapter 6), appraisal (chapter 7), proxy-advisor engagement and litigation defence (chapter 8) — that let the board actually reach the standard it needs.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal advice. Every case citation below points to a Delaware opinion that a corporate lawyer will read directly before advising on your transaction. The doctrine evolves — a formulation that reads one way in a 2015 opinion may have been refined by a 2023 Chancery decision or a 2024 Supreme Court affirmance. Verify against the current caselaw before relying on any specific framing.

## The two fiduciary duties — care and loyalty

Delaware corporate law imposes two overlapping fiduciary duties on directors: the **duty of care** (to act on an informed basis, in good faith, and with the honest belief that the action taken is in the best interests of the corporation) and the **duty of loyalty** (to act in the best interests of the corporation and its stockholders, not in the director's own interest or the interest of any other person or entity). There is a third concept, the **duty of good faith**, which the Delaware Supreme Court in *Stone v. Ritter*, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006) treated as a sub-component of the duty of loyalty rather than as an independent duty. For deal-scale purposes, the operating vocabulary is *care* and *loyalty*, with good-faith as the loyalty-adjacent concept that governs "conscious disregard" and *Caremark* oversight-failure claims.

The duty of care can be **exculpated** by a charter provision under **DGCL §102(b)(7)** — every Delaware corporation charter now includes a §102(b)(7) exculpation clause that eliminates personal monetary liability of directors for breach of the duty of care (but not the duty of loyalty, not bad faith, not intentional misconduct, not improper personal benefit, and — under the 2022 §102(b)(7) amendment — not for officers other than in connection with a claim brought by or in the right of the corporation). The 2022 amendment extends §102(b)(7) to specified corporate officers with a narrower scope than for directors. The upshot: a director sued for breach of the duty of care under a properly-adopted §102(b)(7) charter is usually protected from personal monetary liability, but the corporation is still exposed to injunctive relief, and duty-of-loyalty and bad-faith claims survive §102(b)(7).

The duty of loyalty is the one that matters for deal-scale governance. It is what triggers entire-fairness review when a director is on both sides of a transaction, when the board has a materially conflicting interest, or when a controlling stockholder is on the other side of the deal.

## Rung 1 — the business-judgement rule

The **business-judgement rule** is the deferential presumption that "in making a business decision the directors of a corporation acted on an informed basis, in good faith and in the honest belief that the action taken was in the best interests of the company." *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805, 812 (Del. 1984). The rule is a presumption in favour of the board; the plaintiff must plead specific facts sufficient to rebut it. When the presumption holds, the court will not second-guess the board's decision — the standard is essentially waste, which is nearly impossible to prove where the board received value in the transaction.

The BJR is the default rule. It applies to ordinary-course board decisions and to arms-length transactions where the board has no conflict, no controller is on the other side, and there is no change of control triggering Revlon.

The rule can be **rebutted** by pleading that the board (a) was uninformed (a duty-of-care breach — *Van Gorkom*), (b) was interested or dominated (a duty-of-loyalty breach), (c) acted in bad faith, or (d) engaged in a self-dealing transaction (again a loyalty breach). If rebutted, the standard shifts to entire fairness.

## Rung 2 — Van Gorkom and the duty of informed decision

*Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985) is the case that made "informed decision" a board-facing operational discipline rather than a paper phrase. The Trans Union board approved a $55/share cash-out merger with the Marmon Group after a two-hour meeting, on the basis of a 20-minute oral presentation by the CEO, without a written fairness opinion, without a written report from the CFO, and without documents in advance of the meeting. The Delaware Supreme Court held that the directors had breached their duty of care by failing to inform themselves of "all information reasonably available" — including the specific process by which the $55 price was derived and the intrinsic value of the company. The court declined to apply the business-judgement rule and remanded for a damages calculation.

The immediate corporate-governance response was two-fold: (1) the Delaware legislature enacted §102(b)(7) in 1986 to permit charter-based exculpation of duty-of-care violations, and (2) every board began to build the specific procedural discipline that *Van Gorkom* had signalled was required — advance materials, meaningful meeting time, financial-advisor engagement, fairness opinion, written record.

The **Van Gorkom checklist** as it operates now in a large-cap sale-of-company:

- Written materials distributed to directors sufficiently in advance of the meeting (typically 24–72 hours minimum, longer for complex transactions) to permit meaningful review.
- A financial advisor engaged early enough to have run a meaningful valuation analysis before the decision meeting.
- A written fairness opinion delivered before the vote (or, at minimum, an oral opinion with the written version to follow, which was a specific *Van Gorkom* fact pattern).
- Meaningful meeting time — a several-hour meeting for a bet-the-company transaction, not a 30-minute agenda item.
- Executive session with financial advisor and outside counsel, without management present, at least once during the process.
- Documented consideration of alternatives to the transaction — remain independent, alternative buyer, alternative structure.
- Minute-book entries reflecting the deliberation, not merely the vote.

The *Van Gorkom* discipline is what special-committee counsel operationalises when structuring the committee's calendar. Chapter 4 covers this operationally; chapter 6 covers the fairness-opinion piece.

## Rung 3 — Revlon and the change-of-control trigger

*Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.*, 506 A.2d 173 (Del. 1986) is the case that installed the "auctioneer" duty — when a board has determined that a sale of the company is inevitable (or when a change of control is on the table), the directors' fiduciary duty shifts from preservation of the corporate entity to obtaining the "best price reasonably available" for the stockholders. The Revlon board had entered into a lock-up with a friendly bidder that foreclosed a competing higher bid; the court enjoined the lock-up because it interfered with the auctioneer duty.

The **Revlon trigger** — when is the duty triggered? The Delaware Supreme Court in *Paramount Communications, Inc. v. QVC Network, Inc.*, 637 A.2d 34 (Del. 1994) clarified the three specific scenarios:

1. A cash sale of the company (or predominantly-cash consideration).
2. A break-up of the company.
3. A transaction that results in a change of control — meaning a change from a fluid, aggregated public-market stockholder base to a specific controlling stockholder (or a specific control group), even if the consideration is stock. QVC involved a stock deal where the acquirer had a controlling stockholder, and the court held that Revlon applied because the target's stockholders would go from a fluid public market to a specific control block.

The Revlon trigger *does not* apply to a stock-for-stock merger of equals where control remains in a fluid public-market aggregation post-close (the *Paramount v. Time* pattern, *Paramount Communications, Inc. v. Time Inc.*, 571 A.2d 1140 (Del. 1990)) — Time's stock-for-stock combination with Warner did not trigger Revlon because the combined entity remained widely held.

**What Revlon requires — reasonable process, not maximum price.** Revlon does not require the board to conduct an auction, and does not require the board to achieve the theoretical highest price. It requires a *reasonable* process reasonably designed to obtain the best price reasonably available. *Barkan v. Amsted Industries, Inc.*, 567 A.2d 1279 (Del. 1989) confirmed the "no single blueprint" formulation — a targeted pre-signing market check, a post-signing go-shop, a negotiated single-bidder process with impact-informed diligence and a robust fiduciary-out — any of these can satisfy Revlon depending on the specific facts.

*Lyondell Chemical Co. v. Ryan*, 970 A.2d 235 (Del. 2009) was the Delaware Supreme Court's clarification that a Revlon breach requires a "conscious disregard" of the duties — a good-faith-but-suboptimal process is not a Revlon breach. Lyondell entered into a merger agreement after seven days of negotiation, without a pre-signing market check, with a modest termination fee and a strong fiduciary-out; the court held that the process, while not the textbook auction, did not rise to a "utter failure" bad-faith standard and was defensible.

The **Revlon deal-protection package** — the specific negotiated terms that must be Revlon-compliant when a change-of-control trigger applies:

- **No-shop with fiduciary out.** The target commits not to solicit competing bids, but retains the right to consider a superior unsolicited bid if the board's fiduciary duty requires. The fiduciary-out is essential; a no-shop without a fiduciary-out is per-se problematic under Revlon.
- **Matching rights.** The first bidder gets a specific window (typically 3–5 business days) to match a superior bid before the target can accept the superior bid.
- **Termination fee.** A break-up fee payable if the target terminates for a superior proposal. Termination fees in the 2–4% of equity value range are conventional and rarely challenged; fees at 5%+ start drawing Revlon scrutiny; fees combined with expense-reimbursement provisions that push the effective break economics higher are worth extra care. *In re Toys "R" Us S'holder Litig.*, 877 A.2d 975 (Del. Ch. 2005) upheld a 3.75% termination fee; *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. Cyprus Amax Minerals Co.*, C.A. No. 17398, 1999 WL 1054255 (Del. Ch. Sept. 27, 1999) is one of many that pushed back on preclusive deal-protection packages.
- **Go-shop.** A specific post-signing window (typically 30–50 days) during which the target can actively solicit superior bids, often with reduced-percentage termination fee for a superior-bid emerging from the go-shop process. Go-shops are common in private-equity take-privates where the pre-signing market check was constrained by confidentiality concerns.
- **Voting agreements.** Commitments from specific significant stockholders (typically the private-equity investors on the board) to vote for the transaction. Voting agreements are generally permissible but should not, in combination with other protections, become preclusive.

The **QVC "enhanced scrutiny" formulation** is the current articulation of the Revlon standard — the court probes whether the board's decision was reasonable in the context, whether the process was reasonably designed to secure the best available consideration for stockholders, and whether the board acted in good faith. It is more searching than BJR but less punishing than entire fairness.

## Rung 4 — Unocal and the defensive-tactic proportionality test

*Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co.*, 493 A.2d 946 (Del. 1985) applies when the board adopts a defensive tactic in response to a hostile bid — a poison pill (a shareholder rights plan), a self-tender, a white-knight lock-up, a bylaw amendment that impedes the hostile acquirer. Unocal is the *pre-Revlon* enhanced-scrutiny standard — it applies when the board is trying to *preserve* the company against a hostile bid, not when the board has already committed to sell.

The **Unocal two-part test**:

1. The board must show reasonable grounds for believing a threat to the corporation existed. The "threat" concept is broad — inadequate price, structural coercion, disruption of a specific corporate strategy — and the court gives the board substantial latitude if the board is composed of a majority of independent directors and relied on advice from a financial advisor.
2. The defensive response must be reasonable in relation to the threat posed. A response that is "draconian" — meaning coercive or preclusive — fails Unocal. *Unitrin, Inc. v. American General Corp.*, 651 A.2d 1361 (Del. 1995) refined the standard as a two-part sub-test: the response is preclusive if it makes a bidder's ability to win a proxy contest either mathematically impossible or realistically unattainable; the response is coercive if it forces stockholders' hands.

The **poison pill** — the shareholder rights plan (SRP) — is the archetypal Unocal-cleared defensive tactic. First upheld in *Moran v. Household International, Inc.*, 500 A.2d 1346 (Del. 1985), the pill is now standard corporate machinery. The typical pill has a 10–20% ownership trigger (2020-era pandemic pills often set at 5–10% to guard against opportunistic accumulation), a two-tier structure (flip-in and flip-out), and a redemption feature that lets the board pull the pill in exchange for a superior transaction. *Air Products & Chemicals, Inc. v. Airgas, Inc.*, 16 A.3d 48 (Del. Ch. 2011) is the seminal opinion upholding a "just say no" pill against a hostile bidder, and remains the anchor for the current formulation.

The **poison-pill / Revlon interaction** — when the board pulls the pill in favour of a friendly transaction, Revlon may be triggered depending on the transaction consideration; the pill itself does not trigger Revlon, but the friendly transaction consideration does.

## Rung 5 — Weinberger and entire fairness

*Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983) is the anchor for the **entire-fairness** standard that applies to interested-director transactions and controller-conflict transactions. The Signal Companies (the controlling stockholder of UOP) squeezed out UOP's minority stockholders in a cash-out merger at $21/share; the court held that the merger was subject to entire-fairness review because Signal was on both sides of the deal.

The entire-fairness test has two prongs:

1. **Fair dealing.** The process — how the transaction was initiated, structured, negotiated, disclosed, and approved. Signal's process failed because a Signal-employee director had prepared a confidential feasibility study for Signal that identified a fair price range up to $24/share, and that study was not disclosed to the UOP board or to the minority stockholders. The concealment was the fair-dealing failure.
2. **Fair price.** The economic and financial considerations, including all relevant factors — asset value, market value, earnings, future prospects, and any other factor that affects intrinsic or inherent value. The court explicitly rejected the "Delaware block method" that had previously dominated appraisal valuation, in favour of a modern DCF-based valuation methodology.

Under entire fairness, the **burden is on the defendants** (the interested directors, the controller, the aiding-and-abetting parties) to prove that the transaction was entirely fair. This is a substantial burden shift from the plaintiff-bearing burden under the business-judgement rule.

**Ways to shift or discharge the entire-fairness burden.** The court in *Kahn v. Tremont Corp.*, 694 A.2d 422 (Del. 1997) and later cases identified two mechanisms that shift the burden of proof (from the defendants to the plaintiff) while retaining entire-fairness review: (a) approval by a well-functioning special committee of disinterested directors, and (b) approval by an informed majority of the minority stockholders. Either mechanism, satisfied independently, shifts the burden of proof to the plaintiff but does not restore business-judgement review. Chapter 2's MFW framework requires *both* mechanisms plus specific additional conditions, and yields the further step of restoring BJR.

The entire-fairness standard is intensely fact-specific. Defendants who satisfy it prevail; defendants who miss on either prong lose. It is the standard the board most wants to *avoid* by structuring the transaction to earn either BJR (via Corwin or MFW cleansing) or, at minimum, the burden-shifted version of entire fairness (via a well-functioning special committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote).

## Rung 6 — Corwin and the business-judgement cleansing

*Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings LLC*, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015) is the case that installed the current stockholder-vote cleansing doctrine. The Delaware Supreme Court held that a fully-informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders in favour of a merger transaction has the effect of restoring the business-judgement rule — even in a transaction that would otherwise be reviewed under enhanced scrutiny (Revlon) or entire fairness — provided the transaction does not involve a conflicted controlling stockholder.

The **Corwin conditions**:

1. **Fully informed.** The proxy discloses all material information required for the stockholders to cast an informed vote. Material omissions or misstatements defeat the cleansing. *In re Volcano Corp. S'holder Litig.*, 143 A.3d 727 (Del. Ch. 2016) extended Corwin cleansing to tender-offer contexts, treating a majority-tender as functionally equivalent to a majority stockholder vote.
2. **Uncoerced.** The vote is not structurally coercive. A vote conditioned on continued employment, a vote where the alternative is bankruptcy, a vote where the timing or disclosure structure impairs deliberation — these can be coercive.
3. **Disinterested majority.** The vote is by a majority of the shares held by disinterested stockholders. In practice this is "a majority of the shares voted excluding those held by the controller or the interested directors" — the majority-of-minority mechanism.
4. **No conflicted controller.** Corwin does *not* cleanse a transaction with a conflicted controlling stockholder — that is the MFW territory covered in chapter 2. Corwin is the cleansing doctrine for third-party arms-length transactions and for transactions where no controller is present.

**Corwin practice.** Once the disclosure is disciplined and the majority-of-the-disinterested-stockholder vote is achieved, the case gets dismissed on a motion to dismiss in the vast majority of instances — this is the *In re Volcano* / *Singh v. Attenborough* / *Corwin* pipeline. The strike-suit bar knows this, and has shifted much of its post-Corwin activity to (a) plaintiff-firm disclosure claims filed before the vote to hold up the deal and extract a "mootness fee," and (b) appraisal claims that Corwin does not cleanse (chapter 7). *In re Trulia, Inc. S'holder Litig.*, 129 A.3d 884 (Del. Ch. 2016) largely killed the disclosure-only settlement in Delaware, but the strike-suit bar has migrated the pattern to federal court and to specific state courts. Chapter 8 covers the litigation-defence overlay.

**Corwin and Revlon in one deal.** A typical arms-length cash sale of a widely-held Delaware corporation to a third-party strategic or PE buyer looks like this: the sale triggers Revlon, so the board runs a Revlon-compliant process (market check or negotiated single-bidder with fiduciary-out, robust deal protection, fairness opinion, disclosed process). The stockholder vote is a majority-of-the-disinterested-stockholder vote on a fully-informed proxy. If the disclosure is clean and the vote passes, Corwin applies at the motion-to-dismiss stage and dismisses the Revlon claim under BJR. This is the current default architecture for a large-cap arms-length sale.

## Reading the ladder — which standard applies to my transaction?

The practitioner shorthand for reading which standard applies:

- **No conflict, no change of control, no defensive tactic** → BJR. Ordinary-course transaction. Dismissed on motion.
- **Change of control (cash, break-up, or QVC-type stock-and-control-shift) with no controller conflict** → Revlon enhanced scrutiny. Compliance via reasonable process, fiduciary-out, fairness opinion. Corwin-cleanse via fully-informed disinterested-stockholder vote to restore BJR.
- **Defensive tactic in response to hostile bid** → Unocal proportionality. Compliance via reasonable-threat determination and proportional response.
- **Interested director, self-dealing, or a controller on the other side** → Entire fairness. Compliance via MFW dual protection (chapter 2) to restore BJR; or via well-functioning special committee and majority-of-minority vote to shift burden while retaining entire fairness.
- **Board action that impairs a stockholder vote** → Blasius compelling-justification (chapter 3).

The board's job — in coordination with GC and outside counsel — is to *diagnose the standard early* (typically at the first board meeting where the transaction is discussed) and to *configure the process to earn the most deferential standard available*. A conflict-transaction board that structures the process to satisfy MFW ab initio earns BJR; a conflict-transaction board that discovers the MFW requirements late in the process is stuck with burden-shifted entire fairness at best.

## Summary

The Delaware fiduciary framework at deal-scale is a ladder of standards of review that turns on the specific facts of the transaction. The business-judgement rule is the deferential presumption that applies to ordinary-course arms-length transactions with no conflict, no controller, no change of control, and no defensive tactic. *Van Gorkom* installed the procedural discipline — informed decision, advance materials, meaningful meeting time, written fairness opinion — that operationalises the duty of care and gives the board the record to earn the presumption. *Revlon* triggers enhanced scrutiny when the board decides to sell the company, break up, or enter a transaction that shifts control from a fluid public market to a specific control block; the board's duty shifts to the "auctioneer" pursuit of the best price reasonably available, satisfied through a *reasonable* process (not a required auction) with fiduciary-out, matching rights, and market-standard termination fees. *Unocal* governs defensive tactics in response to a hostile bid; the board must show a reasonable threat and a proportional response, with the poison pill the archetypal cleared tactic. *Weinberger* installs entire fairness for interested-director and controller-conflict transactions; the burden is on the defendants to prove fair dealing and fair price. *Corwin* restores the business-judgement rule when a fully-informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested-stockholder majority approves a transaction, provided no conflicted controller is present. §102(b)(7) charter exculpation neutralises duty-of-care monetary liability but not duty-of-loyalty exposure or entire-fairness review.

The practitioner discipline is *early diagnosis of the applicable standard* and *process configuration to earn the most deferential standard available*. Chapter 2 turns to the MFW framework — the six-condition test for controller-conflict transactions that restores BJR to a class of deal that would otherwise sit in entire fairness.
