# MFW Dual Protection and Controller-Conflict Transactions

## Why this matters

Chapter 1 ended at the entire-fairness / Corwin fork. Corwin restores business-judgement review for arms-length transactions with a fully-informed disinterested-stockholder vote. But Corwin explicitly *does not* cleanse a transaction where a conflicted **controlling stockholder** sits on the other side of the deal. That is a large and growing class of transactions — the VC-backed take-private, the founder-led going-private, the controller-parent squeeze-out, the majority-financial-sponsor consolidation, the recapitalisation that benefits the controller more than the minority. Any of these lands in entire fairness by default, with the burden on the defendants to prove fair dealing and fair price — a hard, expensive, unpredictable posture.

*Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014) — universally referred to as **MFW** — installed a specific six-condition cleansing framework that, when satisfied *from the outset* of the transaction, restores the business-judgement rule to controller-conflict transactions and thereby collapses the case on the pleadings the same way Corwin does for arms-length deals. The framework is powerful — MFW-cleansed deals routinely get dismissed on motion where entire-fairness deals go to trial — but the conditions are technical and the *ab initio* timing rule is unforgiving. A board that misses one condition, or that satisfies them only partway through the process, is stuck with entire-fairness review no matter how process-disciplined the rest of the transaction was.

This chapter walks the six MFW conditions, the *ab initio* timing rule, the specific case-law evolution since 2014, and the specific operating discipline that GC / special-committee counsel / independent legal advisor apply to earn the cleansing. Chapter 4 covers the special-committee construction in operational depth; this chapter covers the doctrinal frame.

> Reminder: MFW is intensely fact-specific and the Court of Chancery has narrowed and clarified the conditions in a number of post-2014 opinions. Verify against current counsel before relying on any specific formulation.

## The controller-conflict problem

Delaware treats a **controlling stockholder** as a fiduciary of the corporation for purposes of a transaction where the controller stands on both sides. "Controlling" is a facts-and-circumstances concept, not a bright-line ownership threshold. A stockholder is a controller if it (a) owns a majority of the voting stock, or (b) owns less than a majority but exercises actual control over corporate affairs — through board composition, contractual rights, voting agreements, or specific-transaction-level dominance. The *In re Tesla Motors Stockholder Litigation*, 2022 WL 1237185 (Del. Ch. Apr. 27, 2022) opinion analysed Elon Musk's 22% Tesla ownership as controller-level given the board dynamics; *Voigt v. Metcalf*, C.A. No. 2018-0828-JTL, 2020 WL 614999 (Del. Ch. Feb. 10, 2020) analysed a 24% holder as controller in the SolarCity transaction context. The absence of majority ownership is not a defence to controller status; the analysis is functional.

When a controller stands on both sides of a transaction — a squeeze-out merger, a controller-benefit recapitalisation, an issuance to the controller on preferential terms, a business combination with a controller-owned entity — the default standard of review is **entire fairness**, with the burden of proof on the defendants (the controller, the interested directors, the aiders-and-abettors). *Kahn v. Lynch Communication Systems, Inc.*, 638 A.2d 1110 (Del. 1994) established that a well-functioning special committee of independent directors *or* a fully-informed majority-of-minority vote could shift the burden of proof to the plaintiff — but both mechanisms individually still resulted in entire-fairness review, just with the plaintiff carrying the burden. MFW built on Lynch by holding that *both* mechanisms, combined with specific additional structural conditions, could restore the business-judgement rule entirely.

## The MFW six-condition test

The *Kahn v. M&F Worldwide* opinion articulated a six-condition test — commonly summarised as the "dual protection" of an independent special committee plus a majority-of-the-minority vote, each of which independently would shift the burden of proof and which together restore BJR:

1. **The controller conditions the transaction on the approval of both an independent special committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote from the outset (ab initio).** This is the *ab initio* condition — the dual conditioning must be in place at the beginning of the process, not adopted mid-negotiation. This condition has generated the most post-2014 doctrinal complication and is discussed in depth below.
2. **The special committee is independent.** Every member is independent of the controller under Delaware caselaw's specific tests — no material business relationships, no meaningful personal or professional dependencies. Chapter 4 covers the independence analysis in operational depth.
3. **The special committee is empowered to freely select its own advisors and to say no definitively.** The committee has the authority to reject the transaction outright. The controller cannot bypass the committee or unilaterally proceed. This is a *charter-scope* condition — the committee's mandate resolution must expressly empower it to say no and to select its own legal and financial advisors.
4. **The special committee meets its duty of care in negotiating a fair price.** This is the process-quality condition — the committee actually functions, actually deliberates, actually negotiates, actually holds meaningful meetings, actually retains and uses its advisors. A committee that meets three times, defers to the controller's initial offer, and rubber-stamps is not MFW-compliant.
5. **The vote of the minority stockholders is informed.** The proxy discloses all material information required for the minority to cast an informed vote — the same disclosure discipline that Corwin requires, applied here to the minority-of-minority vote.
6. **There is no coercion of the minority.** The vote is uncoerced. This overlaps with the fifth condition (informed) but is analytically distinct — a fully-informed vote can still be coercive if the alternative to voting yes is destructive (e.g., a proxy that makes clear the controller will squeeze out at a lower price if the vote fails, in a manner that pressures the minority to accept the current offer).

When all six conditions are satisfied, the standard of review is the business-judgement rule and the case gets dismissed on the pleadings absent waste-level facts. When any one condition is missed, the standard is entire fairness — with the burden potentially shifted to the plaintiff under Lynch if the special committee or the majority-of-minority vote independently qualifies, but with the standard-of-review remaining entire fairness. The gap between MFW-cleansed and entire-fairness is the same gap between motion-to-dismiss and trial.

## The *ab initio* condition — the timing trap

The first MFW condition — the *ab initio* dual conditioning — is the one that trips up boards most often. The Delaware Supreme Court in *Flood v. Synutra International, Inc.*, 195 A.3d 754 (Del. 2018) held that "ab initio" does not require the dual conditioning to be in the very first offer letter, but does require it to be in place *before any economic negotiations begin*. The court permitted the Synutra board to earn MFW cleansing where the controller's first offer letter did not contain the dual-conditioning language but a subsequent letter (delivered before any substantive negotiations) did.

*Olenik v. Lodzinski*, 208 A.3d 704 (Del. 2019) narrowed this further — the Delaware Supreme Court reversed the Court of Chancery's grant of MFW cleansing where the controller and the target had engaged in substantive discussions about the transaction's economics before the dual conditioning was in place. The court held that "substantive economic discussions" — even without a formal offer — could constitute the beginning of the negotiations that must be preceded by the dual conditioning. The practical consequence: a controller who wants to earn MFW must build the dual-conditioning language into its very first communication about the transaction, before anyone talks about price.

*In re CBS Corp. S'holder Class Action & Derivative Litig.*, 2021 WL 268779 (Del. Ch. Jan. 27, 2021) and other post-2019 opinions continue to police the ab initio requirement carefully. The current operating discipline: the controller's initial written communication to the target board (or to the CEO or GC) proposing a transaction must include the MFW dual-conditioning language, and no substantive economic discussions should occur before that written communication is delivered.

The **ab initio drafting formulation** (the language typically inserted in the controller's initial offer letter):

> This proposal is expressly conditioned on (i) the negotiation and execution of a definitive agreement acceptable to a special committee of the [Target Company] Board of Directors comprised solely of directors independent of [Controller], with the special committee empowered to select its own independent legal and financial advisors and to freely reject the proposal, and (ii) the approval of a non-waivable majority-of-the-minority stockholder vote requiring the approval of a majority of the shares of [Target Company] common stock held by stockholders other than [Controller] and its affiliates. [Controller] will not proceed with any transaction that is not approved by both the special committee and the majority-of-the-minority stockholder vote.

The exact drafting is customised per transaction, but the *substance* of the two conditions must be there and must precede any economic discussion.

## The special-committee-independence condition

The special committee must be composed *solely* of directors who are independent of the controller. Delaware independence analysis is fact-specific. The court examines the specific relationships each director has with the controller — business dealings, personal friendships, prior employment, shared board seats, common investment fund ties, familial relationships, professional service relationships (e.g., a director whose law firm has represented the controller on unrelated matters), and any relationship that "could reasonably be expected to affect the impartiality" of the director. *Aronson v. Lewis*, *Beam v. Stewart*, 845 A.2d 1040 (Del. 2004), and the SolarCity / Tesla independence analyses walk through the specific pattern.

Practical special-committee-independence pitfalls:

- **Director owes the controller a board seat.** If the director was nominated by the controller and is understood to serve at the controller's pleasure, the director is presumptively non-independent for MFW purposes.
- **Director has a material business relationship with the controller.** Any material commercial relationship — supplier, customer, financing party — is fatal.
- **Director shares a fund GP relationship with the controller.** Two directors from the same VC firm both serving on the special committee is a specific vulnerability.
- **Director recently sat on a controller-affiliate board.** Prior board service for the controller within the recent past (typically 3–5 years) is a specific concern.
- **Director has personal or family ties to the controller.** Long-standing personal friendships, family relationships, and social ties are examined.

The special-committee construction in chapter 4 walks the practical mechanics of identifying independent directors, running the independence questionnaire, and documenting the analysis for the minute book.

## The empowerment condition — the mandate resolution

The special committee's mandate — the board resolution that constitutes the committee and defines its authority — must expressly grant three specific powers:

1. **The power to select and engage independent legal and financial advisors** at the corporation's expense, without further board approval.
2. **The power to negotiate the transaction directly with the controller** (or the controller's representatives), including the power to conduct market checks, run parallel processes, and explore alternatives.
3. **The power to reject the transaction definitively** and to recommend against stockholder approval if the committee concludes the transaction is not in the best interests of the minority stockholders.

The mandate resolution should also specify:

- The committee's composition (named directors, chairperson, quorum).
- The committee's specific charge (typically "to evaluate and, if appropriate, negotiate and approve the proposed transaction with [Controller]").
- The committee's compensation for its work (often a fixed fee for the committee members separate from ordinary board fees, disclosed in the proxy — the compensation should not be contingent on completing the transaction, which would create a conflict of interest).
- The committee's authority to retain advisors and to expense the retention.

The mandate should *not* include:

- Any conditional or contingent authority that would let the controller or the full board override the committee's decisions.
- Any language that instructs the committee to reach a specific outcome.
- Any deadline that would force the committee to complete work in an unreasonably compressed timeline.

## The duty-of-care condition — the process-quality standard

The special committee must meet its duty of care. This is a *Van Gorkom* discipline applied to the committee's work — advance materials, meaningful meeting time, executive sessions with advisors, financial-advisor engagement, valuation methodology review, competing-bid solicitation (if appropriate), and a documented deliberation reflected in the minutes.

The Chancery Court's post-MFW opinions have policed this condition rigorously. A committee that meets four times and defers to the controller's initial offer is vulnerable. A committee that meets fifteen times over three months, engages a top-tier independent financial advisor, retains independent legal counsel, negotiates the offer up by 20% from the controller's initial bid, considers a market check, documents every meeting with careful minutes, and issues a written recommendation with specific reasons is on solid MFW ground.

The specific process-quality indicators the court reads:

- Number of meetings and hours in session.
- Presence of the committee's legal and financial advisors at meetings.
- Absence of the controller's advisors from committee deliberations (the committee's advisors, not the controller's, should attend).
- Executive sessions of the committee without management or the controller's advisors.
- Written materials distributed in advance of each meeting.
- Documented negotiation — offer-and-counter-offer history, alternatives considered, valuation-methodology review.
- Written committee recommendation with specific reasons.
- Fairness opinion from the committee's financial advisor (chapter 6).

## The majority-of-the-minority-vote condition — informed and uncoerced

The MFW cleansing requires an affirmative vote of a **majority of the minority** — a majority of the outstanding shares held by stockholders *other than* the controller and its affiliates (and, per some formulations, other than interested directors and executive officers). The vote is non-waivable (the controller cannot decide mid-process that the vote requirement will be dropped) and is on a fully-informed, uncoerced basis.

The "fully informed" standard is the same as under Corwin. The proxy must disclose all material information — the process by which the transaction was negotiated (including the controller's initial offer and the committee's counter-offers), the fairness opinion and its underlying methodology, the specific banker-conflicts (chapter 5), the committee's rationale, the committee members' independence, the alternatives considered, and any material information about the controller's plans post-transaction. Material omissions defeat MFW cleansing.

The "uncoerced" standard means the vote is not structurally coercive. A vote is coercive if the alternative is destructive to the minority in a way that pressures acceptance. A proxy that suggests the controller will proceed with a squeeze-out at a materially lower price if the current vote fails is a specific coercion risk; a proxy that presents the vote as a genuine choice between accepting the transaction and remaining a minority in the controlled company is not.

## The Delaware §144 amendment — DGCL controller-conflict framework (2024)

The Delaware General Corporation Law was amended in 2024 to codify certain aspects of the MFW framework in **DGCL §144**. The amendment provides statutory safe harbours for controlling-stockholder transactions that meet specific conditions, and interacts with the case-law MFW framework in ways that continue to evolve. Consult current counsel for the specific statutory text and its interaction with the case-law standard — the codification does not fully replace MFW but provides an alternative statutory pathway in specified circumstances.

## Half-MFW — when the dual conditioning is impossible

Some controller transactions cannot practically meet MFW. Reasons:

- The transaction is time-critical and the ab initio dual conditioning cannot be built in before the substantive economic discussions begin.
- The controller is unwilling to condition the transaction on the majority-of-the-minority vote (a controller-negotiation position that is uncommon in modern practice but occurs).
- The special committee is not feasible because the board lacks a sufficient number of directors independent of the controller.
- The transaction structure (e.g., a tender offer without a subsequent second-step merger) does not admit a majority-of-the-minority-vote mechanism.

In these cases the board falls back to the pre-MFW Lynch framework. The transaction is reviewed under entire fairness, but the burden can be shifted to the plaintiff by either (a) a well-functioning special committee of disinterested directors, or (b) an informed majority-of-the-minority vote. Either mechanism, satisfied on its own, shifts the burden of proof; the standard of review remains entire fairness. This is materially worse than MFW cleansing (the case goes to trial rather than dismissing on motion) but materially better than default entire fairness (the defendants don't carry the burden).

The practical implication: even when full MFW is not achievable, the board and the controller should implement whichever of the two mechanisms is feasible, to shift the burden and improve the litigation posture.

## Aiding-and-abetting exposure — the financial-advisor overlay

MFW cleansing runs to the directors and to the controller. The **financial advisor** engaged by the controller (or by the target) sits outside the MFW framework and can be exposed to an **aiding-and-abetting breach of fiduciary duty** claim under a separate line of doctrine anchored in *In re Rural Metro Corp. Stockholders Litigation*, 88 A.3d 54 (Del. Ch. 2014), aff'd sub nom. *RBC Capital Markets, LLC v. Jervis*, 129 A.3d 816 (Del. 2015). Chapter 5 covers the Rural Metro / Del Monte / El Paso banker-conflict analysis in depth. The specific point here is that MFW does not fully insulate the deal — the financial advisor's conflict exposure is a parallel workstream that needs its own analysis and disclosure discipline.

## Common MFW failure patterns

Six patterns account for most MFW misses:

1. **Ab initio miss.** The controller's initial communication does not include the dual-conditioning language, and substantive economic discussions occur before the dual conditioning is in place. The Olenik / Synutra reading is that this is fatal.
2. **Independence miss.** A special-committee director has a material relationship with the controller that is not disclosed or not analysed rigorously.
3. **Empowerment miss.** The committee's mandate does not clearly grant the power to reject the transaction, or the controller-negotiated terms allow the controller to bypass the committee under specified circumstances.
4. **Process-quality miss.** The committee meets few times, does not engage independent advisors meaningfully, or defers to the controller without evidence of genuine negotiation.
5. **Disclosure miss.** The proxy omits material information — the committee's deliberations, the fairness opinion methodology, the banker conflicts, the alternatives considered.
6. **Coercion miss.** The proxy contains language that could be read as pressuring the minority to accept the deal.

The special-committee counsel and the GC together own the MFW-compliance checklist across the transaction. Chapter 4 walks the operational discipline.

## Summary

MFW / *Kahn v. M&F Worldwide* installed a six-condition dual-protection framework that restores business-judgement review to controller-conflict transactions that would otherwise sit in entire fairness. The six conditions are: ab initio dual conditioning (the controller commits from the outset to both an independent special committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote); independent special committee (each member independent under Delaware caselaw); empowered special committee (able to select advisors and reject the transaction); duty-of-care process quality (meaningful meetings, independent advisors, documented negotiation); informed minority vote (fully-disclosed proxy); uncoerced vote (no structural coercion). The *ab initio* condition, refined by *Flood v. Synutra* and *Olenik v. Lodzinski*, requires the dual-conditioning language to precede any substantive economic discussion — the controller's initial written communication must include the language. When any one condition is missed, the transaction falls back to entire fairness, potentially with the burden shifted to the plaintiff under Lynch if the committee or the majority-of-minority vote independently qualifies. The 2024 DGCL §144 codification provides an alternative statutory pathway with specifics that continue to be refined. The special-committee-construction discipline in chapter 4 is what operationalises MFW in the board's calendar and minute book.
