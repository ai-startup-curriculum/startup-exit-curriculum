# Blasius and the Stockholder Franchise

## Why this matters

Delaware corporate law treats the **stockholder franchise** — the right of stockholders to elect the board and, through the board, to determine the fate of the corporation — as the foundational premise of corporate governance. Every fiduciary duty ultimately runs to the stockholders because the stockholders can vote the board out. When the board takes action that *impairs* the franchise — that makes the vote less meaningful, that changes the rules of the vote after votes have been cast, that dilutes the vote to preclude a specific outcome — the court applies a specifically heightened standard of review that is *more* demanding than Unocal enhanced scrutiny.

That standard is **Blasius**, from *Blasius Industries, Inc. v. Atlas Corp.*, 564 A.2d 651 (Del. Ch. 1988). Blasius requires the board to demonstrate a **compelling justification** for action taken with the primary purpose of impeding the effective exercise of the stockholder franchise. This is one of the highest standards in Delaware corporate law — higher than Unocal, sitting alongside entire fairness in its rigour — and it applies specifically to the narrow class of board actions that interfere with the stockholder vote.

For a transaction-stage board, Blasius surfaces in a specific and repeated pattern: the vote is scheduled, the vote appears likely to fail (or to succeed against the board's preferred outcome), and the board is presented with a menu of tactical options — reschedule the meeting, extend the record date, amend a bylaw to change the quorum requirement, expand the board mid-vote and fill the new seats with allies, adopt a poison pill that dilutes an activist accumulator, cap voting rights on a specific holder. Each of these tactics raises a Blasius question. A board that acts without understanding Blasius is a board that walks into an emergency Chancery hearing three days before the meeting.

This chapter walks the Blasius doctrine, the specific case-law refinements, the poison-pill / Blasius interaction, and the operational discipline for board action affecting the franchise. Chapter 4 covers the special-committee construction that operationalises much of this in a controller-conflict context.

## The doctrinal frame

Chancellor Allen's *Blasius* opinion (1988) set out the compelling-justification standard in a specific factual context: Atlas Industries' board learned that Blasius Industries had launched a proxy contest to expand the Atlas board from 7 to 15 seats and to fill the 8 new seats with Blasius nominees. The Atlas board responded by amending the bylaws to add 2 new seats — pre-empting the Blasius expansion by using up 2 of the 8 seats before the Blasius contest could reach them. The Court of Chancery held that this action, taken with the primary purpose of impeding the stockholder vote, was invalid absent a **compelling justification** — a higher standard than the deferential business-judgement rule *and* higher than Unocal enhanced scrutiny.

The court's formulation:

> When a board acts for the primary purpose of impeding the exercise of the stockholder voting power, it must demonstrate a compelling justification for such action.

The two triggering conditions:

1. **Board action.** The court is reviewing a specific board decision, not a stockholder vote or a market development.
2. **Primary purpose of impeding the franchise.** The board's motivation, judged objectively from the surrounding facts, was to interfere with the stockholder vote. Actions with mixed motives are analysed on a facts-and-circumstances basis; actions with a clear franchise-impairment purpose fall squarely within Blasius.

When Blasius applies, the board must show a **compelling justification** — a standard that Chancellor Allen characterised as "extremely difficult" to meet. The compelling-justification bar is not impossible (see the safe-harbours discussion below), but it is well above the "reasonableness" bar of Unocal and well above the deferential presumption of BJR.

## Interaction with Unocal — the Blasius-inside-Unocal formulation

The Delaware Supreme Court in *Mercier v. Inter-Tel (Delaware), Inc.*, 929 A.2d 786 (Del. Ch. 2007) and later cases refined the Blasius-Unocal interaction. When the board's action is a defensive tactic in response to a hostile bid (Unocal), *and* the defensive tactic impairs the stockholder franchise (Blasius), the court applies Unocal but reads the "reasonable in relation to the threat" element with the Blasius compelling-justification standard in mind. Practically: a defensive action that impairs the franchise (rescheduling a meeting to prevent a specific vote outcome, amending bylaws to change quorum, expanding the board mid-vote) must clear both the Unocal proportionality bar *and* the Blasius compelling-justification bar.

*MM Companies, Inc. v. Liquid Audio, Inc.*, 813 A.2d 1118 (Del. 2003) is the Supreme Court's clearest anchor for this integrated formulation. Liquid Audio's board, facing a hostile MM Companies proxy contest for control, expanded the board by two seats and filled them with allies to preserve a majority. The court struck the expansion under Blasius — the board's action was taken with the primary purpose of impeding the stockholder vote, and no compelling justification supported the action.

## Common franchise-impairment patterns

The transaction context surfaces a small set of recurring Blasius patterns.

**Meeting rescheduling / postponement.** The board reschedules the annual meeting or a special meeting to a later date to gain time — often after learning that a specific vote is likely to fail on the current schedule. *Aprahamian v. HBO & Co.*, 531 A.2d 1204 (Del. Ch. 1987) enjoined the postponement of an annual meeting where the board's evident purpose was to give itself more time to defeat a proxy contest. *Mercier v. Inter-Tel (Delaware), Inc.* (2007) permitted a postponement in a specific factual pattern — the postponement was to allow more time for stockholder consideration of a superior transaction and the court found a compelling justification.

The practitioner discipline: any board decision to reschedule a stockholder meeting, if the practical effect will be to alter the likely outcome of a vote, needs a Blasius analysis and (ideally) documented reasons that survive scrutiny.

**Record-date shifting.** The board changes the record date for a meeting, which changes which stockholders are entitled to vote. Record-date shifting can be a compelling-justification issue when the effect is to disenfranchise a specific holder or to include a specific favourable holder. The court reads the surrounding facts to determine whether the record-date shift was for legitimate administrative reasons or to game the vote.

**Board expansion mid-vote.** The board increases the number of seats and fills the new seats with allies, either to defeat a proxy-contest slate or to preserve a majority against a proxy vote for a specific corporate action. *Blasius* itself and *MM Companies v. Liquid Audio* both fall in this pattern. The court reads board-expansion mid-vote very sceptically.

**Bylaw amendments changing quorum, voting standards, or advance-notice requirements.** The board amends the bylaws to raise the quorum requirement, change the voting standard from plurality to majority (or vice versa), or add advance-notice requirements that a pending stockholder proposal cannot satisfy. Bylaw amendments that have a *neutral* long-term-governance purpose are generally cleared; amendments adopted mid-fight with a specific franchise-impairment purpose face Blasius scrutiny.

**Vote-capping / two-tier voting rights.** The board institutes a voting-rights cap that limits any single holder to a specific percentage of the vote, or adopts a two-tier voting structure that gives specific holders greater voting weight. These structural changes generally require stockholder approval under DGCL §242 (charter amendments); bylaw-only implementations that circumvent stockholder approval face particular scrutiny.

**Poison-pill trigger levels.** A poison pill with a low trigger level (typically 10–20%, but pandemic-era pills at 5%) prevents an accumulator from crossing the trigger and thereby denies the accumulator the leverage of a substantial stockholding. When the accumulator's purpose was to launch a proxy contest, a low pill trigger can be a franchise-impairment. The interaction is nuanced — the poison pill itself is Unocal-cleared machinery, but its use in a specific circumstance to impede a vote can raise Blasius questions. *Yucaipa American Alliance Fund II, L.P. v. Riggio*, 1 A.3d 310 (Del. Ch. 2010) walked through the pill-and-proxy-contest interaction in the Barnes & Noble context.

**Advance-notice bylaw enforcement.** The board rejects a stockholder proposal or a director nomination on the basis that the stockholder's advance-notice submission was deficient. Enforcement of a facially neutral advance-notice bylaw is generally cleared; targeted enforcement or last-minute amendments to advance-notice requirements to disqualify a specific submission face Blasius scrutiny.

## Safe harbours — when a franchise-impairing action can be cleared

A compelling justification exists in specific fact patterns, and boards can structure actions to earn the safe harbour:

**Adopted for legitimate long-term-governance purposes prior to any specific vote-context threat.** A bylaw amendment adopted six months before any active proxy contest, in the ordinary course of corporate governance and without any specific vote-context motivation, is generally not Blasius-vulnerable even if the amendment later has franchise-impairment effect.

**Necessary to complete a beneficial transaction.** A postponement to allow more time for stockholder consideration of a superior transaction, where the board can document that the primary purpose is transaction-facilitation rather than vote-manipulation, can clear Blasius (Mercier).

**Response to a specific stockholder-side manipulation.** Where the board is responding to specific manipulative stockholder conduct (a stockholder that misrepresented its intentions, that gamed the record date, that used illegal vote-buying), targeted board action to restore franchise integrity has a stronger compelling-justification argument.

**Stockholder ratification.** A subsequent informed stockholder vote ratifying the board's action can cleanse a Blasius-vulnerable decision. The ratification must be fully-informed and uncoerced, and must actually re-vote on the specific issue rather than a bundled proposal that obscures the specific action.

## The DGCL §211 hard stop — annual meeting requirement

Delaware requires an annual meeting of stockholders under **DGCL §211**. A corporation that has not held an annual meeting within the required period (typically 13 months from the last meeting) is subject to a stockholder-initiated Chancery action to compel the meeting. §211 is a hard-stop backstop against indefinite postponement of the franchise; a board that tries to indefinitely postpone a meeting to prevent a specific vote outcome will face a §211 action to compel.

## The poison-pill / Blasius interaction — the "just say no" and pill-and-vote patterns

The **poison pill** — a shareholder rights plan — is Unocal-cleared machinery. The pill's typical structure: any accumulator crossing a specified ownership threshold (often 10–20%) triggers a rights issuance that dilutes the accumulator to prevent an unwanted change of control. The board can redeem the pill in favour of a friendly transaction; the pill is a deterrent against unsolicited accumulation.

The pill / Blasius interaction surfaces in two scenarios:

1. **Pill-as-vote-blocker.** An activist accumulator plans to accumulate a large stake and use that stake's voting weight to win a proxy contest for board control. A low-trigger pill (or a mid-fight pill adoption with a specific trigger targeting the activist) may effectively prevent the accumulation and thereby the vote. The court analyses whether the pill's use in the specific context was Unocal-proportional (a defensive-tactic analysis) and whether the pill's franchise-impairment effect is Blasius-vulnerable. *Third Point LLC v. Ruprecht*, 2014 WL 1922029 (Del. Ch. May 2, 2014) walked through a Sotheby's-context pill adoption with a specific activist in mind.

2. **Pill-redemption in a proxy contest.** The board is asked to redeem the pill to permit a hostile bidder to complete a tender offer. The board's refusal to redeem is Unocal-analysed as a defensive tactic; if the refusal has the effect of preventing a stockholder vote (a tender offer functioning as an alternative to a proxy contest), the refusal can raise Blasius considerations. *Air Products v. Airgas* is the current anchor for the "just say no" pill posture and reads on both Unocal and Blasius dimensions.

## Operational discipline — how to run a franchise-affecting decision

The board's practical discipline when facing a franchise-affecting decision:

1. **Identify the Blasius question early.** At the first board meeting where a potentially-franchise-affecting action is discussed, GC (or outside counsel) should flag the Blasius standard and walk the board through the compelling-justification analysis.

2. **Distinguish the primary purpose.** Document the *business* purpose of the action, distinct from any franchise-affecting effect. A postponement adopted primarily to allow SEC comment resolution is different from a postponement adopted primarily because the board expects the vote to fail. The minute-book documentation should reflect the business purpose.

3. **Consider less franchise-impairing alternatives.** Delaware courts read board action in light of available alternatives. If the board could have achieved the business objective through a less franchise-impairing action (a shorter postponement, a narrower bylaw amendment, a targeted disclosure rather than a schedule change), the court will read the more impairing choice sceptically.

4. **Consult with outside counsel before acting.** Blasius-vulnerable actions typically get vetted by specialist Delaware corporate counsel (Wachtell, Cravath, Skadden, Latham, Wilson Sonsini, Cooley, Fried Frank, Weil, Simpson Thacher, Davis Polk depending on the specific issuer's counsel). The consultation is documented in the minutes.

5. **Prepare for the emergency Chancery hearing.** Franchise-affecting board actions routinely draw an immediate Chancery motion for a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction. The board should be prepared to defend the action at an expedited hearing within days of the decision, with a full evidentiary record.

6. **Consider the disclosure obligations.** The board's action typically requires a disclosure to stockholders — a proxy supplement, an 8-K, a specific press release — and the disclosure must fully and fairly describe the action and its reasons. Incomplete or misleading disclosure compounds the Blasius problem.

## Private-company Blasius — the pre-IPO context

Blasius is doctrinally a public-company-vote framework, but the underlying franchise principle applies to private-company stockholder votes as well. The specific pre-IPO transaction-governance scenarios:

**Charter amendment for IPO-graduation.** The pre-IPO charter amendment eliminates preferred-stock special voting rights, converts preferred to common, cleans up the equity structure, and installs the IPO-target charter. The amendment typically requires stockholder approval; the pre-IPO investor consents (often via written consent under DGCL §228) are the vote. If a specific preferred class objects, the mechanics of the vote and any board action to structure the vote can raise franchise-adjacent concerns.

**Recap or restructuring vote.** A pre-IPO recap or restructuring often includes a stockholder vote (or written consent). Board action that structures the vote to disenfranchise a specific class or holder should be reviewed against the franchise principle.

**Drag-along invocation.** The NVCA voting agreement typically includes a drag-along that binds minority stockholders to a sale approved by a specified majority. The drag mechanic is *contractual* rather than *fiduciary* and is generally enforceable; the specific mechanics of invocation (proper notice, proper vote, proper class-of-holder scope) should be reviewed against the specific agreement. Chapter 7 covers the drag-along mechanics in appraisal context.

## Summary

Blasius / *Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp.* installed the compelling-justification standard for board action taken with the primary purpose of impeding the stockholder franchise. The standard is more demanding than the business-judgement rule and more demanding than Unocal enhanced scrutiny. Common franchise-impairment patterns include meeting rescheduling, record-date shifting, mid-vote board expansion (*MM Companies v. Liquid Audio*), bylaw amendments that change quorum or voting standards mid-fight, vote-capping / two-tier voting, low-trigger or targeted poison pills, and targeted advance-notice enforcement. Safe harbours include actions adopted in the ordinary course before any specific vote-context threat, actions necessary to complete a beneficial transaction (*Mercier v. Inter-Tel*), responses to specific stockholder-side manipulation, and stockholder ratification via a subsequent informed vote. The DGCL §211 annual-meeting requirement is a hard-stop backstop against indefinite postponement. The poison-pill / Blasius interaction surfaces in pill-as-vote-blocker and pill-redemption scenarios; the current anchor for "just say no" is *Air Products v. Airgas*. Operational discipline requires early identification of the Blasius question, documented business-purpose analysis, consideration of less franchise-impairing alternatives, consultation with specialist Delaware counsel, preparation for the emergency Chancery hearing, and full disclosure of the action and its reasons.
