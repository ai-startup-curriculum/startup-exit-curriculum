# Fairness Opinion Procurement and Disclosure

## Why this matters

The **fairness opinion** is the written statement from a qualified financial advisor that a specific transaction consideration is "fair, from a financial point of view" to the target's stockholders (or to a specific class of stockholders). It is the document the board — and the special committee — hold up to the standard of *Van Gorkom* to demonstrate informed decision-making. It is the analytic backbone that the proxy summarises for the stockholder vote. It is the exhibit that Chancery will read line-by-line if the transaction is subsequently challenged.

The fairness opinion is *not* an appraisal, *not* a valuation guarantee, *not* a recommendation to vote in favour, and *not* a substitute for the board's own business judgement. It is a professional opinion from a financial advisor that, on the basis of specified analyses and specified assumptions, the consideration is fair from a financial point of view. The distinction matters because Chancery will read the opinion for what it *says* and what it *does not say* — and the disclosure regime imposed by SEC Item 1015 (for going-private transactions) and by market convention (for other transactions) will make the opinion's methodology, assumptions, and limitations visible to the stockholder.

This chapter walks the fairness-opinion decision (banker vs. independent advisor at deeper resolution than chapter 5), the valuation-methodology mix that a modern opinion typically presents, the discount-rate and terminal-value disclosure discipline, the specific SEC disclosure regime, and the practitioner discipline for procuring, delivering, and using the opinion.

## When is a fairness opinion required — and when is it merely conventional

**Required by SEC rule.** Rule 13e-3 (going-private transactions) requires specific "fairness" statements and disclosure in the Schedule 13E-3 that the issuer must file. The statement typically incorporates a fairness opinion from a financial advisor, though the rule technically requires the board's *own* fairness statement rather than the banker's opinion — the banker's opinion is the analytic backbone of the board's statement.

**Required by market convention.** In substantially all M&A transactions involving a US-listed public target — sale of the company, merger, controller take-private, going-private — market convention (and the practical fiduciary discipline of Van Gorkom) makes a fairness opinion effectively mandatory. Boards that proceed without one are anomalies and face specific vulnerability under Van Gorkom.

**Required by specific stockholder or debt agreements.** Preferred-stock protective provisions, debt covenants, and other contractual arrangements sometimes require a fairness opinion as a condition to specific corporate actions.

**Optional but common at private-company scale.** For a private-company sale, a fairness opinion is not required by SEC rule and is not universal market convention. Many private-company sales proceed without a fairness opinion, particularly where the process was a clean auction with substantial competitive tension. Fairness opinions become more common at private-company scale when (a) the transaction involves controller or interested-director conflicts, (b) the deal is complex or contested, (c) preferred-stock or debt-contract requirements attach, or (d) the buy-side is exposed to potential post-close claim risk that a fairness opinion helps insulate.

**Not required for the IPO governance-graduation vote.** The IPO does not require a fairness opinion in the ordinary case (there is no "transaction consideration" of the kind a fairness opinion evaluates). Fairness opinions occasionally attach to specific pre-IPO recap transactions or to specific components of a going-public transaction structure (e.g., a de-SPAC), depending on the structure. Chapter 8 covers ISS / Glass Lewis engagement on IPO-governance votes.

## The banker vs. independent-advisor decision at deeper resolution

Chapter 5 introduced the single-banker-vs-separate-fairness-opinion framework. The specific decision variables:

**Conflict severity of the sell-side banker.** If the sell-side banker has no material conflicts — no buy-side pitching, no stapled financing, no material firm-level or individual banker holdings — the single-banker structure is typically defensible. If conflicts are material, a separate independent fairness opinion becomes the preferred structure.

**Transaction sensitivity.** High-profile transactions (large controller take-privates, contested M&A, transactions with a specific litigation-risk profile) draw a separate fairness opinion more often than routine third-party arms-length deals.

**Rule 13e-3 applicability.** Going-private transactions carry a specific Item 1015 disclosure regime (below) and a heightened SEC-review posture. A separate independent fairness opinion strengthens the disclosure and litigation posture.

**Board and committee preferences.** The special committee (with its counsel's advice) may prefer a separate fairness opinion for the litigation-defence posture even where a single-banker structure would be technically defensible.

**Cost.** A separate fairness opinion typically costs $500K–$2.5M+ depending on complexity, deal size, and firm. The cost is generally modest against the deal's transaction fees and against the litigation-risk exposure.

**Availability of the specific reputational franchise.** Some firms have specific reputational franchises as "clean fairness opinion" providers. Duff & Phelps / Kroll in particular has built the modal independent-fairness-opinion practice for controller take-privates. Houlihan Lokey has a broad-based independent-advisor franchise. Stout Risius Ross, VRC, Mesirow, and specialty valuation practices are additional options. The M&A-advisory boutiques (Evercore, Lazard, Moelis, Guggenheim, Centerview, PJT Partners, Perella Weinberg, Rothschild) frequently take second-opinion engagements when their sell-side franchise is not primary.

The committee's counsel typically prepares an engagement-decision memo walking the specific factors and recommending the structure.

## The valuation-methodology mix — what a modern fairness opinion contains

A modern fairness opinion is typically supported by a **valuation presentation** (30–100 pages) that walks the analyses supporting the opinion. The opinion itself is a 3–5-page letter that lists the analyses performed, the assumptions made, the limitations of the opinion, and the fairness conclusion. The valuation presentation is what the special committee and the board read to understand the analysis; the letter is what gets filed as an exhibit to the proxy and Schedule 13E-3.

The standard analyses in a modern valuation presentation:

**Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) analysis.** The core intrinsic-value analysis. Projects the target's free cash flows over an explicit forecast period (typically 5–10 years), applies a terminal-value calculation, and discounts back to present value at a weighted-average cost of capital. Presents a range of enterprise values (and equity values) under different discount-rate and terminal-value sensitivities. The DCF is the most Chancery-scrutinised analysis in the post-*Aruba* / *Dell* / *DFC Global* era (chapter 7) — appraisal courts have increasingly focused on DCF-derived fair-value calculations.

**Precedent-transactions analysis.** Reviews specific comparable acquisitions in the target's sector, computes implied multiples (EV/Revenue, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, P/E, and sector-specific metrics), and applies the median or range of multiples to the target's financials to imply a valuation range. The specific "comparability" analysis — which transactions are truly comparable, how the multiples should be normalised for size / growth / margin differences — is a specific area of banker judgement.

**Trading-comparables analysis.** Reviews specific comparable publicly-traded companies, computes trading multiples, and applies to the target. This is a "public market value" reference — what the market would pay for the target if it were standing alone as a public company.

**Leveraged Buyout (LBO) analysis.** For controller take-privates or private-equity acquisitions, an LBO analysis computes the internal rate of return a hypothetical financial sponsor could achieve on the transaction, given specified leverage assumptions and exit multiples. The analysis is a "what would a PE buyer pay" reference and is particularly relevant when the actual transaction is a PE deal.

**Premia analysis.** Computes the premium over specific pre-announcement stock prices — 1-day, 30-day, 90-day, 52-week high, 52-week low — and compares against comparable-transaction premia. Not itself a valuation methodology but a market-context reference.

**Sum-of-parts analysis.** For diversified companies, separately values each business segment and sums to a total. Particularly relevant when the target's segments have materially different growth, margin, or capital-intensity profiles.

**Sector-specific analyses.** Sector conventions add specific methodologies — for pharma / biotech, probability-adjusted DCFs for pipeline value; for real estate, cap-rate analyses; for insurance, embedded-value analyses; for banks, tangible-book-value multiples.

The opinion typically summarises the ranges implied by each analysis and states that the transaction consideration is (or is not) within the range implied by the analyses collectively. A consideration meaningfully below the DCF range or meaningfully below the median precedent-transaction implied value creates a specific "fairness" question that the opinion must address.

## Discount rate and terminal value — the two most-scrutinised assumptions

The two DCF inputs most scrutinised by Chancery Court in appraisal and fiduciary-duty cases:

**Discount rate — the weighted-average cost of capital (WACC).** Typically computed as (cost of equity × equity-weight) + (after-tax cost of debt × debt-weight). Cost of equity is typically CAPM-based (risk-free rate + equity risk premium × beta + specific-adjustments); cost of debt is typically the market yield on comparable-rated debt. The specific assumptions:

- Risk-free rate: typically the current 10-year US Treasury yield or a specific longer-duration Treasury yield.
- Equity risk premium: typically 5–8% depending on the market context; the specific choice matters because the range spans multiple percentage points.
- Beta: typically computed as a levered beta from a comparable-set of public companies, adjusted for capital structure. Beta choices vary meaningfully between 5-year and 2-year regressions, weekly and monthly return frequencies, and comparable-set selections.
- Size premium: often a specific add-on for the equity risk premium to reflect the additional risk of small-cap companies (typically 1–5% depending on size).
- Country/company-specific risk premium: additional adjustments for specific-company risks or emerging-market exposures.
- Capital structure weights: typically at market values, though specific normalisation questions arise for companies with non-traded debt or preferred stock.

A DCF's implied value is highly sensitive to the discount rate. A 100-basis-point change in WACC can move implied value by 15–25%. Chancery reads the specific WACC assumptions carefully — a WACC materially above the comparable-set-implied range is a red flag for a low-price bias.

**Terminal value.** The value at the end of the explicit forecast period, typically computed as either (a) a perpetuity growth methodology (terminal free cash flow × (1 + g) / (WACC - g), where g is a terminal growth rate) or (b) an exit-multiple methodology (terminal EBITDA × an exit multiple). The specific assumptions:

- Terminal growth rate: typically 2–3.5% (approximately long-term GDP growth), with specific adjustments for the target's sector and long-term growth prospects.
- Exit multiple: typically the current-trading-multiple average of the comparable set, sometimes adjusted for cycle or sector-specific factors.
- Perpetuity vs. exit-multiple weighting: many analyses present both and weight them; the specific weight allocation matters.

Terminal value typically represents 60–85% of the total DCF value. Small changes in terminal-value assumptions move implied value substantially.

The **discount-rate and terminal-value sensitivity table** — a two-dimensional grid showing implied value at different discount rates (rows) and terminal growth rates or exit multiples (columns) — is a standard component of the valuation presentation and is the most scrutinised page by Chancery.

## The Item 1015 disclosure regime — going-private transactions

**SEC Regulation M-A Item 1015** requires specific disclosure about any report, opinion, or appraisal received from an outside party materially relating to the transaction that is required to be filed under Schedule 13E-3 (going-private transactions). The specific Item 1015 disclosure elements:

- **Identity and qualifications of the outside party.** Name of the financial advisor, brief description of qualifications and experience.
- **Method of selection.** How the advisor was selected — competitive process, specific relationship, prior work.
- **Any material relationship** between the advisor and the filing persons (issuer, controller, affiliates) — past 2 years and currently, with specific fee amounts.
- **Bases and methods of preparation.** The specific analyses performed, the specific data used, the specific assumptions applied.
- **Any limitations imposed** by any party on the advisor.
- **Whether the report is filed as an exhibit.** The full text of the fairness opinion, and often the full valuation presentation, is filed as an exhibit to Schedule 13E-3.

The Item 1015 disclosure is the anchor for Rule 13e-3 fairness-opinion disclosure and drives a significant portion of the proxy content in going-private transactions.

For non-13e-3 transactions, the Item 1015 requirements are not directly applicable, but market convention and the disclosure discipline of Corwin (chapter 1) and MFW (chapter 2) drive comparable disclosure in the proxy for M&A transactions generally.

## The opinion letter — what it says and what it does not

The fairness opinion letter itself — filed as an exhibit to the proxy — is a specific formal document with a highly conventionalised structure:

**Salutation.** Addressed to the board of directors (or the special committee).

**Description of the transaction.** A summary of the transaction being evaluated — the parties, the consideration, the specific structure.

**Description of the advisor's engagement.** How and by whom the advisor was retained, the scope of the engagement, the fee arrangement (typically at a summary level, with more detail in the proxy).

**Description of analyses performed.** A list of the specific analyses performed (DCF, precedent transactions, trading comparables, etc.) with high-level description of each.

**Description of information relied upon.** Financial statements, management projections, industry data, third-party reports, and other information the advisor relied upon.

**Assumptions and limitations.** The specific assumptions (e.g., "assumed the accuracy and completeness of information provided by management," "assumed the transaction will close on the terms described"), the specific limitations (e.g., "does not address the underlying business decision to enter the transaction," "does not constitute a recommendation to vote"), and the specific reservation of the opinion to the specific date.

**Fairness conclusion.** The specific statement — typically "on the basis of and subject to the foregoing, we are of the opinion, as of [date], that the [Consideration] to be received by the [Class of Stockholders] pursuant to the [Agreement] is fair, from a financial point of view, to such [Class of Stockholders]."

The opinion letter is 3–5 pages, formatted conventionally, signed by the advisor firm. It is the *headline* of the fairness workstream but not the full analytic content — the valuation presentation is the analytic backbone.

**What the opinion does not say.** Explicitly:

- It does not opine on the underlying business decision to enter the transaction.
- It does not opine on the relative merits of the transaction versus alternatives.
- It does not constitute a recommendation to any stockholder as to how to vote.
- It does not address the fairness of the transaction to any specific stockholder other than the specified class.
- It does not opine on tax, legal, regulatory, or accounting matters.
- It does not opine on the fairness of any specific element of consideration (e.g., a specific earn-out component) unless expressly stated.

## The oral vs. written opinion sequence

The fairness opinion is typically delivered in a **two-step sequence**:

**Oral opinion at the decision meeting.** At the special committee's (and full board's) meeting to approve the transaction, the financial advisor delivers an oral fairness opinion, accompanied by the presentation of the valuation analysis. The board's decision to approve the transaction is taken with the benefit of the oral opinion.

**Written opinion delivered promptly thereafter.** A written opinion, formalising the oral opinion, is typically delivered within 24–48 hours of the decision meeting. The written opinion is dated as of the decision-meeting date. It is filed as an exhibit to the proxy.

The oral-then-written sequence is a specific *Van Gorkom* discipline — Trans Union's board notoriously received only an oral opinion (and a partial one at that) at the decision meeting, and Van Gorkom is one of the reasons that the modern discipline is to have (at minimum) an oral opinion at the decision meeting with a written follow-up.

## The bring-down opinion — mid-process updates

Between the initial fairness opinion and the closing, material events may occur — market movements, target-financial developments, competing bids, regulatory developments. If the transaction structure includes a period between signing and closing (typical for any transaction requiring stockholder vote), the committee's counsel typically evaluates whether a **bring-down opinion** (an updated fairness opinion as of a specific later date) is required.

The bring-down opinion is common when:

- Material adverse target-business developments occur.
- Material market movements affect the consideration's value (typical for stock-consideration deals).
- A competing bid emerges that must be evaluated.
- The transaction structure changes materially (e.g., an increase in consideration, a structural amendment).

The bring-down is delivered following the same oral-then-written sequence, and is disclosed in the proxy or in supplemental proxy materials.

## Fairness opinion fees

Fairness-opinion fees vary widely by deal size, firm, and structure:

**Sell-side banker delivering fairness opinion within broader engagement.** The fairness opinion is typically bundled into the overall engagement fee (retainer + success fee). No separate fairness-opinion fee is charged; the opinion is understood as part of the sell-side deliverable.

**Sell-side banker with a specifically identified fairness-opinion fee.** Some engagement letters break out a specific non-contingent fairness-opinion fee (paid on delivery of the opinion regardless of transaction close) from the success fee. This structure — Rural-Metro-influenced — insulates the fairness opinion from the success-fee-driven skew.

**Independent-fairness-opinion provider.** Engaged specifically for the fairness opinion. Fees range from $250K–$1.5M+ for smaller transactions to $1.5M–$5M+ for major controller take-privates or complex deals. Fees are typically non-contingent (paid on delivery regardless of transaction close).

**Second-opinion for very-large or contested transactions.** Occasionally the committee engages both a bulge-bracket sell-side banker (with fairness opinion as part of engagement) and an independent second-opinion provider. Second-opinion fees typically run $500K–$2M+.

All fees are disclosed in the proxy (Item 1015 for 13e-3 transactions, and by market convention for all others).

## Fairness opinion vs. appraisal — why they are different

The fairness opinion is not an appraisal. The distinction:

- **Fairness opinion** — an opinion that a specific consideration is "fair from a financial point of view." It considers a range of implied values from multiple methodologies and evaluates whether the consideration falls within that range. It is qualitative and probabilistic — "is this price reasonable given a range of methodologies?"
- **Appraisal** — a specific determination of "fair value" for purposes of DGCL §262 statutory appraisal (chapter 7). It is a specific dollar-per-share determination, typically arrived at through Chancery Court's own analysis of the target's intrinsic value. It is quantitative and specific — "what is this share worth?"

The distinction matters because Chancery's appraisal caselaw (Aruba / Dell / DFC Global — chapter 7) has increasingly emphasised deal price as the presumptive fair value in an arm's-length sale process, which has interesting doctrinal interactions with the fairness opinion's range-of-value formulation.

## Common fairness-opinion failure patterns

Six patterns account for most fairness-opinion vulnerabilities:

1. **Methodology cherry-picking.** The opinion excludes an analysis (typically DCF or precedent transactions) that would imply a materially higher value. Chancery reads exclusion decisions carefully.
2. **Comparable-set gaming.** The precedent-transactions or trading-comparables set is selected to include specific-lower-multiple transactions and to exclude specific-higher-multiple transactions.
3. **Discount-rate skew.** The WACC is set at the high end of the reasonable range, biasing DCF implied value downward.
4. **Terminal-value skew.** The terminal growth rate is set low or the exit multiple is set low, biasing DCF implied value downward.
5. **Management-projection substitution.** The advisor uses management projections that are meaningfully below management's own base-case forecasts (or above them, in an appraisal-vulnerable scenario), without adequate explanation.
6. **Undisclosed conflicts.** The opinion is issued by an advisor with undisclosed conflicts (Rural Metro pattern, chapter 5) that skew the analysis.

The committee's counsel and the committee itself should probe each pattern before accepting the opinion.

## Summary

The fairness opinion is the professional financial-advisor opinion that specific transaction consideration is fair, from a financial point of view, to the specified stockholder class. It is required by SEC Rule 13e-3 for going-private transactions (Item 1015 disclosure regime), effectively mandatory by market convention for US-listed M&A, and increasingly common for private-company transactions with conflict or complexity. The banker vs. independent-advisor decision turns on conflict severity, transaction sensitivity, and litigation-defence posture; Duff & Phelps / Kroll, Houlihan Lokey, and M&A-advisory boutiques are the modal separate-fairness-opinion providers. The standard valuation-methodology mix includes DCF (with heavily-scrutinised discount-rate and terminal-value assumptions), precedent-transactions, trading-comparables, LBO (for PE deals), premia analysis, sum-of-parts (for diversified companies), and sector-specific analyses. Item 1015 requires specific disclosure about the advisor's identity, qualifications, selection method, material relationships, methodologies, and limitations. The opinion letter is a 3–5-page formal document with a standardised structure; it opines on financial fairness only, not on business merits or voting recommendations. The oral-then-written sequence is the modern Van Gorkom-compliant discipline. Bring-down opinions address mid-process material events. Common failure patterns include methodology cherry-picking, comparable-set gaming, discount-rate and terminal-value skew, and undisclosed conflicts.

Chapter 7 turns to appraisal-rights exposure under DGCL §262 — the statutory appraisal mechanism, the Aruba / DFC Global / Dell arc that repositioned deal price, appraisal-arbitrage economics, and the drag-along mechanics at private-company scale.
