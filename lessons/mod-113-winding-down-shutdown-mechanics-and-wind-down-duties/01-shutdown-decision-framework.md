# The Shutdown Decision Framework — Five Mechanisms and How to Choose

## Why this matters

The shutdown decision arrives late, arrives with a compressed timeline, and arrives with the founder-CEO and CFO already exhausted by the months of runway-preservation, failed-round, or failed-sale work that preceded it. Under those conditions the temptation is to reach for the mechanism that feels most familiar — the informal wind-down the founder has heard peers describe, the Chapter 7 that sounds like "how companies formally close," or the Chapter 11 that sounds like "reorganisation but for our case it becomes a liquidation." The temptation is expensive. Each of the five real mechanisms — informal wind-down, sale-for-parts, Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors (ABC), Chapter 7, Chapter 11 — has a distinct cost, speed, creditor-treatment, D&O-exposure, employee-outcome, and IP-disposition signature, and picking the wrong mechanism can cost creditors 20–40 cents on the dollar of realisable value, expose officers to trustee-driven avoidance-action litigation they would not otherwise face, or extend the wind-up from an eight-week ABC to an eighteen-month Chapter 11 that consumes what recoveries remain.

The remedy is not to have the "right" answer in your head before the decision arrives — the right answer depends on facts you will only have at the decision moment. The remedy is to have the *framework* installed cold, so that when the moment arrives the founder-CEO, CFO, and GC are collecting the specific data the framework needs and asking counsel the specific questions the framework isolates, instead of learning the vocabulary from scratch. This chapter installs that framework. The rest of the module goes deep on the two branches that shoulder most venture-backed shutdowns (ABC in chapter 2; Chapter 7 / Chapter 11 in chapter 3) and on the workstreams that attach to every branch (WARN, employee obligations, IP disposition, D&O tail, personal liability) in chapters 4–9.

> Reminder: this module is education, not legal, restructuring, tax, employment, or insurance advice. Every live shutdown engages a specific set of advisors on a specific timeline. This chapter is what you read *before* you engage them, so that when you do their time goes to judgment rather than education.

## The five mechanisms in one paragraph each

Before we compare, name the five mechanisms concretely. The differences are not aesthetic — they are statutory, procedural, and (for the officer-and-director personal-liability layer covered in chapter 9) consequential.

### 1. Informal wind-down (out-of-court dissolution)

The company pays what it can, negotiates informal settlements with the creditors it cannot pay in full, and formally dissolves under state corporate law (typically Delaware General Corporation Law §275–§283 for Delaware corporations, similar sections in other jurisdictions). No court process, no assignee, no trustee. This works when the residual asset pool is enough to satisfy every creditor in full — i.e., the company is *solvent* on both a balance-sheet and cash-flow basis at the moment it wraps up — or when a small handful of creditors will consent to a specific settlement in exchange for a specific payment. It does not work when creditors will be left short and could later challenge the wind-down as a fraudulent transfer, a preference, or a breach of the officers' fiduciary duty in the zone of insolvency. The Delaware §278 three-year survival period for post-dissolution claims still runs; the company is not immunised just because it filed a certificate of dissolution.

### 2. Sale-for-parts (asset sale before shutdown)

Before triggering an ABC or a bankruptcy, the company runs a compressed sell-side process on its remaining assets — technology, customer contracts, IP, in some cases the operating team via an acqui-hire — and closes the sale as an asset purchase agreement (rarely a stock purchase in a distressed sale-for-parts). The proceeds go to satisfy creditors in the priority order the shutdown mechanism then applies (whatever mechanism the company then triggers to distribute proceeds and dissolve). Sale-for-parts is not an alternative to the other four mechanisms; it is a *step* that happens under one of them. A common pattern is a pre-negotiated sale-for-parts *inside* an ABC — the assignee runs the closing and distributes the proceeds — or *inside* a Section 363 sale process inside a Chapter 11 or a Chapter 7. The reason to name it as its own bullet in the framework is that the *decision to run a sale-for-parts* is often made before the decision on which shutdown mechanism will house it, and getting the sale done before shutdown often materially improves the realisable value.

### 3. Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors (ABC)

A state-law (not federal-bankruptcy) mechanism in which the company (the assignor) transfers all its assets to a specific third-party assignee (a firm like Sherwood Partners in California, Development Specialists Inc., SierraConstellation, Armanino, or FTI Consulting) under an assignment agreement. The assignee holds the assets in trust for the benefit of the creditors, liquidates them (often via a Section-363-style sale process the assignee runs), and distributes the proceeds in a priority-of-claims waterfall that broadly tracks bankruptcy priorities but is administered under state law. ABC is the *default choice for a venture-backed company shutdown* in California and increasingly nationwide because it is faster (4–8 weeks vs. Chapter 7's 6+ months or Chapter 11's 12+ months), cheaper (assignee fees are a small fraction of trustee / DIP-professional fees), and more efficient at delivering proceeds to creditors. Chapter 2 develops this mechanism in depth.

### 4. Chapter 7 (federal bankruptcy — trustee-controlled liquidation)

The company (the debtor) files a voluntary petition under Chapter 7 of the US Bankruptcy Code (Title 11). The automatic stay of 11 U.S.C. §362 fires immediately and stops creditor collection actions. A trustee is appointed (initially an interim trustee under 11 U.S.C. §701, then either that trustee or an elected trustee under §702). The trustee takes control of the estate, liquidates the assets, and distributes proceeds in the priority order of 11 U.S.C. §507. The officers-and-directors are displaced from the wind-up entirely — the trustee is running it — and the trustee has statutory duties to investigate the debtor's pre-petition affairs, which can include avoidance actions against insiders under 11 U.S.C. §547 (preferences) and §548 (fraudulent transfers). Chapter 3 develops this mechanism.

### 5. Chapter 11 (federal bankruptcy — debtor-in-possession reorganisation)

The company files a voluntary petition under Chapter 11. The automatic stay fires. But rather than a trustee taking over, the company remains in possession of the estate as a *debtor-in-possession* (DIP) under 11 U.S.C. §1107, running the business through the case under court oversight. The purpose of Chapter 11 is a plan of reorganisation confirmed under 11 U.S.C. §1129 — a document that restructures the company's liabilities and, ideally, allows it to emerge as an operating going concern. For a venture-backed startup, an operating-emergence Chapter 11 is *rare* because there is usually no going-concern value the plan can preserve; the far more common shape is a *Section 363 sale* under 11 U.S.C. §363 (the DIP sells substantially all its assets to a buyer, free and clear of liens under §363(f), with the sale approved by a specific court order) followed by a liquidating plan or a conversion to Chapter 7. A pre-packaged Chapter 11 — where the plan is pre-negotiated with the key creditor constituencies before filing — compresses the timeline. Chapter 3 develops this mechanism.

## The six comparison axes

The choice among the five mechanisms is made on six axes. Not one — six. A framework that reasons on cost alone (or speed alone, or creditor-treatment alone) will pick the wrong mechanism roughly as often as it picks the right one. The six axes are:

### Axis 1 — Cost

The all-in cost of running the mechanism from decision to close. It includes:

- **Assignee / trustee / DIP-professional fees.** The single largest cost driver. An ABC assignee typically works for a percentage of asset recoveries (roughly 5–15% depending on the assignee and the deal, with a floor) plus expenses. A Chapter 7 trustee's compensation is set under 11 U.S.C. §326 as a sliding percentage of moneys disbursed to parties in interest (25% of the first $5K, 10% of the next $45K, 5% of the next $950K, 3% of anything above $1M — capped by a specific formula). Chapter 11 professional fees (debtor's counsel, financial advisor, investment banker for the §363 sale, creditors-committee counsel, US Trustee fees) can easily total $500K–$2M+ for a modest venture-backed case and $5M–$20M+ for a mid-market case.
- **Counsel fees.** Company-side restructuring counsel through the decision and the execution. For an ABC, roughly $50K–$200K depending on complexity. For Chapter 7 or Chapter 11, materially higher — Ch 11 counsel for a modest case typically starts at $250K and scales rapidly with case complexity, contested motions, and adversary proceedings.
- **Court fees.** Chapter 7 filing fee is currently $338 (per the US Courts fee schedule); Chapter 11 filing fee is currently $1,738; both plus a $571 administrative fee for Ch 11 (verify current amounts on <https://www.uscourts.gov/services-forms/fees/bankruptcy-court-miscellaneous-fee-schedule>). ABC has no court filing fee but often has small state-court or Superior Court filing costs (in California a General Assignment can be filed as a public record but is not required to be filed with a court in the same way a bankruptcy is).
- **Officer-and-director involvement time.** Not usually counted as a "cost" but a real one — an ABC can consume 40–120 hours of CEO / CFO / GC time; a Chapter 11 can consume 500+ hours of the same seats and often forces personal-time contribution during the case.

**Rough ranking** (ordered cheapest to most expensive for a mid-sized venture-backed company shutdown): Informal wind-down < Sale-for-parts (marginal on top of whichever mechanism houses it) < ABC ≪ Chapter 7 ≪ Chapter 11. The gap between ABC and Chapter 7 / Chapter 11 is substantial and is one of the primary reasons ABC has become the venture-backed default.

### Axis 2 — Speed

Timeline from decision to substantial completion.

- **Informal wind-down.** 2–12 weeks depending on the number of creditors to be settled and the state-law dissolution procedure. A Delaware §275 dissolution requires a stockholder vote (or written consent) plus a certificate of dissolution filed with the Secretary of State, then a §278 winding-up period that continues for at least three years for the pursuit and defence of claims.
- **Sale-for-parts.** As a step inside another mechanism, 4–12 weeks from marketing to close, sometimes compressed to 2–4 weeks in a "melting ice cube" situation where the assets lose value week by week.
- **ABC.** 4–8 weeks from the assignment agreement to substantial distribution, plus a longer tail (6–24 months) for claims resolution and residual distributions. The company itself is largely wound-up by the end of the first tranche.
- **Chapter 7.** 6+ months from filing to a first distribution to creditors; often 12–18 months to case closure. The trustee has statutory duties (341 meeting of creditors, asset investigation, proof-of-claim review) that structurally take time.
- **Chapter 11.** 12+ months from filing to plan confirmation for an average case; 6–9 months for a pre-packaged case; 3–4 months for a very compressed "Section 363 sale followed by dismissal or conversion" case. The plan-confirmation process (disclosure statement, plan solicitation, hearing, cramdown analysis, confirmation order) is inherently multi-month even at speed.

**Rough ranking** (fastest to slowest): Informal wind-down ≈ ABC < Chapter 7 < Chapter 11. Speed matters both because time consumes cash (professional fees, ongoing insurance and rent obligations) and because "melting ice cube" assets (customer contracts with change-of-control clauses, engineering-team goodwill, patent-application-in-flight status) can lose value materially over the wind-down timeline.

### Axis 3 — Creditor treatment

How efficiently the mechanism delivers value to creditors, and in what priority order.

- **Informal wind-down.** Depends on the negotiated settlements. Efficient if the company is solvent and every creditor is paid in full. Risky if creditors are being left short — a shorted creditor can later challenge the wind-down as a fraudulent transfer or as a breach of directors' zone-of-insolvency duties. Not recommended when the residual asset pool is insufficient to satisfy every creditor.
- **Sale-for-parts.** By itself, no creditor-distribution framework; the proceeds are distributed under whichever mechanism houses the sale.
- **ABC.** State-law priority framework (broadly tracks bankruptcy priorities: secured creditors → priority unsecured creditors (wages, taxes) → general unsecured creditors → stockholders). The assignee has fiduciary duties to the creditors and a statutory or contractual obligation to run the priority waterfall correctly. Historically the most efficient venture-backed-shutdown mechanism at delivering proceeds to creditors because the process is short (less consumed by professional fees) and the assignee is a specialist at running the sale process quickly.
- **Chapter 7.** Statutory priority framework under 11 U.S.C. §507 (administrative expenses first — including trustee and professional fees — then priority unsecured, then general unsecured, then equity). The trustee-driven investigation can produce additional recoveries via avoidance actions (preferences, fraudulent transfers) that increase the estate available to creditors, but the case takes longer and the professional fees consume more of the estate.
- **Chapter 11.** Statutory priority framework under §507, but with more flexibility (a plan can propose a different treatment for a specific class if the class consents, and the plan-confirmation process can approve deviations from strict priority through negotiated concessions). The professional-fee consumption of the estate is materially higher than in a Chapter 7 or ABC, and for a venture-backed company where the plan-confirmation is not preserving going-concern value the extra process cost typically eats what would otherwise be creditor recovery.

**Rough ranking** (most efficient delivery to creditors for a typical venture-backed shutdown): ABC > Chapter 7 > Chapter 11. Informal wind-down is efficient only when solvent; sale-for-parts is a tool used inside another mechanism.

### Axis 4 — Director-and-officer exposure

How the mechanism shapes the post-shutdown litigation exposure of the directors and officers who ran the company into it.

- **Informal wind-down.** No court-appointed fiduciary investigating the company's pre-shutdown affairs. But: (a) creditors left short can bring their own claims (breach of fiduciary duty in the zone of insolvency, fraudulent-transfer challenges); (b) if the wind-down is later found to have been improper, a subsequent involuntary bankruptcy petition can be filed by creditors under 11 U.S.C. §303 and a trustee installed. The absence of a court-appointed investigator is not the same as the absence of exposure.
- **Sale-for-parts.** The sale is a specific transaction subject to fiduciary-duty analysis (in the zone of insolvency the duty runs to the creditor class, not the stockholder class — see the [mod-109](../mod-109-transaction-governance-and-fiduciary-duty/README.md) treatment). The sale process needs to be defensible (market-check, arm's-length pricing, board approval with disinterested directors).
- **ABC.** The assignee has statutory or contractual duties to run the process well but does not have the same court-imposed investigatory duty a bankruptcy trustee has. Officers and directors are typically not the focus of assignee investigations in a well-run ABC, but the assignee will investigate transactions the assignee is concerned about (insider transfers in the pre-assignment period, for example). Post-close, ordinary directors-and-officers claims (securities, wrongful-termination, WARN, wage-and-hour) run under the same statutes as they would after any other shutdown; the ABC does not immunise them.
- **Chapter 7.** The trustee has an *affirmative statutory duty* to investigate the debtor's pre-petition affairs (11 U.S.C. §704(a)(4)) and to bring avoidance actions under §547 (preferences within 90 days pre-petition, or 1 year for insiders) and §548 (fraudulent transfers within 2 years pre-petition, with §544(b) reaching further via state fraudulent-transfer law). Insider transactions in the run-up to the filing are a specific focus. A well-designed pre-petition period (paying trust-fund taxes and wages first, avoiding preferential payments to insiders, avoiding transfers to related parties for less than reasonably-equivalent value) is the primary defence, but the *investigation itself* is a cost that ABC does not impose in the same form.
- **Chapter 11.** In a DIP structure the debtor-in-possession has the avoidance-action powers rather than a trustee, so the debtor's own management is nominally running the analysis. But: (a) the creditors' committee can pursue avoidance actions on behalf of the estate if the DIP is conflicted; (b) if a trustee is appointed under 11 U.S.C. §1104 the trustee assumes the investigatory role; (c) the case is public, disclosures are extensive, and the exposure surface is materially wider than in an ABC.

**Rough ranking** (least D&O exposure to most): Informal wind-down (only if creditors are paid in full) < ABC < Chapter 11 (DIP) < Chapter 7 (trustee) — but note the informal-wind-down ranking is *only* valid when the wind-down is solvent; in a distressed informal wind-down the exposure can jump to the top of the ranking because the officers were running the process without any of the safe-harbour discipline the other mechanisms provide.

### Axis 5 — Employee outcomes

How the mechanism treats employees on final pay, WARN notice, PTO payout, and severance.

- **Informal wind-down.** Employees are paid whatever the company can pay. In a solvent wind-down, they are paid in full and PTO is paid out per state law. WARN Act still applies if the workforce meets the coverage tests (100+ full-time employees in the aggregate, or a single site of 50+, with a mass layoff or plant closing) — the mechanism does not exempt the company from WARN. Severance is discretionary.
- **Sale-for-parts.** WARN Act obligations may attach to the *seller* (if the sale results in a mass layoff), the *buyer* (if the buyer commits to retain employees for at least the transitional period and then does not), or both. Successor-liability doctrine can extend the seller's WARN liability to the buyer in specific fact patterns.
- **ABC.** WARN Act applies to the same coverage tests. The assignee will pay wages already earned as a priority claim under state and (indirectly) federal law. PTO payout follows state law. COBRA administration continues with the pre-assignment plan sponsor (or, if the plan is terminated, the alternative COBRA-continuation rules apply).
- **Chapter 7.** WARN Act still applies — bankruptcy is not a general WARN exemption, though the "unforeseeable business circumstances" exception has been asserted with mixed success in specific fact patterns (chapter 4 develops this). Wages earned in the 180 days before filing up to a per-employee cap ($15,150 as of 2024, indexed for inflation — verify current cap at <https://www.uscourts.gov/services-forms/bankruptcy/bankruptcy-basics/priority-claims>) are priority claims under §507(a)(4). Employees whose wages are earned post-petition are administrative-expense claims and paid ahead of priority unsecured. PTO is treated similarly. Severance is generally a general unsecured claim unless it is a specifically-structured priority obligation.
- **Chapter 11.** Similar to Chapter 7 on wages, PTO, WARN. The DIP typically pays "critical" wage obligations under a first-day order to avoid workforce departures during the case. Severance may be structured under a Key Employee Retention Plan (KERP) or Key Employee Incentive Plan (KEIP) subject to court approval under 11 U.S.C. §503(c) — this is a specific and litigated area.

**Rough ranking** (best employee outcomes to worst, for a typical distressed venture-backed shutdown): Informal solvent wind-down > ABC ≈ Chapter 11 with first-day wage order > Chapter 7. Chapter 5 develops the state-by-state matrix that determines the actual final-pay obligations regardless of mechanism.

### Axis 6 — IP disposition

How the mechanism handles the intellectual-property estate — patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets, software source code, domain names, brand assets.

- **Informal wind-down.** The company can sell, assign, license-back, or abandon IP as ordinary corporate acts, subject to the same fiduciary-duty framework as any other transaction. If the wind-down is solvent, no external constraint. If not, the IP disposition is a fraudulent-transfer risk if it goes to insiders for less than reasonably-equivalent value.
- **Sale-for-parts.** The IP is typically the largest realisable asset in a venture-backed shutdown and is often the subject of the sale. Buyer-diligence on IP chain-of-title, licensor consents, and open-source-license compliance is compressed but real. Chapter 6 develops this decision.
- **ABC.** The assignee holds the IP as trust property and can sell it to a strategic buyer, license it back to founders or a spin-out, or abandon it. Because the assignee is running an arm's-length process, an insider purchase of the IP from the ABC (e.g., a founder-led spin-out that buys the IP from the assignee) can be a defensible transaction if the assignee runs a market-check. The pattern is common and well-documented.
- **Chapter 7.** The trustee holds the IP as estate property and disposes of it. Insider purchases from a Chapter 7 estate are possible but subject to trustee approval and (often) court approval under the debtor's plan or a specific §363 sale order — the process is more procedural and more public.
- **Chapter 11.** IP is estate property and dispositions are typically done through a §363 sale (chapter 3 develops the mechanics), with the buyer receiving the assets "free and clear" of liens and interests under §363(f) — a specific advantage over an out-of-court sale when the IP carries encumbrances.

**Rough ranking** (most flexible IP disposition to most procedural): Informal wind-down > ABC ≈ Chapter 11 §363 sale > Chapter 7. The Chapter 11 §363 sale has the "free and clear" advantage when the encumbrance-shape is complex; the ABC has the speed-and-cost advantage when the encumbrance-shape is simple.

## The decision-tree pattern

The framework is not a single flowchart because the specific mechanism turns on facts that vary. But the *sequence* of questions is stable and worth internalising:

**Question 1 — Is the company solvent?** If yes (residual assets ≥ all creditor claims, and cash flow is sufficient to complete the wind-down), an informal wind-down is on the table. If no (creditors will be left short on the current asset base), the informal wind-down is off the table and the mechanism will be ABC, Chapter 7, or Chapter 11.

**Question 2 — Is there going-concern value the reorganisation could preserve?** This is rare for venture-backed startups. If yes (a specific operating business that could exit reorganisation as a going concern under a plan), Chapter 11 with an operating plan is on the table. If no (the operating business does not have value beyond its asset-sale value), Chapter 11 is only on the table as a Section 363 sale vehicle, and the ABC-vs-Chapter-7-vs-Chapter-11-as-§363-vehicle comparison becomes the live question.

**Question 3 — Is there a specific §363-sale advantage that only Chapter 11 provides?** The most common reason to choose Chapter 11 over ABC for a venture-backed shutdown is that a specific buyer requires "free and clear of liens and interests" title under §363(f) that the ABC cannot deliver in the same statutory form. If yes, Chapter 11 with a §363 sale is the answer. If no, Chapter 11 is expensive process for no advantage.

**Question 4 — Are the pre-shutdown transactions clean enough for an ABC / self-managed wind-down?** If there are recent insider transfers, preferential payments, related-party transactions, or other transactions that a trustee-driven investigation would target, Chapter 7 offers no better protection (the trustee will find them) and ABC offers only marginally more privacy. Counsel needs to look hard at the pre-shutdown period before recommending the mechanism.

**Question 5 — Is there a specific creditor constituency that requires the automatic stay of §362?** If a specific creditor is bringing (or is about to bring) collection actions that the company cannot manage informally (a lawsuit, a UCC-1 foreclosure, a landlord eviction), only Chapter 7 or Chapter 11 delivers the automatic stay. ABC does not deliver a statutory automatic stay — though it does deliver a specific practical stay through the assignment agreement's transfer of the assets.

**Question 6 — What is the jurisdiction?** ABC is most-established in California under a well-developed statutory and case-law framework; it is available in Delaware (though administered under Delaware trust law, which is different from California's ABC framework — see chapter 2); it is available in most states in some form but with meaningfully varying procedural depth. Jurisdiction can move the mechanism ranking materially.

## Common misreads and how to avoid them

Three failure modes recur across venture-backed shutdowns:

- **"Bankruptcy is the formal way to close a company."** No. ABC is the state-law formal way to close a company for the benefit of its creditors, and it is faster, cheaper, and more efficient at delivering proceeds than Chapter 7 for a company that does not need the automatic stay or the §363-free-and-clear machinery. Naming this misread in the decision conversation surfaces the assumption that would otherwise drive the choice.
- **"We should do Chapter 11 to preserve optionality."** Almost always wrong for a venture-backed startup. Chapter 11 is expensive process; the "optionality" a Ch 11 preserves is the ability to confirm a plan of reorganisation, which requires going-concern value that most shutdown-candidate startups do not have. The specific defensible reason to file Ch 11 is a §363-free-and-clear sale that a specific buyer requires; if that is not present, the Ch 11 is consuming money the ABC or Ch 7 would have distributed to creditors.
- **"We can just wind down informally and skip the mechanisms."** Only if the company is solvent. Otherwise, the informal wind-down is a *choice to leave creditors' claims unresolved*, which (a) subjects the directors and officers to zone-of-insolvency fiduciary-duty exposure (chapter 9 develops the personal-liability layer), (b) subjects specific pre-shutdown transactions to fraudulent-transfer challenge, and (c) can be forced into a bankruptcy by an involuntary petition filed by creditors under §303. The "informal" wind-down of an insolvent company is not informal — it is exposed.

## What the framework produces

A founder-CEO, CFO, or GC with the framework installed does not walk into the counsel meeting knowing the answer. They walk in knowing:

- Which of the six axes are the load-bearing ones for their specific facts (cost, speed, creditor treatment, D&O exposure, employee outcomes, IP disposition).
- What data the framework needs to make the axis-scoring meaningful (residual asset pool, creditor list with amounts and priority classifications, employee count and locations, secured-vs-unsecured-debt shape, jurisdiction, IP encumbrance-shape, buyer interest).
- Which of the six questions in the decision-tree pattern will discriminate among the mechanisms for this specific situation.
- Which pre-shutdown transactions in the last 90 days (or 1 year for insiders under §547, or 2 years under §548) need to be reviewed with counsel before recommending a mechanism that would trigger a trustee-driven investigation.

That preparation converts the counsel conversation from "explain the mechanisms to us and tell us what to do" (a many-hour education spend) to "here is our profile, here is our axis reading, help us confirm the mechanism and start execution" (a specific judgment ask that respects both parties' time). The rest of this module develops the depth on each branch so the framework's outputs are actionable.

## Summary

There are five shutdown mechanisms — informal wind-down, sale-for-parts, Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors, Chapter 7, Chapter 11 — and the choice among them turns on six axes: cost, speed, creditor treatment, D&O exposure, employee outcomes, IP disposition. For a typical venture-backed shutdown the framework's default answer is ABC (fastest, cheapest, most-efficient creditor delivery, workable IP disposition), with Chapter 11 as a §363-sale vehicle when a specific buyer requires the free-and-clear machinery, Chapter 7 when the company cannot manage the pre-petition period well enough for a self-managed mechanism, and informal wind-down only when the company is solvent. The next chapter develops the ABC branch in the depth a founder-CEO, CFO, and GC need to run one.
