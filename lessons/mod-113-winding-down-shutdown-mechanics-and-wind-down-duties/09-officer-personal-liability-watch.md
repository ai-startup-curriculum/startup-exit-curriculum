# Officer and Director Personal-Liability Watch

## Why this matters

Corporate limited liability is the default — the directors and officers of a Delaware or California or Washington corporation are generally not personally liable for the corporation's debts. The default is not absolute. A specific set of statutes, imposed in a specific set of fact patterns, pierces the corporate shield and reaches the specific officer or director as a *personally liable person*. In a shutdown — when the company is running out of cash, is paying some creditors but not others, is making specific payment-sequencing decisions under acute pressure, and is terminating employees under compressed timelines — several of those statutes are at their most live. A shutdown that pays general unsecured creditors (vendors) before paying trust-fund taxes (federal payroll withholding) or wages, or that defers employee final-pay past the state-law deadline, or that cancels the retirement plan without the ERISA fiduciary process, can produce a specific personal-liability exposure to the CEO, CFO, and GC that *survives the dissolution of the entity* and that *the D&O tail may not fully cover* (specific exclusions, specific sub-limits, specific carve-outs for intentional acts).

The remedy is a specific *watch* — a specific set of risks the founder-CEO, CFO, and GC are tracking continuously through the shutdown, with specific pay-order discipline (trust-fund taxes and wages first, before general unsecured) and specific documentation discipline (board resolutions, counsel-blessed pay-order memos, specific minute-book entries that evidence the specific business-judgement reasoning). Chapter 8 developed the D&O tail — the insurance that responds when a claim is made. This chapter develops the specific statutes that generate the claims that the tail (and specifically-exclusion-tested parts of the tail) must respond to, and the specific operational discipline that keeps the claims from being brought in the first place.

## The pay-first discipline

Before the specific statutes, the single operating principle. In a shutdown where the asset pool will not pay every creditor in full, the officer-and-director personal-liability layer imposes a specific *pay-order* on the officers:

1. **Trust-fund taxes first.** Federal payroll withholding (the employee's income tax and the employee's share of FICA), state payroll withholding (where applicable), sales tax (where applicable). These are not the company's money — they are the employees' or customers' money the company is holding in trust. Failing to remit them triggers IRC §6672 personal liability on the "responsible person."
2. **Wages second.** Unpaid wages for work already performed. Many state wage-and-hour statutes impose officer personal liability for unpaid wages; FLSA has its own officer-personal-liability doctrine; California PAGA layers an additional civil-penalty regime on top of the ordinary wage claim.
3. **Priority unsecured claims third.** Specific statutory priorities (certain tax obligations, certain employee benefit contributions, specific landlord claims, specific trust-fund-type obligations with specific statutory priority).
4. **Secured creditors fourth** (to the extent of the collateral value).
5. **General unsecured creditors fifth.** Trade payables, non-priority tax, non-priority judgment creditors.
6. **Equity sixth** (preferred then common per the certificate of designation).

The discipline is counter-intuitive: in a shutdown where general unsecured creditors are going to be paid in full under an informal wind-down, the pay-order discipline says *still* pay the trust-fund taxes and wages first, in advance of any general unsecured payment, because those are the only categories that reach the officers personally if left unpaid.

The specific exception: if secured creditors have perfected security interests in specific collateral, and the collateral value exceeds the secured claim, the secured creditor takes its collateral first. The pay-first discipline above operates on the *free* assets, not the secured collateral. In an ABC or Chapter 7, the assignee or trustee runs the waterfall; in an informal wind-down or distressed sale-for-parts, the officers run it. The officers who run it correctly protect their personal position; the officers who do not, do not.

## IRC §6672 — the trust-fund recovery penalty

Internal Revenue Code §6672 imposes a 100% penalty — equal to the full amount of the trust-fund tax that was not paid over — on any person who (a) is a "responsible person" with respect to the tax, and (b) "willfully" failed to collect, truthfully account for, and pay over the tax. The penalty is *not* a tax on the entity; it is a personal liability of the responsible person, enforceable against the person's assets, with the same lien and collection powers the IRS exercises against taxpayers for income tax owed.

### What are "trust-fund taxes"?

- **Federal income tax withheld from employee wages.** The amount the company withheld from each paycheck but had not yet remitted to the IRS.
- **The employee's share of FICA (Social Security and Medicare).** The 7.65% withheld from each paycheck.
- Note the employer's own share of FICA is *not* a trust-fund tax; it is a company liability but not a trust-fund tax under §6672. Similarly the Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA) tax is a company liability but not trust-fund. The §6672 penalty applies only to the amounts held in trust.
- **Collected excise taxes** in some specific industries (airline ticket tax, communications excise, specific other excise taxes). Narrow applicability but can be live in specific companies.

### What is a "responsible person"?

The term is specifically broader than "the person who signed the cheque." Under *Slodov v. United States*, 436 U.S. 238 (1978) and its progeny, a responsible person is anyone with "the status, duty, and authority to collect, account for, and pay over the trust-fund taxes." Specific factors courts consider:

- Signature authority on the company's bank accounts.
- Authority to hire and fire employees.
- Authority to decide which creditors to pay.
- Participation in day-to-day management.
- Position (CEO, CFO, Treasurer, Controller — each at structural risk).

Multiple persons can be "responsible persons" simultaneously. In a shutting-down company the CEO, CFO, Treasurer, and sometimes the Controller or the specific person running the payroll are all at risk. The IRS pursues all responsible persons jointly and severally for the full 100% — any one of them can be made to pay the full amount, with contribution-rights among them.

### What is "willful"?

Willfulness under §6672 is not criminal; it is a specific civil standard. The responsible person "willfully" fails to pay the tax when the person knows the tax is owed and nevertheless pays other creditors instead. There is no requirement of a bad motive, no requirement of specific intent to defraud. Under *United States v. Pomponio*, 429 U.S. 10 (1976) and later cases, willfulness is established by the specific choice to pay other creditors in preference to the trust-fund tax, with knowledge of the obligation.

The specific pattern in a shutdown is specifically a willfulness pattern: the CEO knows the payroll taxes are owed, knows the vendors are also owed, and chooses to pay the vendors to keep operations running. That specific choice is specifically willful under §6672.

### The specific defence

The defence is pay-first. Trust-fund taxes get paid in full before any general unsecured creditor receives a payment. In a cash-constrained shutdown, this often means specific vendors do not get paid. The IRS does not accept "we needed to keep the lights on" as a defence. The courts have been unambiguous: *Slodov* and progeny read §6672 to require the responsible person to prioritize trust-fund tax payment.

If the trust-fund tax has already been incurred but not paid at the time the shutdown decision is made, the specific priority is immediate — pay the accrued trust-fund tax from available cash before any payment to a non-trust-fund creditor. If the cash is insufficient to pay the full accrued trust-fund tax, pay as much as possible and document the specific reason the balance cannot be paid.

The specific documentation: a specific board or officer memo that specifically identifies the trust-fund tax obligation, the specific available cash, the specific decision to pay the trust-fund tax in priority, and the specific reasoning. This memo is a specific defence to a §6672 willfulness analysis and specifically evidences the officers' specific attention to the obligation.

## PAGA — California Private Attorneys General Act

California Labor Code §§2698 et seq. (Private Attorneys General Act of 2004) authorises an aggrieved employee to bring a civil action on behalf of the state of California to recover civil penalties for Labor Code violations. The specific civil penalties — specifically $100 per employee per pay period for an initial violation and $200 per employee per pay period for a subsequent violation — specifically compound rapidly across a workforce.

### Why PAGA is a personal-liability exposure

PAGA actions are typically brought against the specific employing entity, but California Labor Code §558.1 (added in 2015) specifically extends PAGA liability to any "owner, director, officer, or managing agent of the employer" who "violates, or causes to be violated" specific Labor Code provisions governing wages, hours, and working conditions.

The specific effect: the CEO, CFO, HR officer, or specific operating officer of the shutting-down California company who specifically caused a specific Labor Code violation (unpaid final pay, failed meal-and-rest-break, misclassified-worker, specific wage-statement-defect) can be personally liable for the PAGA civil penalties, independent of the entity's own liability.

### Common PAGA triggers in a shutdown

- **Unpaid final pay beyond the statutory deadline.** California Labor Code §201 requires immediate payment of all wages on termination (if the company terminates); §202 requires payment within 72 hours of resignation (if the employee resigns). Failure to comply triggers Labor Code §203 "waiting-time penalties" of up to 30 days of wages per employee, plus the PAGA civil penalty on top.
- **PTO not paid out at termination.** California Labor Code §227.3 treats accrued vested vacation as wages that must be paid at termination. Failure triggers the same §203 penalty plus PAGA on top.
- **Final wage statement defects.** Labor Code §226 requires specific wage-statement content; defects trigger specific penalties.
- **Mass-termination without the California WARN notice.** California Labor Code §§1400–1408 (California WARN).

### The specific defence

Pay all employees in full on the termination date, with the correct final-pay calculation, with a compliant wage statement, with full PTO payout. Do not defer any wage payment to a general unsecured category — wages are a specific pay-first category under the operating principle at the top of this chapter.

In the specific case where the company does not have enough cash to pay all wages in full, the specific allocation decision is a specific business-judgement decision with specific legal consequences and must be made with specific employment counsel. The specific risk of under-paying some employees to pay others in full is specifically a PAGA (and specifically §558.1 personal-liability) risk and specifically a FLSA / state wage-and-hour risk.

## WARN Act personal liability

The federal WARN Act (29 U.S.C. §§2101–2109) and the state mini-WARN statutes (California, New York, Illinois, New Jersey, and others) impose specific damages liability on employers that fail to give the required advance notice of a plant closing or mass layoff. The ordinary liability is on the employer; several specific state mini-WARN statutes extend liability (and in some cases civil penalties) to the officers and directors.

### California mini-WARN

California Labor Code §§1400–1408 — the specific statute requires 60 days' notice for a mass layoff of 50+ employees in a 30-day period. Damages are back pay for each day of violation plus specific civil penalties up to $500 per day under §1403. The civil penalties can be collected against the "employer," which the California courts have specifically read to include specific officers in specific fact patterns.

### New York WARN

New York Labor Law §§860–860-i — specifically more stringent than the federal WARN (90 days' notice; 25-employee threshold; 33% workforce threshold). Specific liability includes officers who were specifically responsible for the WARN decision.

### Federal WARN personal liability

The federal WARN Act does not specifically authorise officer-personal-liability by statute. But (a) specific state fiduciary-duty principles can reach officers whose specific WARN-decision breached their specific duties; (b) specific federal courts have specifically pierced the corporate shield in specific WARN cases where the parent-and-subsidiary structure was specifically used to evade the Act; (c) specific jurisdictions have specifically applied specific "single-employer" doctrines to reach specific parent-company officers.

### The specific defence

Issue WARN notices on the specific statutory timeline (60 days federal; 90 days New York; specific state timelines for other mini-WARN jurisdictions). If the "faltering company" or "unforeseeable business circumstance" exceptions are claimed, document the specific reasoning at the specific time with specific board approval and specific counsel review.

Specific pattern recognition: a shutdown that specifically compresses the notice period to zero days without a specific defensible exception specifically generates specific WARN exposure, specific state-mini-WARN exposure, and specific officer-personal-liability exposure in specific jurisdictions. The specific "we don't have the cash to run payroll for 60 more days" defence is specifically not a defence to WARN — the Act requires the notice, and the cash shortfall is specifically what the Act was designed to address.
