# D&O Tail Coverage Procurement

## Why this matters

Directors-and-officers liability insurance is a *claims-made* policy — it responds to claims first made against an insured during the policy period, not to acts that occurred during the policy period. When the entity dissolves, the ordinary D&O programme lapses with it: the renewal does not happen because there is no entity to renew, and the final policy period runs out on its stated expiration date. The claims that fire *after* the entity dissolves — the plaintiff-firm WARN complaint filed six months after the shutdown announcement, the SEC Rule 10b-5 action brought by a disappointed investor eighteen months after the last 10-K, the wrongful-termination action from the senior engineer who was walked out on shutdown day — do not have a policy to respond to unless the board has specifically purchased a **run-off** (also called a **tail**, **extended reporting period**, or **ERP**) endorsement before the policy expires.

The tail endorsement is a one-time premium paid at policy expiration that extends the *reporting* period — not the policy period — for a defined number of years (customarily six for a public-company D&O policy, though three-year, five-year, and ten-year tails exist). Any claim first made against an insured during the extended reporting period, for a wrongful act that occurred before the policy expiration date, is treated as if the claim had been first made during the policy period. For the dissolved entity's former directors and officers, the tail is the only mechanism that keeps the D&O coverage *responsive* to post-dissolution claims. The policy cannot be purchased after the fact: once the policy expires without a tail, the window closes and no insurer will underwrite a retroactive tail on a dissolved company.

This chapter installs the discipline a founder-CEO, CFO, GC, and board chair need to procure the tail. It is not a substitute for an insurance broker — every shutdown engages a specific broker and, often, specific coverage counsel on the specific policy language. It is the substrate on which the broker conversation sits.

> Reminder: this module is education, not insurance or legal advice. The specific policy forms vary materially among carriers; the specific tail terms vary materially among transactions; the specific coverage counsel reviews the specific policy for the specific exposures. This chapter describes the shape of the decision; it does not substitute for engagement of a broker and (where appropriate) coverage counsel.

## How claims-made coverage works

A claims-made policy responds when two things are both true in the same policy period:

1. The *claim* is first made against an insured during the policy period (or the extended reporting period if a tail is purchased).
2. The underlying *wrongful act* occurred on or after the policy's **retroactive date** (typically the inception of continuous D&O coverage, often many years before the current policy) and before the policy expiration.

For an operating company this architecture is intuitive — each year's policy is renewed, each renewal typically preserves the original retroactive date through continuity-of-coverage endorsements, and the overlapping-period issue is managed through specific notice-of-circumstances provisions that let an insured notify the current carrier of a pre-expiration circumstance that becomes a claim later.

For a shutting-down company the architecture is a trap. The policy expires at the entity's dissolution (or at the next renewal date, whichever comes first), and from that date forward:

- Claims made after expiration are not covered under the expired policy. The policy's reporting period has ended.
- No new policy will be issued to the dissolved entity. There is no insured to renew.
- Individual former directors and officers who are later sued have no D&O policy to tender a claim to, absent a tail.

The tail endorsement rewires the reporting period. It does not change the retroactive date or the policy period for *wrongful acts*. It only changes the period during which a *claim* can first be made and still be covered. A six-year tail on a policy expiring on 2027-06-30 says, in effect: a claim first made on 2032-04-15 for a wrongful act that occurred during the policy period (2026-07-01 to 2027-06-30) is treated as if the claim had been first made on 2027-06-30 — i.e., it is covered.

## The six-year convention

Public-company and venture-backed-company D&O tails are typically procured for **six years** following the policy expiration. The convention is not arbitrary: it tracks the longest-tail claims the policy must respond to.

- **Securities fraud (Section 10(b), Rule 10b-5).** The statute of repose under 28 U.S.C. §1658(b)(2) is five years from the date of the violation. Six years provides a buffer for discovery-rule disputes and for the lag between a wrongful act and the first filing.
- **Section 11 Securities Act claims.** The statute of repose is three years from the bona fide offering under Section 13 of the Securities Act. For a company that had an IPO or a subsequent registered offering before the shutdown, the three-year clock begins at the offering date and six years from policy expiration captures the full exposure window for a late-vintage offering.
- **Delaware fiduciary-duty claims.** Three-year statute of limitations under 10 Del. C. §8106, extendable in certain circumstances.
- **ERISA fiduciary-breach.** Six years from the date of the last action constituting a breach, with a three-year "actual knowledge" variant, under 29 U.S.C. §1113.
- **WARN Act claims.** Two-year statute of limitations under the DOL's standard application (the Act itself does not specify, and courts have applied the most-analogous state statute, typically producing a two-year window).
- **State wage-and-hour claims.** Vary by state — California PAGA has one year with specific tolling provisions, FLSA has two years (three for willful violations) under 29 U.S.C. §255.

A six-year tail captures essentially all of these windows. Shorter tails (three or five years) leave specific exposures uncovered; longer tails (seven to ten years) exist but add material premium and typically do not materially increase the share of claims captured.

Some companies choose a shorter tail (three years) with specific reasoning — a company with limited securities-law exposure, no ERISA plan, and a clean recent operating history may rationally choose a cheaper three-year tail. The reasoning should be specific and documented. "We bought three years because it was cheaper" without an exposure-mapping is not a defensible choice; the fiduciary duty of the board to secure directors' and officers' indemnification extends through the wind-up and the exposure period.

## Pricing — the 200–300% rule of thumb

The industry convention for tail pricing on a six-year run-off is **200–300% of the annual premium** at policy expiration. For a company paying $150,000 per year in D&O premium, the six-year tail premium is typically quoted in the $300,000–$450,000 range.

The specific number varies with:

- **Policy limits.** The tail generally preserves the same aggregate limit as the underlying policy for the entire extended reporting period (not a fresh limit for each year of the tail) — so a $5 million aggregate on the expiring policy becomes a $5 million aggregate across the full six-year tail. Underwriters price for the exposure extension at that limit.
- **Carrier and tower structure.** A single-carrier policy is simpler to tail than a tower of primary and excess carriers, each of whom must separately agree to run-off the layer at a specific premium. Towers are the norm for public-company D&O, and each layer carries its own tail quote.
- **Transaction type.** A tail procured as part of an acquisition (buyer purchases the target's company's run-off from the seller's carrier) prices differently from a tail procured as part of an insolvent shutdown. Acquirer-funded tails on healthy sold companies price lower; distressed-shutdown tails price higher because the carrier anticipates more claims.
- **Loss history.** A company with recent claim notices (an SEC inquiry, a pending wage-and-hour matter, a former-employee demand letter) attracts a higher tail premium than one with a clean history.
- **Carrier appetite.** Some carriers specifically price distressed-run-off business attractively (because they already have the risk on their books and want to retain it); others price it as discouragement. A broker who knows the market knows which carrier is likely to quote competitively.

Specific recent anecdotal data points from the D&O broker canon:

- A late-stage venture-backed company with a $5M primary D&O limit paying $75K-$120K per year typically sees a six-year tail quoted in the $180K-$300K range.
- A newly-public company with a $10M primary limit and multi-carrier excess paying $300K-$600K per year typically sees a six-year tail quoted in the $750K-$1.5M range (the premium concentrates toward the primary and the first excess layer).
- A pre-IPO decacorn with a $25M limit paying $1M+ per year typically sees a tail in the $2M-$4M range.

Verify current pricing with Marsh, Aon, WTW, Woodruff Sawyer, or Newfront — the market shifts annually with the broader D&O cycle, and the 200–300% convention is a *starting* reference, not a quote.

## What the tail responds to

The tail preserves the coverage of the underlying policy for claims first made during the extended reporting period. The core coverage grants in a modern D&O policy — subject to policy-specific wording — include:

### Side A — individual indemnity (non-indemnifiable loss)

Covers the directors' and officers' personal loss when the company does not indemnify them. The most important Side A context is corporate insolvency — a dissolved or insolvent company cannot indemnify, so Side A becomes the only source of individual-director coverage. Side A is typically broader than Side B / Side C in two specific ways: (a) it has a lower deductible or no deductible, and (b) it has fewer exclusions, particularly around derivative-suit treatment.

For a dissolved company, Side A is the load-bearing coverage. The tail should preserve Side A at the full policy limit, with the "non-indemnifiable loss" trigger clear in the tail endorsement.

### Side B — corporate reimbursement

Covers the company's indemnification of its directors and officers. For a dissolved company this coverage is largely academic — there is no company to be reimbursed. The tail premium does not need to be allocated heavily to Side B.

### Side C — entity coverage (for public companies)

Covers the company itself for securities-law claims (Section 10(b), Section 11, Section 12). For a dissolved public company this is still live — the entity may be dissolved but the securities-law claims against it do not disappear; they continue against the estate or against specific successor entities.

### Side D — regulatory investigations

Covers specific regulatory investigations (SEC Wells notice, SEC subpoena, DOJ grand-jury-subpoena-leading-to-settlement) in some policies. If the company has specific SEC exposure (securities offerings during the policy period, public-company status, dual-track IPO workstream before the shutdown), Side D or the equivalent is worth confirming in the tail.

### Specific claim types the tail is intended to cover

For a shutting-down company the primary tail-covered claims are:

- **Securities fraud (Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5).** The dissolved company has no entity to defend, but the directors and officers do. A late-vintage 10b-5 action for pre-shutdown statements fires squarely into the tail.
- **Securities Act Section 11 / Section 12 claims.** For any registered offering before the shutdown (IPO, follow-on, convertible-note registration), the three-year statute of repose captures these within a six-year tail.
- **Breach of fiduciary duty.** Delaware §8106's three-year statute of limitations, with specific "zone of insolvency" claim theories that arise specifically because the company became insolvent.
- **Fiduciary duty in the zone of insolvency.** The specific creditor-side claim theories under *North American Catholic Educational Programming Found., Inc. v. Gheewalla*, 930 A.2d 92 (Del. 2007) and *Credit Lyonnais Bank Nederland, N.V. v. Pathe Communications Corp.*, 1991 WL 277613 (Del. Ch. Dec. 30, 1991) — see [mod-109](../mod-109-transaction-governance-and-fiduciary-duty/README.md) for the doctrinal treatment. The tail is particularly load-bearing for these claims because they typically fire *after* the dissolution and are brought by creditors or their representatives.
- **Wrongful termination.** Specific claims from employees walked out on shutdown day — age discrimination (ADEA), race / gender discrimination (Title VII), disability (ADA), retaliation claims. The ordinary D&O employment-practices-liability (EPL) module (if the policy has one, which is common for venture-backed D&O) picks these up.
- **WARN Act.** Federal and state WARN claims brought by terminated employees for the 60-day notice period. The tail should specifically preserve coverage for WARN under the EPL section.
- **Wage-and-hour / FLSA / state wage-and-hour.** PAGA in California, FLSA federal, state wage-and-hour. Some D&O policies specifically carve-out wage-and-hour claims; the tail procurement is the specific moment to interrogate the carve-out and (where possible) negotiate it narrow.
- **ERISA fiduciary breach.** Specific claims brought under 29 U.S.C. §1109 for breach of ERISA fiduciary duty in retirement-plan administration. ERISA liability often has its own fiduciary liability policy (sometimes called a "Fiduciary Liability" or "ERISA Liability" policy, distinct from the main D&O) — the tail procurement should cover both.

### What the tail typically does NOT respond to

- **Claims first made before the policy expiration.** These should have been noticed to the carrier during the policy period under the ordinary notice provisions; the tail does not revive expired notice obligations.
- **Claims arising from wrongful acts after the policy expiration.** The policy period for wrongful acts ends at expiration; subsequent acts are not covered. For a dissolved entity this is typically academic (no one is acting under the entity's authority post-dissolution), but it can be live if a specific former officer is engaged in a specific wind-up activity that could constitute a wrongful act.
- **Fraudulent acts (fraud, personal profit, deliberate criminal act).** Standard exclusions. The tail does not change the exclusions; it preserves them.
- **Claims under specific policy-level exclusions.** Insured-vs-insured, prior-litigation, specific-regulatory-matter — standard exclusions that the tail preserves.

The specific exclusions are policy-form-specific and should be reviewed with coverage counsel before the tail is bound.

## Timing — the non-substitutability window

The tail must be bound **before** the policy expires. There is no mechanism to buy a tail retroactively on a dissolved entity. The policy expires on its stated expiration date; the entity dissolves on its certificate-of-dissolution date; the tail decision is made in advance of both.

The practical timeline:

1. **90–120 days before policy expiration:** engage the broker, request tail quotes, review the specific policy forms for tail-election provisions. Some policies specify that the tail must be elected within 30 days of the policy expiration or forfeited; many allow 60 days; some are open-ended but require written election. Reading the specific endorsement language is the starting point.
2. **60–90 days before policy expiration:** receive tail quotes from the carriers. Compare pricing, specific coverage grants, specific exclusions, specific endorsement language.
3. **30–60 days before policy expiration:** finalize the tail selection, negotiate specific language (if available — on some carriers the tail is take-it-or-leave-it; on others specific endorsements are negotiable), secure board approval of the tail procurement as a specific board resolution.
4. **At or before policy expiration:** bind the tail. The specific binding mechanism varies — some carriers issue a specific endorsement; some issue a specific run-off policy document.
5. **After policy expiration:** the tail is in force. Any claim first made during the tail period on a covered wrongful act is noticed to the carrier under the tail terms.

The alignment with the shutdown mechanism:

- **Informal wind-down.** The certificate of dissolution is filed at a specific date; the policy may continue to its ordinary expiration date before the dissolution. The tail procurement runs on the policy's schedule, which may be in advance of the dissolution.
- **ABC.** The assignment occurs at a specific date; the policy continues until expiration (which may be after the assignment). The ABC often does not immediately dissolve the entity — the entity remains in existence as a legal matter while the assignee holds the assets. The tail procurement is still on the policy schedule, but the board authorising the tail is making the decision in advance of the policy expiration.
- **Chapter 7.** The petition is filed at a specific date. The ordinary D&O policy continues through the bankruptcy case; the tail is procured at the policy's ordinary expiration. If the bankruptcy continues past the policy expiration, the tail covers the post-expiration period. The specific interaction with the automatic stay and with the trustee is coverage-counsel territory.
- **Chapter 11.** Similar to Chapter 7. The DIP typically continues the D&O programme through the case under a first-day motion; the tail is procured at the policy expiration, which may be during the case or after the plan confirmation.

The specific failure mode: a founder-CEO who procrastinates the tail decision past the policy expiration date discovers that the carrier will not retroactively bind. The exposure for the former directors and officers runs from that point forward with no coverage. The specific lesson is that the tail procurement is a *calendar event* driven by the policy expiration, not a *waiting event* driven by the shutdown execution.

## Side A difference-in-conditions (DIC) and the independent-director layer

Beyond the main D&O programme, many venture-backed and public companies carry a specific Side A DIC policy — a dedicated Side A-only policy that sits above the main programme and specifically covers independent directors for non-indemnifiable loss when the main programme fails (specific exclusion, carrier insolvency, exhausted limit).

For a shutting-down company the Side A DIC is particularly load-bearing for independent directors — the specific board members whose personal exposure is most at risk after the entity dissolves. The tail procurement should extend to the Side A DIC as well as the main programme.

The Side A DIC tail is typically quoted on a similar 200–300% basis, with specific pricing driven by the specific carrier (Chubb, Berkshire Hathaway Specialty, AIG, Allied World, and others have specific Side A DIC offerings) and the specific exposure profile.

For independent directors joining a company that may face a shutdown, the Side A DIC is often a specific condition of the independent director accepting the seat — and the Side A DIC tail is a specific condition of their departure. The specific board resolution procuring the tail should name the Side A DIC specifically.

## The specific board resolution

The board authorises the tail procurement by resolution. The specific resolution typically:

- **Names the specific policy or policies** being tailed (the primary D&O, each excess layer, the Side A DIC, the fiduciary liability policy if separate, the employment-practices-liability policy if separate).
- **Names the tail term** (typically six years, or three or ten depending on the specific decision).
- **Names the tail premium** (specific aggregate amount, or specific aggregate with specific allocation across the carriers).
- **Authorises the specific officer** (typically the CFO or GC) to bind the tail.
- **Includes a specific finding** that the tail procurement is in the best interests of the company and consistent with the directors' and officers' indemnification rights under the specific charter / bylaws / indemnification agreements.
- **Records the specific advice** of the broker and (where appropriate) coverage counsel.

The resolution is a specific document in the minute book. The minute book records the specific board deliberation and the specific business judgment supporting the decision — specific minute-book discipline is itself a specific element of the officer-personal-liability defence (chapter 9).

## Specific pitfalls

Specific recurring failure modes in shutting-down-company tail procurement:

### Pitfall 1 — procrastinating past the election window

The tail-election window closes. The tail cannot be bound. The former directors and officers have no coverage. The specific lesson: calendar the tail-election window at the start of the shutdown workstream.

### Pitfall 2 — buying the wrong term

A three-year tail on a company with Section 10(b) exposure leaves the back three years of the statute of repose uncovered. A six-year tail on a company with no securities exposure, no ERISA exposure, and a clean operating history may be more coverage than needed. The specific decision is a specific exposure-mapping, not a default.

### Pitfall 3 — forgetting the layers

The primary D&O is tailed but the first excess layer is not. The former directors have primary coverage up to the primary limit and then no coverage at all. Each layer of the tower must be tailed separately.

### Pitfall 4 — forgetting Side A DIC

The main programme is tailed but the Side A DIC is not. Independent directors who were specifically protected by the Side A DIC during the operating period lose that protection in the tail period. The Side A DIC must be tailed separately.

### Pitfall 5 — forgetting the ERISA / Fiduciary Liability / EPL policies

The main D&O is tailed but the separate Fiduciary Liability policy (for ERISA exposure) and the separate EPL policy (for employment claims) are not. The ERISA and employment claims — which are the most likely post-shutdown claims — are then uncovered. Every management-liability policy in the stack needs its own tail decision.

### Pitfall 6 — "the acquirer will handle it"

In a shutdown that includes a sale-for-parts, the acquirer may be purchasing the operating assets but not the dissolved entity's historical liabilities. The acquirer's tail arrangement, if any, covers the acquirer's directors and officers — not the sold company's former directors and officers. The dissolving entity's board must procure its own tail; the acquirer's indemnity or tail does not substitute.

### Pitfall 7 — relying on the §102(b)(7) charter exculpation and indemnification alone

The DGCL §102(b)(7) charter exculpation of directors covers the duty of care but not the duty of loyalty, and the specific indemnification agreements run against the entity. For a dissolved entity, the indemnification agreement is only as good as the entity's remaining assets (if any) to indemnify from. The §102(b)(7) exculpation does not substitute for insurance. The tail is the insurance; the §102(b)(7) is the backup; the indemnification agreement is the policy wrapper. All three run together, and each has limits.

### Pitfall 8 — not reading the fine print

Specific tail endorsements have specific limitations — specific-exclusion carvebacks, specific-aggregate-limit shared across the extended reporting period rather than refreshed, specific anti-stacking provisions that prevent claims from accessing multiple policies. The specific endorsement language should be read with the specific coverage counsel before binding.

## Summary

D&O tail coverage is a one-time, pre-expiration purchase that extends the reporting period of the expiring D&O policy for a defined term (customarily six years) so that claims first made against former directors and officers after the entity dissolves can still be covered. The convention is a six-year tail priced at 200–300% of the annual premium, covering securities fraud, breach of fiduciary duty in the zone of insolvency, wrongful termination, WARN, wage-and-hour, and ERISA claims that typically fire after dissolution. The procurement is calendar-driven by the policy expiration date, not by the shutdown execution — missing the election window is a non-recoverable error. The specific board resolution authorises the tail across the main D&O programme, each excess layer, the Side A DIC, the Fiduciary Liability policy, and the EPL policy; each must be separately tailed. The next chapter develops the officer-and-director personal-liability watch — the specific statutes that can impose personal liability on officers and directors regardless of insurance, and the specific pay-order discipline that defuses them.
