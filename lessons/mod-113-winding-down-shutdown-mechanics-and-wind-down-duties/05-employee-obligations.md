# Employee Obligations — Final Pay, PTO, COBRA, Severance, Non-Competes

## Why this matters

The WARN Act (chapter 4) is only one of the employee-side workstreams in a shutdown. Layered under it are the state-specific final-pay obligations (some states require payment on the day of termination; others allow the next scheduled payroll), the state-specific PTO-payout obligations (some states require payout of accrued PTO; others allow employer forfeiture policies), the federal-and-state COBRA continuation-coverage obligations for health benefits, the discretionary or statutory severance question, and the enforceability of non-competes and non-disclosures post-shutdown — a landscape reshaped materially in 2024 by the FTC's non-compete-ban rule and the ongoing federal-court litigation over it.

Each of these workstreams has a specific statutory or contractual base, a specific state-by-state variation, and a specific pattern of officer-and-director personal-liability exposure when the workstream is mishandled. The wage-and-hour framework in particular — California's Labor Code §201 immediate-payment rule, PAGA under Labor Code §2698 et seq., FLSA under 29 U.S.C. §201 et seq., ERISA fiduciary duty under 29 U.S.C. §1104 — is one of the highest-frequency litigation exposures post-shutdown. Chapter 9 develops the personal-liability layer; this chapter installs the substantive employee-side workstreams that the personal-liability layer sits on top of.

> Reminder: state employment law varies materially. The specific final-pay timing, PTO-payout requirement, COBRA administration, severance obligation, and non-compete enforceability for a specific workforce depend on the specific state each employee works in — not the state of the company's incorporation. Every live shutdown engages employment counsel who reviews the specific workforce composition state-by-state.

## Final pay — the state-by-state matrix

The most immediate and highest-personal-liability-risk workstream. Final pay is *not* discretionary — every state has a statutory framework governing when wages earned but not yet paid must be paid on termination, and non-compliance produces penalties that in some jurisdictions escalate rapidly.

### California — the strictest regime

California Labor Code §201: employees who are discharged must be paid all wages *immediately at the time of termination*. Labor Code §202: employees who quit with at least 72 hours' notice must be paid at the time of quitting; employees who quit with less than 72 hours' notice must be paid within 72 hours of the notice.

California Labor Code §203: for wilful failure to pay under §§201–202, waiting-time penalties are assessed at the employee's daily wage for each day the payment is late, up to 30 days. This penalty compounds — a $500/day employee who is paid 30 days late owes the company $15,000 in penalties in addition to the wages.

Personal-liability layer under California Labor Code §558.1: an owner, director, officer, or managing agent who "on behalf of an employer" violates specific wage-and-hour provisions can be held personally liable — this is the specific hook that makes California final-pay non-compliance a personal-liability exposure for the CEO / CFO / GC, not just a corporate exposure. Chapter 9 develops the personal-liability doctrine.

### New York

New York Labor Law §191: manual workers must be paid weekly; other employees on the schedule agreed to but at least twice per month. Final wages must be paid by the regular pay day for the pay period in which the termination occurred — which in practice means final pay can be delayed up to 6 days.

New York does not have a §203-style waiting-time penalty on the same terms as California, but the state Attorney General and private plaintiffs can bring wage-and-hour claims under the Labor Law, and specific liquidated damages under New York Labor Law §198 (equal to 100% of the underpaid wages) can apply.

### Illinois

Illinois Wage Payment and Collection Act (820 ILCS 115/) requires final compensation to be paid at the time of separation "if possible" but no later than the next regularly scheduled payday. Interest and attorneys' fees are recoverable on non-payment.

### Texas

Texas Payday Law (Tex. Lab. Code §§61.001 et seq.) requires payment of final wages to a discharged employee within 6 days of the date of discharge; for employees who quit, on the next regular pay day.

### Other states

- **Colorado.** Final wages due immediately if discharged; next scheduled payday if quit. C.R.S. §8-4-109.
- **Connecticut.** Final wages due next business day if discharged; next regular payday if quit. Conn. Gen. Stat. §31-71c.
- **Massachusetts.** Final wages due immediately if discharged (with specific case-law nuance); next regular payday if quit. M.G.L. c. 149 §148.
- **Minnesota.** Final wages due within 24 hours if requested by discharged employee. Minn. Stat. §181.13.
- **Washington.** Final wages due end of next pay period. RCW 49.48.010.

The practical implication: a multi-state workforce shutdown requires final-pay planning state-by-state. The company cannot simply run its normal payroll cycle for termination; several states require faster payment, and the CFO / payroll operations lead needs a state-by-state matrix for the specific workforce.

## PTO payout — the accrued-vacation question

Whether accrued but unused PTO must be paid out on termination is state-specific and depends both on statute and on the specific company policy in place.

### States that require PTO payout on termination

- **California.** Labor Code §227.3 provides that vested vacation is a form of wages and must be paid out on termination. "Use it or lose it" policies are unenforceable in California — vacation, once accrued, is a vested wage. California also treats PTO as wages for §201 immediate-payment purposes.
- **Colorado.** Colorado Wage Act treats vacation as wages under specific circumstances; the CO Supreme Court's Nieto decision (2021) held that vacation pay under specific policies is wages that must be paid on termination.
- **Massachusetts.** M.G.L. c. 149 §148 requires payment of vacation as wages.
- **Illinois.** IL Wage Payment and Collection Act requires payment of "final compensation" including "earned vacation" per specific policy.
- **Nebraska.** Nebraska Wage Payment and Collection Act.
- **North Dakota.** Century Code §34-14-09.2.
- **New Mexico.** Cases have interpreted accrued vacation as wages under specific circumstances.

### States that allow forfeiture with a clear policy

Many states allow a clearly-written employer PTO policy to specify that unused PTO is forfeited on termination — Georgia, Florida, Alabama, and others follow this approach.

### The implication for shutdown

The shutdown obligates the company to pay out accrued PTO in states that require it. The dollar magnitude for a company of 50–100 employees with ordinary PTO accrual can be substantial — often 3–8% of annualised payroll depending on accrual rates and average tenure — and the payment obligation runs on the same statutory timing as final pay (i.e., immediately in California, within a few days in most other states).

The specific implication for the shutdown mechanism:

- **Informal wind-down / solvent shutdown.** PTO paid out in full per state law.
- **ABC / Chapter 7 / Chapter 11.** PTO earned in the pre-shutdown period is a wage claim treated under the priority framework (typically priority unsecured up to the statutory cap, general unsecured above the cap). Post-petition PTO for employees who continue to work is an administrative expense.

The California §227.3 vested-wage treatment interacts with the officer-personal-liability layer — California Labor Code §558.1 exposure extends to unpaid PTO to the same extent it extends to unpaid wages. This is a specific and non-obvious exposure that CFOs and GCs sometimes miss.

## COBRA continuation coverage

The Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1985 (COBRA), 29 U.S.C. §§1161–1169 (ERISA provisions) and 26 U.S.C. §4980B (Internal Revenue Code provisions), requires group health plans of employers with 20 or more employees to provide continued coverage to qualified beneficiaries who lose coverage due to a specific qualifying event.

### The qualifying event and the coverage period

Termination of employment (other than for gross misconduct) is a qualifying event. The maximum coverage period is 18 months (or 29 months for a disabled beneficiary, or 36 months for certain other events).

### Notice requirements

- **Initial notice.** When an employee first becomes covered under the group health plan, the plan administrator must provide notice of COBRA rights within 90 days.
- **Election notice.** When a qualifying event occurs, the plan administrator must provide notice to qualified beneficiaries of their right to elect COBRA within 14 days of receiving notice of the qualifying event. The qualified beneficiary then has 60 days to elect coverage.
- **Notice of unavailability.** When the plan administrator receives notice of a qualifying event but determines the beneficiary is not entitled to COBRA, notice of unavailability must be provided within 14 days.

Content requirements for the notices are set out in the DOL regulations at 29 C.F.R. §2590.606-4.

### The shutdown-specific problem — plan termination

The wrinkle in a shutdown: COBRA continuation requires that a group health plan remain in place. If the employer *terminates the group health plan* entirely (which many shutting-down employers do to stop the ongoing premium spend), COBRA no longer applies because there is no group plan to continue.

The alternatives:

- **Keep the plan in place through the COBRA continuation period.** Expensive; the employer typically pays a portion of the premium even under COBRA, and administrative overhead continues.
- **Terminate the plan and provide notice of the termination.** COBRA obligations end when the plan terminates, but the qualified beneficiaries are typically left to seek individual coverage through the ACA marketplace or Medicaid. The employer must provide specific notice of the plan termination to affected employees.
- **Transition through a subsequent buyer / successor.** In some sale-for-parts structures the buyer assumes the group health plan and continues COBRA obligations for departing employees.

The COBRA administration in a shutdown typically involves a third-party COBRA administrator (WageWorks, Businessolver, Discovery Benefits, and others) who handles notice generation, election processing, and premium collection. Coordination with the administrator before the shutdown announcement is important — a shutdown that surprises the COBRA administrator produces missed notices and specific liability exposure.

### The interaction with severance

A common shutdown-severance structure includes employer-paid COBRA premium for a specific period (typically 3–6 months) as part of the severance package. This is materially valuable to employees (individual health-insurance premiums under the ACA marketplace can be $500–$2,000+ per month depending on family size and location) and is a specific negotiation-and-design point in the severance package.

## Severance — statutory vs. discretionary

Severance in a shutdown is typically discretionary — no federal statute requires severance pay, and most states do not either. But three specific exceptions:

1. **New Jersey.** As of 2023 (delayed implementation), NJ WARN requires mandatory severance of one week per year of employment for each affected employee in a covered mass layoff or plant closing. This is unique among US jurisdictions in imposing statutory severance.
2. **Puerto Rico.** Act 80 of 1976 requires severance for termination without just cause; the amount depends on tenure.
3. **Employment contracts and severance plans.** Individual employment contracts (typical for executives), collective bargaining agreements, and ERISA-covered severance plans can create binding severance obligations that persist through a shutdown.

For most US workforces, severance in a shutdown is a discretionary decision by the company (typically the board), constrained by:

- **The available cash.** In an insolvent shutdown, severance beyond specific priority obligations may not be payable at all under the priority framework.
- **The WARN Act pay-in-lieu-of-notice defence.** Structuring some or all of the severance as pay-in-lieu of the WARN notice period is a specific defence-and-design choice (chapter 4).
- **The release-of-claims consideration.** Severance is typically the consideration for a signed release of claims — including WARN claims, wage-and-hour claims, discrimination claims, and any other employment claims. The release must be knowing and voluntary and (for age-discrimination claims under OWBPA) meet the specific ADEA release standards.
- **The consistency-and-non-discrimination requirement.** Severance decisions must not discriminate on protected characteristics. The safe pattern is a formula-based severance (e.g., "2 weeks per year of service, minimum 4 weeks, maximum 24 weeks, plus 3 months of employer-paid COBRA") applied consistently across the workforce.

### The severance-plan-as-ERISA-plan question

A one-time severance program may or may not constitute an "employee welfare benefit plan" under ERISA depending on the specific facts (Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1 (1987), and progeny). Formalising a severance program as an ERISA plan carries administrative burden (plan document, Summary Plan Description, filing requirements) but also affords ERISA preemption of state-law claims and specific pre-emption-based defences. For a shutdown-severance program, employment counsel typically drafts the specific structure with the ERISA-vs-non-ERISA question resolved deliberately.

### The interaction with the shutdown mechanism

Severance decisions interact with the shutdown mechanism:

- **Informal solvent wind-down.** Company pays severance from available cash.
- **ABC.** Severance can be paid pre-assignment (as part of the wind-down runway) or through the assignee (though the assignee typically prioritises statutory obligations over discretionary severance). Pre-assignment payment is common.
- **Chapter 7.** Severance is generally a general unsecured claim unless structured as a priority obligation. Trustee is not likely to pay discretionary severance from the estate.
- **Chapter 11.** Severance under a Key Employee Retention Plan (KERP) or Key Employee Incentive Plan (KEIP) requires court approval under 11 U.S.C. §503(c). This is a specific and litigated area — Chapter 11 does not automatically permit severance to insiders.

## Non-competes and non-disclosures — the post-2024 landscape

The enforceability of non-compete agreements after termination has been reshaped materially by the FTC's 2024 non-compete-ban rule and the subsequent federal-court litigation.

### The FTC Non-Compete Rule (2024)

In April 2024 the Federal Trade Commission adopted a final rule (the "Non-Compete Clause Rule," 16 C.F.R. Part 910) purporting to declare non-compete clauses in most employer-employee relationships an "unfair method of competition" under Section 5 of the FTC Act. The rule was scheduled to take effect September 4, 2024, and would have:

- Prohibited new non-compete clauses for all workers.
- Made existing non-compete clauses unenforceable for most workers.
- Preserved existing non-compete clauses only for "senior executives" (defined by specific compensation and policy-making authority tests).

The rule was immediately challenged in multiple federal courts. In August 2024 the US District Court for the Northern District of Texas (Ryan LLC v. FTC) issued a nationwide order setting aside the rule under the Administrative Procedure Act. The FTC has appealed to the Fifth Circuit; as of this writing the rule is not in effect nationwide. <!-- needs-research: monitor the Fifth Circuit appeal and any Supreme Court review of Ryan LLC v. ### State law — the pre-existing and continuing landscape

Regardless of the FTC rule status, state law governs the enforceability of non-competes in a specific employment relationship, and state law has been evolving toward tighter restrictions:

- **California.** Labor Code §16600 makes non-competes unenforceable in California for employees, with narrow exceptions (in connection with sale of a business, in the LLC / partnership context). SB 699 (effective January 2024) expanded this to make out-of-state non-competes unenforceable against California-resident employees and provided a private right of action against employers who attempt to enforce them. AB 1076 (effective January 2024) requires employers to give specific notice to affected employees that their non-competes are void.
- **North Dakota.** N.D. Cent. Code §9-08-06 makes non-competes generally unenforceable, similar to California.
- **Oklahoma.** 15 O.S. §219A similar to California.
- **Minnesota.** Minn. Stat. §181.988 (effective July 2023) bans non-compete agreements for most employees.
- **Washington.** RCW 49.62 permits non-competes only for high-earning employees (with an annual salary threshold that indexes; verify at <https://apps.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=49.62>).
- **Massachusetts, Illinois, Colorado, and several others.** Have adopted "garden-leave" requirements, salary thresholds, or notice requirements for non-competes.

The practical implication for shutdown:

- Non-competes signed by California-based employees are typically unenforceable regardless of the FTC rule.
- Non-competes signed by employees in other states may or may not be enforceable — a specific state-by-state analysis is required.
- Attempting to enforce a non-compete against a former employee post-shutdown produces material litigation exposure in California under SB 699's private right of action and in other jurisdictions under state-law wrongful-termination or restraint-of-trade doctrines.

### Non-disclosure and trade-secret obligations

Non-disclosure obligations — as distinct from non-competes — remain enforceable in most jurisdictions. A confidentiality agreement that prohibits an employee from disclosing the company's trade secrets, customer lists, technical information, and other confidential information post-termination is typically enforceable regardless of the FTC rule or state non-compete law.

For a shutdown, the specific concerns:

- **Trade-secret exposure.** Former employees moving to competitors with knowledge of the company's technology or customer relationships is a specific trade-secret concern. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 (18 U.S.C. §1836) provides a federal cause of action for trade-secret misappropriation.
- **Return of company property.** The shutdown agenda should include specific return of laptops, phones, access credentials, and any physical documents. A checklist and a formal return protocol are standard.
- **Data destruction / retention.** Employee-personal-data and customer-personal-data obligations under GDPR, CCPA / CPRA, and other privacy statutes continue past the shutdown. Data retention and destruction should be planned in coordination with privacy counsel.

## The comprehensive employee-side shutdown checklist

Pulling the workstreams together, a comprehensive employee-side shutdown checklist includes:

1. **WARN notice** (chapter 4) — federal and state mini-WARN as applicable.
2. **Final pay** — per state-specific timing (immediate in California, next payday in others).
3. **PTO payout** — per state-specific requirements and the company's written policy.
4. **COBRA administration** — election notices, plan continuation or termination decisions, third-party administrator coordination.
5. **Severance and release** — package design, formula-based application, release-of-claims consideration.
6. **Non-compete and non-disclosure** — enforceability analysis by state, notice-of-void requirements in California and other jurisdictions.
7. **Return of company property** — checklist, protocol, verification.
8. **Data privacy compliance** — employee-personal-data destruction / retention per GDPR / CCPA / CPRA.
9. **Retirement plan wind-up** — 401(k) plan termination, participant notification, distribution processing (chapter 9 develops the ERISA fiduciary layer).
10. **Unemployment insurance filings** — state UI agency notification, employee assistance with filing.
11. **Referral and outplacement services** — employer-provided outplacement (Right Management, Lee Hecht Harrison, Randstad RiseSmart, and others) as part of severance; internal referral network activation.
12. **Public communications** — coordinated with the transaction-communications workstream ([mod-110](../mod-110-transaction-communications-and-stakeholder-management/README.md)).

## The interaction with the officer-and-director personal-liability layer

Every workstream on the employee-side shutdown checklist has a specific tie to officer-personal-liability exposure (chapter 9 develops the full landscape):

- **Final pay** → IRC §6672 responsible-person liability for unpaid federal payroll tax withholding; California Labor Code §558.1 for wage-and-hour violations; state-equivalent statutes in New York, Massachusetts, and other jurisdictions.
- **PTO payout** → same as final pay in states that treat PTO as wages.
- **COBRA administration** → ERISA fiduciary liability for the plan administrator and, in some structures, for the responsible corporate officer.
- **Severance and release** → generally not a personal-liability exposure unless the release is coerced or the severance is misused to defraud creditors.
- **Non-compete enforcement** → California SB 699 private right of action includes attorneys' fees and can extend to the employer's officers under specific facts.
- **Retirement plan wind-up** → ERISA §404 fiduciary duty applies to the plan administrator and to any corporate officer acting as an ERISA fiduciary.

The specific implication: the employee-side shutdown workstreams are not just corporate obligations. They are the primary source of personal-liability exposure for the CEO, CFO, and (in some cases) other officers. Chapter 9 develops the personal-liability doctrine; this chapter installs the substrate.

## Common failure modes

Five recurring failure modes in employee-side shutdown workstreams:

1. **State-by-state pattern collapse.** Treating "final pay" as a single national obligation instead of running it state-by-state. California immediate-pay is legally distinct from Illinois-next-payday, and the practical schedule differs.
2. **PTO liability underestimate.** Failing to model the PTO-payout obligation at the state-specific level. A 60-person California-heavy company can easily owe $500K–$1M in PTO payout on termination.
3. **COBRA notice mishandling.** Missing the 14-day / 60-day election notice windows or failing to coordinate with the COBRA administrator on plan-termination decisions.
4. **Non-compete enforcement mis-attempt.** Attempting to enforce a California non-compete against a departing employee, exposing the company to SB 699 damages and attorneys' fees.
5. **Severance-release mis-structure.** Providing severance without a properly-drafted OWBPA-compliant release, or providing severance as "consideration" for wages the employee was already owed (which is not consideration).

## What good employee-side shutdown execution produces

- Compliant final pay per state-specific timing and PTO-payout requirements.
- Timely COBRA notices and coordinated plan continuation or termination.
- Consistently-designed severance-and-release package with knowing-and-voluntary releases in place.
- Clear communication to affected employees about non-compete unenforceability (in California and other states requiring specific notice) and about non-disclosure obligations that survive.
- Cleanly-returned company property and cleanly-handled employee-personal-data.
- Terminated retirement plan with proper participant notifications and distributions.
- Coordinated unemployment-insurance filings and outplacement services.
- Public communications aligned with the internal workstream so employees learn material information from the company first, not from external sources.

## Summary

The employee-side of a shutdown includes final pay (state-specific timing, with California's immediate-pay rule being the strictest), PTO payout (required in California / Colorado / Massachusetts / Illinois / Nebraska / North Dakota and treated as wages under Labor Code §227.3 in California), COBRA administration for health-benefits continuation, discretionary or statutory severance (New Jersey being the specific exception with mandatory severance under NJ WARN), non-compete enforceability (heavily restricted in California under SB 699 / AB 1076 and increasingly restricted in other states; the FTC 2024 non-compete ban was set aside by the Northern District of Texas and its status is under appeal), and specific data-privacy and retirement-plan wind-up obligations. Each workstream has a specific officer-personal-liability exposure that chapter 9 develops. The next chapter turns to the IP-disposition question, which for many venture-backed startups is the highest-value single decision in the shutdown.
