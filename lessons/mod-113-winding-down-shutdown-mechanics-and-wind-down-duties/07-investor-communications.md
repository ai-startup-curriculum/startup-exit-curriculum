# Investor Communications and the Wind-Up-vs-Distribute Choice

## Why this matters

Venture capitalists who invested in the shutting-down company have their own downstream stakeholders — the limited partners (pension funds, endowments, family offices, sovereign wealth funds, funds-of-funds) who committed capital to the fund. The VC firm's LP-report obligations, the specific disclosure the firm owes its LPs about a portfolio loss, and the fund-lifecycle-and-mark-to-market accounting for the loss are a specific institutional workstream that runs alongside the operational shutdown. Founders and CFOs who ignore this workstream produce misaligned incentives, delayed shutdown decisions, and — in some cases — post-shutdown investor-relationship damage that costs the founders on future ventures. Founders and CFOs who understand it manage the investor-communications workstream deliberately and align the shutdown timing with the investors' institutional obligations.

The second half of this chapter turns to the *distribution-vs-wind-up* choice — for a company with remaining cash after satisfying all creditors (a rare but real situation), do you distribute the remaining cash to preferred stockholders under the liquidation-preference stack, wind up the shell entity, or hold the shell entity open for some period to pursue residual claims? The choice interacts with the shutdown mechanism, the specific investor preferences, and the state-law dissolution timeline.

This chapter installs both workstreams. It does not substitute for specific investor-relations advice, specific fund-side counsel, or specific tax counsel on the distribution question — every live shutdown with meaningful investor participation engages those advisors. It is the substrate the founder-CEO, CFO, and GC read before those engagements.

## The VC firm's LP-report obligations

Venture funds report to their LPs on a regular cadence — typically quarterly for standard fund reporting, plus specific event-driven communications for material developments in portfolio companies.

### The quarterly-report treatment

At each quarter-end the fund marks its portfolio to fair value under the framework the fund's Limited Partnership Agreement adopts. The primary frameworks:

- **ASC 820 / Topic 820** — the FASB standard for fair-value measurement, applied to the fund's investment portfolio.
- **IPEV Valuation Guidelines** — the International Private Equity and Venture Capital Valuation Guidelines, endorsed by the National Venture Capital Association (NVCA) and the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA). The IPEV guidelines are the de-facto standard for venture-fund portfolio valuation.
- **AICPA PE/VC Valuation Guide** — additional US-specific practitioner guidance.

A portfolio company that has just filed for shutdown (ABC, Chapter 7, Chapter 11) is typically marked to zero or to a small residual value (representing any expected distribution from the wind-up) in the quarter of the shutdown decision. This mark is the fund's specific reported loss for the quarter.

### The event-driven communication

Beyond the quarterly mark, the VC firm typically communicates a portfolio loss to its LPs at the time of the shutdown — a specific LP-communication (letter, email, or investor-update) explaining:

- The specific company shutting down.
- The specific reasons for the shutdown.
- The specific mark being taken.
- The specific status of any wind-up recoveries (particularly if a sale-for-parts or an ABC is expected to produce a distribution to the fund as an equity holder — usually zero in an insolvent shutdown).

This communication happens in real-time with the shutdown announcement — LPs typically want to hear about a portfolio loss from the fund before they read about it in TechCrunch or on Twitter.

### The fund's incentive on shutdown timing

The fund has specific incentives that interact with the shutdown timing:

- **Quarter-end timing.** Some funds prefer to consolidate portfolio losses into a single quarter's reporting rather than spread across multiple quarters. A shutdown decision made in the last week of a quarter may be preferred by the fund over one made in the first week of the next quarter.
- **Fund-cycle timing.** If the fund is fundraising for a new fund vintage, a large portfolio loss immediately before the fundraise can be complicating. Funds sometimes prefer to complete a wind-up either before the fundraise closes or after — not in the middle of it.
- **Reserved capital.** If the fund has reserved specific capital for follow-on investments in the shutting-down company, the shutdown releases that reserve (it can be redeployed to other portfolio companies or returned to LPs as recycled capital). The reserve-release calculation affects the fund's capital-deployment strategy.

The founder-CEO's obligation is not to accommodate the fund's timing preferences — the fiduciary duty to creditors in the zone of insolvency runs to the company's creditors, not to the fund. But the founder-CEO who understands the fund's institutional timing can raise timing considerations with the fund in a specific and productive way, rather than being surprised by fund push-back on a shutdown timeline.

### The specific alignment pattern

The typical alignment conversation between the founder-CEO and the fund's investing partner (or the fund's specific board member) on shutdown timing:

1. **The founder-CEO signals the shutdown consideration** — typically at a board meeting where the runway shortfall or the failed round is discussed, with an explicit request for the fund's read on timing.
2. **The fund's investing partner reads the fund's own timing constraints** — quarter-end, fundraise-cycle, follow-on-reserve considerations — and shares them with the founder-CEO.
3. **The founder-CEO and the fund agree on a specific target timing** — within the constraints of the company's fiduciary duty to creditors, which is not negotiable, but with alignment on the specific date within the workable window.
4. **The founder-CEO, the fund, and the other investors align on the specific LP-communication pattern** — who says what to whom, on what timing, with what specific facts.

The pattern breaks down when either (a) the founder-CEO delays the shutdown to accommodate the fund's preferences at the cost of creditor recovery (which is a specific fiduciary breach), or (b) the fund pressures the founder-CEO for a specific timing that the company's cash runway does not support (which forces a compressed and worse shutdown execution).

### The delay-worsens-outcomes pattern

A specific empirical pattern that founders and investors often under-appreciate: *delaying the shutdown decision typically produces worse outcomes*. The mechanics:

- **Runway consumed by the delay.** Every week of continued operation consumes cash that could otherwise have been distributed to creditors or (in a solvent wind-up) to equity.
- **Asset deterioration.** Customer contracts churn, engineering-team goodwill decays, IP-market-window closes, key employees depart. Assets are worth more when sold from a going-concern with a functioning team than from a wind-up with a departing team.
- **Fiduciary-duty exposure accumulates.** As the company operates in the zone of insolvency for longer, the specific exposure to creditor-side fiduciary-duty claims grows.
- **Employee-obligation accumulation.** Additional wage claims, PTO accrual, and WARN-exposure calendar-time accumulate with continued operation.
- **Compressed execution at the end.** A shutdown that finally starts under acute pressure (out of cash next week) executes worse than one that starts with 8–12 weeks of runway to plan the workstreams.

The founder-CEO's specific discipline is to *make the shutdown decision at the point the framework in chapter 1 indicates*, not at the point the runway forces it. Investors who understand this pattern support the decision at the earlier time; investors who do not tend to advocate for delay in the hope of a specific favourable outcome (a rescue round, a strategic bidder emerging) that in most cases does not materialise.

## The distribution-vs-wind-up choice

For most venture-backed shutdowns the question of "what do we do with the remaining cash" is answered by the priority waterfall (chapter 2) — the cash goes to creditors, and equity gets nothing. But in a specific subset of shutdowns — typically solvent wind-ups where the company has more cash and asset-sale proceeds than it has creditor claims — a specific distribution question arises.

### The pattern

The company has (after all creditors are paid in full) some remaining cash. The distribution options:

1. **Distribute to preferred stockholders under the liquidation-preference stack.** The remaining cash flows to preferred stockholders in the order of their liquidation preferences, subject to any participation-rights and caps. Common stockholders (founders and employees) receive whatever is left after the preferred stack.
2. **Wind up the shell entity with a state-law dissolution.** File the certificate of dissolution, run the state-law post-dissolution period (three years under Delaware §278), distribute residual amounts at the end.
3. **Keep the shell entity open for a defined period to pursue residual claims.** For example, if the company has a specific pending patent-infringement claim, a specific tax refund pending, or a specific insurance-recovery pending, keeping the shell entity open allows those claims to be pursued and their recoveries distributed.

### The Delaware §278 winding-up period

For Delaware corporations, the winding-up period under DGCL §278 is a specific statutory pattern:

- The corporation may be continued for three years from the date of dissolution or such longer period as the Court of Chancery may direct.
- During that period the corporation continues for the purpose of prosecuting and defending suits, closing the business, disposing of property, discharging liabilities, and distributing remaining assets — but not for the purpose of continuing the business.
- After the three years (or the extended period), the corporation ceases to exist for all purposes.

Similar provisions exist in most other state corporate-law frameworks (with varying periods).

### The specific distribution mechanic

Distributing remaining cash to preferred stockholders in the liquidation-preference order:

1. **Compute the total remaining cash** after satisfaction of all creditor obligations.
2. **Compute the total liquidation preferences** of each preferred class in the order of their seniority (typically last-round preferred first, then the round before that, and so on, unless the specific certificate of incorporation provides otherwise).
3. **Distribute in seniority order** until either (a) all preferred classes are paid in full, or (b) the remaining cash is exhausted.
4. **Distribute any remaining cash** after preferred is paid in full to common stockholders per their pro rata ownership.

The specific liquidation-preference math depends on the preferred stock terms — is it participating (participates alongside common after the preference is paid) or non-participating (converts to common if that would produce a higher payout)? Are there caps on participation? Are there specific senior-preferred provisions? The [`startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum`](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum) mod-104 develops the specific liquidation-preference mechanics in depth; this chapter assumes the reader is comfortable with the framework.

### When to distribute vs. hold vs. dissolve

The specific factors:

- **Immediate distribution to preferred.** Appropriate when the remaining cash is meaningful (justifying the administrative cost of the distribution), the preferred stockholders want the distribution promptly for their own fund-lifecycle reasons, and there are no material pending claims that require the shell entity to remain open.
- **Hold pending residual claims.** Appropriate when there are specific pending or reasonably-anticipated claims (patent infringement recovery, tax refund, insurance recovery) whose disposition should be completed before final distribution.
- **Dissolve promptly.** Appropriate when the administrative cost of holding the entity open exceeds the expected recoveries from residual claims — for a small remaining balance the state-law wind-up period may not justify the additional legal and administrative expense.

The typical pattern in a venture-backed shutdown with residual cash:

- File the certificate of dissolution promptly (initiating the state-law wind-up period).
- Distribute the bulk of the residual cash to preferred stockholders in the first months after dissolution.
- Hold a modest reserve in the shell entity for the state-law wind-up period.
- Distribute any residual reserve at the end of the wind-up period or on completion of specific pending matters.

### The tax overlay

Distributions to stockholders on liquidation are treated as sales or exchanges under IRC §331 — the stockholder receives a return of basis (tax-free) up to their stock basis and capital gain thereafter. For founders holding common stock with a low basis, a liquidating distribution can produce meaningful capital gain (long-term if the stock was held more than a year, which is nearly always the case in a founder context). For investors holding preferred stock with a high basis relative to the distribution, the distribution may produce a capital loss.

The IRC §1202 qualified small business stock exclusion (mod-114 develops this) can apply to a portion of the founder's gain if the stock meets the §1202 requirements. The IRC §1244 ordinary-loss treatment can apply to stock in a small business corporation for losses recognised on liquidation — a specific favourable treatment for founders and early investors on stock that has become worthless. Consult mod-114 and specific tax counsel.

## The investor-communications workstream

Alongside the LP-communications, the founder-CEO manages a specific communications workstream with the company's investors themselves. The typical pattern:

### The board-level communication

The board is informed of the shutdown decision first, typically at a specifically-called board meeting where the framework in chapter 1 is walked through, the recommended mechanism is presented, and the board authorises the specific execution (assignment agreement, bankruptcy filing, informal wind-down authorisation).

For venture-backed startups the board typically includes:

- The founder-CEO.
- Investor-directors from the lead investor in each priced round.
- Independent directors (fewer in early-stage, more in growth-stage).

The board deliberation should be run with specific fiduciary-duty discipline — in the zone of insolvency the duty runs to the corporate enterprise for the benefit of the residual claimants, which now include creditors. Independent directors' role is especially significant in this context (see mod-109 for the fiduciary-duty framework).

### The all-investors communication

Beyond the board, all preferred stockholders receive the notice under the assignment agreement (in an ABC), the bankruptcy petition (in Chapter 7 or 11), or the state-law dissolution notice (in an informal wind-down). This is typically a formal legal notice rather than a warm communication.

Alongside the formal notice, the founder-CEO or the lead investor typically sends a specific investor-update explaining:

- The shutdown decision.
- The recommended mechanism.
- The expected recovery for preferred (typically zero in insolvent shutdowns; specific number in solvent wind-ups).
- The specific timeline for the wind-up.
- The specific point-of-contact for questions.

### The employee-facing implications

Employees learn of the shutdown decision through a specific and separately-choreographed communication (mod-110 develops the transaction-communications workstream). The investor-communications workstream is coordinated with the employee-communications workstream so that employees are not surprised by the shutdown after investors have already been informed — a specific alignment discipline that avoids information asymmetry that would otherwise leak.

### The customer-and-partner communication

Customers and commercial partners typically learn of the shutdown after employees but before the general public. The specific communication depends on the mechanism:

- **ABC.** The assignee sends specific notice to counterparties about the assignment and the wind-up. The company's founders typically also send a personal note to key customers explaining the situation and the transition.
- **Chapter 7.** The trustee sends specific notice about the bankruptcy filing. Customer-continuity is typically not a priority in a Chapter 7.
- **Chapter 11 with §363 sale.** The DIP or the buyer typically sends specific customer-transition communications.
- **Informal wind-down.** The company sends customer-transition communications directly.

### The press and public communication

The specific public communication depends on the company's public profile, the sector, and the specific shutdown facts. Typical patterns:

- **Founder-authored public post.** A LinkedIn post, blog post, or Medium post by the founder-CEO explaining the shutdown, thanking employees and investors, and stating what comes next. This is the standard pattern for venture-backed startups with any degree of public profile.
- **Press outreach.** For companies with press relationships, a specific press communication (typically via the PR agency or in-house comms) to key sector journalists.
- **Regulatory or exchange notifications.** For any company subject to specific regulatory or exchange notification obligations (rare for private companies but relevant for companies that had specific regulatory registrations).

## Common failure modes

Five recurring failure modes in the investor-communications-and-distribution workstream:

1. **Delayed shutdown to accommodate investor preferences.** The founder-CEO holds off on the shutdown decision to accommodate a specific investor's timing preference, consuming runway and worsening creditor recovery. Fiduciary-duty exposure for the officers.
2. **Investor-first communications leak.** Investors informed of the shutdown before employees, creating an information asymmetry that leaks and destroys employee trust. The alignment discipline with mod-110's transaction-communications workstream avoids this.
3. **Misapplied liquidation-preference distribution.** In a solvent wind-up, the distribution to preferred stockholders is computed incorrectly — participating vs. non-participating, seniority order, participation caps. Requires specific counsel review before distributing.
4. **Missed state-law dissolution filings.** Failing to file the specific certificate of dissolution and to run the state-law wind-up period leaves the shell entity technically active, exposing directors to continuing filing and reporting obligations.
5. **Missed §1244 election / §1202 analysis.** Founder and early-investor tax treatment on the loss recognised on liquidation depends on specific §1244 and §1202 elections and analysis. Missing these produces materially worse tax outcomes for the individuals.

## What good investor-communications produces

- A board decision made with the specific fiduciary-duty discipline for the zone of insolvency.
- An investor-communication package that provides material information to investors without leaking before employee communications.
- A specific LP-communication pattern (through the fund) that meets the fund's reporting obligations without shifting the shutdown timeline improperly.
- A solvent-wind-up distribution executed correctly under the liquidation-preference stack.
- A dissolved-and-wound-up entity at the end of the state-law wind-up period.
- Preserved founder-investor relationships for future ventures — investors who saw the founder handle the shutdown well are typically willing to back the founder's next venture.

## Summary

The investor-communications workstream in a shutdown includes the fund's LP-reporting obligations (quarterly mark-to-fair-value under ASC 820 / IPEV guidelines, event-driven LP-communication), the founder-CEO's board and investor communications (with fiduciary-duty discipline in the zone of insolvency and with alignment against the employee-communications workstream), and the specific distribution-vs-wind-up choice for any solvent shutdown with residual cash. The pattern of delaying the shutdown to preserve optionality typically produces worse outcomes; the specific alignment between founders and investors on shutdown timing is what preserves both the creditor recovery and the founder-investor relationship for future ventures.
