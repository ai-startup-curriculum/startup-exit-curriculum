# WARN Act Compliance in a Shutdown

## Why this matters

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act (WARN, 29 U.S.C. §§2101–2109) is the specific federal statute that requires 60 days' advance notice to affected employees, unions, and state and local government officials before a mass layoff or plant closing at a covered employer. Layered on top of it are the *state mini-WARN* statutes — California's Cal-WARN (California Labor Code §§1400 et seq.), New York's WARN (NY Labor Law §860), New Jersey's WARN (NJ Rev. Stat. §34:21-1 et seq.), Illinois's WARN, Minnesota's, and several others — each with their own coverage thresholds, notice periods (some longer than federal), exception standards, and enforcement mechanisms.

For a shutdown, WARN is the highest-frequency source of officer-and-director personal-liability exposure and the highest-frequency source of post-close plaintiff-firm inbound. The federal statute allows employees to recover up to 60 days of back-pay and benefits for the notice-period violation, plus attorneys' fees (§2104(a)); the state statutes often add on top with their own damages. A shutdown that misfires WARN by even a few days — much less announces "we're closing tomorrow" with no notice — routinely produces class-action litigation with settlement amounts in the seven figures for a company of even modest size. The "faltering-company" and "unforeseeable business circumstance" exceptions exist, but they are tightly construed by courts and are frequently unavailable to a shutdown that was foreseeable in the last quarter.

This chapter installs the substance a founder-CEO, CFO, and GC need to understand what WARN requires, when the exceptions actually apply, how the state statutes overlay, how successor-liability doctrine can transfer WARN exposure in an asset sale, and how the standard shutdown-WARN-defence pattern looks. It does not substitute for employment counsel — every live shutdown engages counsel who will drive the specific notice-generation, the specific state-agency filing, and the specific defence if litigation is filed.

> Reminder: WARN compliance is technical and jurisdiction-specific. The specific coverage thresholds, notice periods, damages caps, and defence-availability rules require review with employment counsel for the specific jurisdictions and specific workforce composition at issue. This chapter is the substrate you read before that engagement.

## Federal WARN — coverage, notice, and damages

### Covered employers

29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(1) defines a covered employer as any business enterprise that employs:

- 100 or more employees, excluding part-time employees (those working fewer than 20 hours per week or fewer than 6 months in the preceding 12 months), or
- 100 or more employees, including part-time employees, who in the aggregate work at least 4,000 hours per week (exclusive of overtime).

This is a *whole-company* count — not a per-site count. A company with 60 employees at one site and 50 at another has 110 employees and is covered even if no single site meets any threshold. Independent contractors are not counted; only employees.

### Covered events — plant closing and mass layoff

Notice is required before either:

- A **plant closing** — the permanent or temporary shutdown of a single site of employment (or a facility or operating unit within a single site) that results in an employment loss for 50 or more employees (excluding part-time employees) during any 30-day period. 29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(2).
- A **mass layoff** — a reduction in force that is *not* a plant closing and results in an employment loss during any 30-day period for either:
  - 500 or more employees (excluding part-time employees), *or*
  - 50 to 499 employees (excluding part-time employees) if they constitute at least 33% of the employer's active workforce (excluding part-time employees) at the single site.

29 U.S.C. §2101(a)(3). Both prongs of the mass-layoff test require aggregation over the 30-day period. A rolling 90-day aggregation rule (§2102(d)) requires aggregation of employment losses that individually would not trigger WARN but collectively do so if they occur within 90 days of each other and are for one or more related causes.

An **employment loss** under §2101(a)(6) includes:

- An employment termination (other than a discharge for cause, voluntary departure, or retirement).
- A layoff exceeding 6 months.
- A reduction in an employee's hours of work of more than 50% in each month of a 6-month period.

### Notice requirement

For a covered event, §2102(a) requires 60 days' advance notice to:

- Each affected employee (individually), or to the representative of the employees (a union) if the employees are represented.
- The state dislocated-worker unit (typically the state Department of Labor or Employment Development Department).
- The chief elected official of the unit of local government within which the closing or layoff is to occur (typically the mayor or county executive of the specific city or county).

Content requirements for the notice are set out in the Department of Labor regulations at 20 C.F.R. §639.7 — including a statement of whether the action is permanent or temporary, the expected date of the first separation, the expected schedule of separations, the job titles of positions to be affected, the number of affected employees in each classification, and (for the employee-specific notice) the name and contact information of a company official for further information.

### Damages under §2104(a)

An employer who violates the notice requirement is liable to each aggrieved employee for:

- Back pay for each day of violation, at the higher of the average regular rate received during the last 3 years or the final regular rate.
- Benefits under an employee benefit plan (medical expenses that would have been covered under a benefit plan).

The liability is capped at 60 days of pay and benefits — the notice period. Damages are reduced by any wages paid during the notice period, any voluntary payments the employer was not required to make, and any payments to a third party or trustee on behalf of the employee.

Additional damages under §2104(a)(3) include a civil penalty of up to $500 for each day of violation payable to the local government (waivable if employer pays the aggrieved employees within 3 weeks after the closing or layoff).

The employer is also liable for attorneys' fees under §2104(a)(6) — a specific and important provision because it turns even modest damages cases into economically-viable litigation for plaintiffs' firms.

### The three exceptions under §2102(b)

Three exceptions reduce (not eliminate) the notice period:

1. **Faltering-company exception (§2102(b)(1)).** Applies *only* to plant closings (not mass layoffs). Available when the employer:
   - Was actively seeking capital or business at the time the 60-day notice would have been required, *and*
   - Reasonably and in good faith believed that giving notice would have precluded obtaining the needed capital or business, *and*
   - The capital or business, if obtained, would have enabled the employer to avoid or postpone the shutdown.

   The DOL regulations at 20 C.F.R. §639.9(a) construe each element strictly. "Actively seeking capital or business" requires specific, documented efforts (specific investors approached, specific financing rounds in process, specific business deals being pursued). "Reasonably and in good faith believed" requires contemporaneous evidence that management believed notice would jeopardise the effort. "Would have enabled" requires a realistic prospect that the capital or business would have solved the problem.

2. **Unforeseeable business circumstance exception (§2102(b)(2)(A)).** Applies to both plant closings and mass layoffs. Available when the closing or layoff is caused by business circumstances that were not reasonably foreseeable at the time the 60-day notice would have been required. The DOL regulations at §639.9(b) define "not reasonably foreseeable" narrowly: the circumstance must be sudden, dramatic, and outside the employer's control. Examples the DOL cites include a principal client's sudden and unexpected termination of a major contract, a strike at a major supplier, or a natural disaster.

3. **Natural-disaster exception (§2102(b)(2)(B)).** Applies to both plant closings and mass layoffs. Available when the closing or layoff is caused by a natural disaster (flood, earthquake, drought, storm, tidal wave, or similar effect of nature).

All three exceptions *reduce* the notice period — they do not eliminate the notice requirement. The employer must still give as much notice as is practicable at the time the notice becomes practicable, and the notice must include a "brief statement of the basis for reducing the notification period" (§2102(b)(3)).

### Why the exceptions are frequently unavailable in a shutdown

Two specific patterns make the exceptions harder to invoke than founders typically expect:

- **Foreseeability creep.** By the time the shutdown decision is made, the underlying business trouble has often been visible for weeks or months. "The board decided to shut down last Tuesday" does not make the shutdown *unforeseeable* — courts look at when a reasonable employer *should have foreseen* the closing, not when the specific decision was made. A company that ran out of runway after a failed round in Q4 typically foresees the shutdown by Q3.
- **Faltering-company scope limits.** The faltering-company exception is available only to *plant closings*, not mass layoffs, and requires that the company was actively seeking capital or business at the time notice would have been required. A company that stopped fundraising three months before the shutdown will struggle to establish "actively seeking" for the notice-period window.

The practical implication: assume the notice period is 60 days (or the state-specific longer period) and plan the shutdown timeline around that assumption. Assume the exceptions will *not* apply and manage the specific case if they do.

## State mini-WARN statutes

Several states have their own WARN statutes that supplement (or in some cases pre-empt) the federal statute. The overlays matter for any shutdown with employees in these states — the federal statute is a floor, not a ceiling.

### California — Cal-WARN

California Labor Code §§1400 et seq. Covers employers with 75 or more employees (lower than the federal 100). Requires 60 days' advance notice for a mass layoff (50+ employees at a single covered establishment over a 30-day period), relocation, or termination of operations at a covered establishment. Notice must go to affected employees, the Employment Development Department (EDD), the local workforce investment board, and the chief elected official of each city and county government within which the layoff, relocation, or termination occurs.

Cal-WARN does not have a faltering-company exception; it has narrower unforeseeable-business-circumstance and physical-calamity exceptions. Damages under §1402 are similar to federal (back pay and benefits for the notice-period violation, plus a civil penalty of up to $500 per day and attorneys' fees) but the coverage threshold is lower and the exceptions are narrower — so a shutdown that would qualify for the federal faltering-company exception may still be non-compliant under Cal-WARN.

The California courts and Ninth Circuit have construed Cal-WARN strictly. Practitioner counsel typically advises assuming Cal-WARN compliance is required in parallel with federal WARN compliance for any California-based workforce.

### New York — NY WARN

NY Labor Law §860 et seq. Covers employers with 50 or more employees. Requires 90 days' advance notice (30 days longer than federal) for a mass layoff (25+ employees at a single site in a 30-day period, if they constitute at least 33% of the employer's workforce at the site, or 250+ employees regardless of percentage), plant closing (25+ employees), or reduction in work hours.

NY WARN has exceptions similar to federal but with the specific longer 90-day baseline. Damages track federal but with the extra 30 days of exposure.

### New Jersey — the Millville Dallas Airmotive Plant Job Loss Notification Act

NJ Rev. Stat. §34:21-1 et seq. Amended significantly in 2020 (with implementation delayed and phased in through 2023) to require:

- 90 days' advance notice.
- Severance pay of one week for each full year of employment for each affected employee, mandatory (not discretionary) — a specific and significant obligation not present in federal or most other state statutes.
- Coverage threshold of 100 employees (aligned with federal).

The mandatory-severance feature is unique to NJ WARN and materially changes the shutdown-cost calculation for any New Jersey-based workforce.

### Illinois, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Maine, and others

Several other states have their own mini-WARN statutes with varying coverage thresholds and notice periods:

- **Illinois WARN** (820 ILCS 65/) — 75-employee threshold, 60-day notice.
- **Minnesota Notification Statute** (Minn. Stat. §116L.976) — 50-employee threshold with specific notification requirements.
- **Wisconsin Business Closing and Mass Layoff Law** (Wis. Stat. §109.07) — 50-employee threshold, 60-day notice.
- **Maine's Severance Pay Law** (26 M.R.S. §625-B) — mandatory severance in specific situations.

The practical implication: for a multi-state workforce, WARN compliance is not a single national analysis; it is a per-state analysis with the specific overlay for each jurisdiction. Employment counsel will drive this.

## The successor-liability pattern in an asset sale

When a company runs a sale-for-parts before shutdown or a §363 sale in a Chapter 11, the WARN exposure does not always stay with the seller. Federal courts have applied a *successor-liability* doctrine in specific fact patterns.

The pattern (varies by circuit):

- If the buyer takes over substantially all of the seller's operations and continues to operate at the same site.
- If the buyer employs a substantial portion of the seller's workforce.
- If the buyer knew or should have known of the WARN exposure at the time of the transaction.
- If treating the buyer as a successor is consistent with the equities.

Then the buyer may be held liable for the seller's WARN violation.

The specific implication for shutdown planning:

- The asset-purchase agreement should specifically allocate WARN liability between seller and buyer.
- A buyer who intends to continue operations should be given the opportunity to provide WARN notice (or to make representations that allow the seller to reduce notice under a specific exception).
- A buyer's stated intent to employ some or all of the seller's workforce affects the WARN-notice analysis on the seller's side — if the buyer will hire the employees on day one after the sale, they may not have suffered an "employment loss" as defined by WARN.

The specific pattern varies by circuit — the Ninth Circuit, the Fifth Circuit, and the Third Circuit have all issued opinions in this area. Employment counsel should be consulted on the specific facts and jurisdiction.

## The standard shutdown-WARN-defence pattern

When WARN notice was not given (or was given late), the standard defensive posture involves:

- **Identifying which exception(s) might apply and documenting the factual basis contemporaneously.** The exceptions require contemporaneous evidence; retrospective reconstruction is much weaker.
- **Giving as much notice as is practicable at the time it becomes practicable.** Even a shortened notice period is better than none, and the DOL regulations at §639.9 require the shortened notice to include a brief statement of the basis for the reduction.
- **Providing pay-in-lieu of notice.** Paying employees the wages and benefits they would have received during the notice period is a defence in most jurisdictions (it satisfies the damages measure under §2104(a)). Structurally, "we're closing next Friday and here are 60 days of pay-in-lieu of notice" is the standard damage-mitigation posture.
- **Filing state-agency notifications.** Even when the federal or state-court exposure is being managed, the state agency notifications (EDD, dislocated-worker unit, local government) should be filed to avoid additional exposure and to trigger workforce-transition assistance for affected employees.

The standard settlement pattern in WARN class-action litigation (when it is filed) is typically 50–100% of the notice-period wages, plus plaintiffs' attorneys' fees. The specific settlement amount depends on the exception-availability analysis, the size of the class, and the jurisdiction; plaintiffs' firms bring these actions on a contingency basis, so the settlements need to produce a specific fee recovery.

## The interaction with severance and separation-agreement releases

A common shutdown pattern is offering severance conditioned on a release of claims (including WARN claims). The release must comply with specific standards:

- **Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), 29 U.S.C. §626(f).** For employees 40 or older, a release of ADEA claims requires specific language, a 21-day consideration period (45 days for a group termination), and a 7-day revocation period. A non-compliant release does not effectively release ADEA claims but may still release non-ADEA claims (including WARN claims) if drafted correctly.
- **Knowing and voluntary standard.** Federal courts have held that WARN claims are subject to a general "knowing and voluntary" release standard — the release must be signed with adequate knowledge and without coercion, and courts will scrutinise releases signed under compressed shutdown timelines.
- **Consideration.** The release must be supported by consideration the employee was not otherwise entitled to. Severance in exchange for release is the standard structure; wages already earned or PTO already vested are typically not sufficient consideration because the employee was owed them regardless.

The specific implication for shutdown design: the severance-and-release package is often the primary WARN-defence tool. Design it with employment counsel from the outset, not after employees have already been notified of termination.

## The interaction with the shutdown mechanism

WARN applies regardless of the shutdown mechanism (informal wind-down, ABC, Chapter 7, or Chapter 11) — it is a federal statute that is not preempted by state ABC frameworks and is not discharged by bankruptcy for the officer-and-director personal-liability layer (chapter 9). Specifically:

- **Informal wind-down.** WARN applies. The company is liable; officers may be personally liable in specific jurisdictions (chapter 9).
- **ABC.** WARN applies. The company is liable; the assignee will treat WARN claims as priority or general unsecured depending on the specific facts.
- **Chapter 7.** WARN claims can be pursued in the bankruptcy case; wages claims for the notice period are typically priority under §507(a)(4) up to the per-employee cap and general unsecured above the cap. Officers may be personally liable.
- **Chapter 11.** Similar to Chapter 7 for the estate liability; the DIP may have specific authority to pay pre-petition WARN claims as part of critical wage orders (though this is contested and jurisdiction-specific).

The Bankruptcy Code does not provide a general shield from WARN Act liability. Filing bankruptcy does not solve the WARN problem — it just moves it into the case.

## Common failure modes

Six recurring failure modes in shutdown-WARN compliance:

1. **"We'll use the faltering-company exception."** Almost always over-optimistic. The exception is narrow, is available only to plant closings, and requires specific contemporaneous documentation of active fundraising. Assume it does not apply.
2. **"The mass-layoff didn't hit the threshold."** The 90-day aggregation rule under §2102(d) can aggregate smaller reductions in force that individually would not trigger WARN into a covered event. A "layoff last month, another layoff this month, closing next month" pattern typically aggregates.
3. **"We're a small company."** The 100-employee threshold is company-wide, not site-specific. A company with 60 people at HQ and 50 in a remote site is at 110 and is covered.
4. **"The employees know we're in trouble."** Informal knowledge is not statutory notice. The DOL regulations require specific written notice with specific content.
5. **"The state statute is the same as federal."** Cal-WARN has lower thresholds and narrower exceptions; NY WARN has a longer notice period; NJ WARN has mandatory severance. The state overlays are material.
6. **"The buyer will hire the employees on day one after the sale."** May reduce or eliminate WARN exposure on the seller side, but only if the transaction is structured that way and the buyer's commitments are firm and documented in the purchase agreement. A verbal understanding is not a defence.

## What good WARN compliance produces

A well-executed WARN compliance workstream produces:

- Timely notice to all affected employees, their representatives, the state dislocated-worker unit, and the chief local elected official.
- A specific severance-and-release package that either provides pay-in-lieu of notice (defence for missed notice) or supplements the notice with additional consideration in exchange for a release of claims.
- Documented factual basis for any exception invoked, contemporaneous with the shutdown decision.
- State-agency filings for any applicable state mini-WARN statute (California EDD, New York DOL, New Jersey DOL, and others as applicable).
- Coordination with the shutdown mechanism (ABC assignee, Chapter 7 trustee, or Chapter 11 DIP) so that WARN-related claims are handled correctly in the priority framework.
- A defensible posture if litigation is filed post-shutdown — which happens frequently regardless of compliance quality, so the goal is to be prepared to defend rather than to avoid.

## Summary

WARN Act compliance is the single most common source of officer-and-director personal-liability exposure and post-close plaintiff-firm litigation in a shutdown. Federal WARN requires 60 days' notice at a 100-employee company for a plant closing (50+ single-site) or mass layoff (500+ single-site, or 50–499 constituting 33%+); state mini-WARN statutes in California, New York, New Jersey, Illinois, and other jurisdictions add lower thresholds, longer notice periods, mandatory severance (NJ), and narrower exceptions on top of the federal baseline. The three federal exceptions — faltering company, unforeseeable business circumstance, natural disaster — are tightly construed and frequently unavailable in a shutdown that was foreseeable in the prior quarter. The standard damage-mitigation posture (pay-in-lieu of notice, severance-conditioned-on-release, state-agency filings) manages the exposure but does not eliminate it. The next chapter turns to the wider set of employee-obligation workstreams (final pay, PTO, COBRA, severance, non-competes) that run in parallel with WARN and complete the employee-side shutdown workstream.
