# Sell-Side Quality of Earnings — Pre-Empting the Buyer's Finding

## Why this matters

The single most-underinvested sell-side artefact in venture-backed M&A is the pre-transaction quality-of-earnings report. Founding teams and their bankers spend six figures on a data room, another six figures on M&A counsel, and treat the QoE as either optional or as something the *buyer* is going to do anyway. The buyer will indeed do a QoE — that fact is not in dispute. The question is whether the target arrives at the buyer's Q of E findings with a document already in the buyer's hands that names the adjustments, defends the presentation of run-rate EBITDA, walks through the ASC 606 posture, and cites the specific evidence in the data room supporting each conclusion. When the target arrives with that document, three things happen that materially reshape the transaction. First, the buyer's QoE completes weeks earlier because the buyer's provider is *reviewing and testing* a document rather than *building one from raw ledgers*. Second, the buyer's price re-trade is anchored to a much smaller delta between the buyer's view of EBITDA and the sell-side view, because both parties are working from a common presentation. Third, when the buyer's provider does surface an adjustment the sell-side did not book, the discussion is a specific line-item rebuttal rather than a global dispute over the target's ability to close its books. In practitioner practice, targets that arrive with a well-prepared sell-side QoE close at LOI headline more often than targets that do not — the exact incidence varies by sector and study, but the direction is well-established across the SRS Acquiom and Big Four practitioner materials.

The sell-side QoE is not the target's own management-reporting EBITDA reconciliation. It is a specific-purpose transaction document, authored by a Big Four accounting firm or a QoE-specialist firm (Frank, Rimerman + Co., Riveron, Kroll, RSM, Grant Thornton, BDO, Cherry Bekaert), designed for the buyer's QoE provider to read and test. It has a particular structure, a particular set of adjustment categories, and a particular presentation architecture that has evolved in practitioner practice over the last twenty years. This chapter installs that architecture.

Two vocabulary points. First, the term *quality of earnings* is not a GAAP concept; it is a practitioner analytic framework layered on top of financial statements. A QoE report is not an audit and does not opine on GAAP compliance. Second, *sell-side QoE* is sometimes called a *seller QoE* or a *vendor due diligence* (the UK / European term); the substance is the same.

## What a QoE report is (and is not)

A QoE report is a practitioner analysis of the *sustainability* and *cash-conversion* of a target's earnings, presented in a form that anchors a transaction discussion of enterprise value. The typical major sections:

- **Executive summary** — reported EBITDA for the trailing twelve months (TTM), the run-rate EBITDA after normalisation adjustments, the pro-forma EBITDA reflecting run-rate adjustments and any pending transactions, and the working-capital position.
- **Adjusted-EBITDA bridge** — the specific adjustments (owner comp normalisation, non-recurring items, run-rate revenue, deferred-revenue accounting alignment, ASC 606 restatement, one-time expenses, out-of-period items) that reconcile reported EBITDA to adjusted-EBITDA. Each adjustment is a line item with dollar amount and a supporting-evidence pointer to the data room.
- **Revenue analysis** — cohort retention, gross vs. net retention, revenue recognised vs. billings, backlog and deferred revenue, revenue by customer / product / geography, revenue concentration.
- **Cost analysis** — cost of revenue and gross margin walk, operating-expense analysis by category, headcount analysis, sales-and-marketing efficiency (magic number, LTV/CAC, payback).
- **Working-capital analysis** — accounts receivable ageing, accounts payable, deferred revenue as a working-capital component, historical seasonality of working capital, normalised working-capital estimate for the purchase-price adjustment mechanic (mod-103 chapter 4).
- **Cash-flow analysis** — EBITDA-to-cash conversion, capitalised software development costs, capital expenditure history, changes in working capital.
- **Debt-like items** — items that a buyer will treat as debt-like in the enterprise-value-to-equity-value bridge — deferred revenue (partially), unpaid earn-outs from prior acquisitions, unpaid transaction bonuses, unfunded pension obligations, uncured deferred-comp balances, sales-tax accruals not-yet-remitted, unclaimed-property, litigation reserves.
- **Off-balance-sheet items** — operating leases now on-balance-sheet under ASC 842 but historically off, contingent liabilities, guaranteed obligations.
- **Trend-and-quality analysis** — quarterly and monthly trends, historical variance to budget, historical write-offs and credit memos, unusual month-end journal entries.

What a QoE report *is not*: an audit, a review, an attestation, or a certification. A QoE provider does not opine that the financial statements are GAAP-compliant. Practitioner language typically says the provider "has performed procedures agreed with the [seller / buyer]" and the deliverable is a report of "findings" rather than an opinion. This scope-and-purpose framing matters because it distinguishes the QoE provider's obligation from the audit firm's; a QoE provider is not liable to the buyer as an auditor would be under the professional-services standards.

## The pre-transaction QoE as marketing document

The sell-side QoE has two distinct audiences. Its *primary* audience is the buyer's QoE provider. Its *secondary* audience is the buyer's deal team (corp-dev VP, sponsor's operating partner, board-level buyer-review committee) that will read the executive summary and the adjusted-EBITDA bridge without necessarily digesting the underlying workpapers.

Framing decisions that follow from the two-audience recognition:

- **The executive summary is written for a deal-team read.** It names the headline reported-EBITDA number, walks through the adjustment categories at a summary level, and lands on an adjusted-EBITDA number that supports the LOI purchase-price presentation. It does not bury the number in caveats; it does not present adjustments in the buyer's-favour direction only.
- **The adjustment bridge is exhaustive and defensible.** Every adjustment has a defensible rationale, a supporting-evidence pointer into the data room, and a fair-and-balanced application. A bridge that adds back $12M of owner comp but does not adjust for $8M of run-rate hiring the target has committed to is not credible; the buyer's provider will call it out and the credibility of every other adjustment suffers.
- **The workpapers are ready for testing.** The buyer's provider will re-perform selected adjustments to confirm the reconciling calculation. The seller's provider anticipates this by preparing the workpaper package with the calculation, the supporting-evidence citation, and the derivation from source data.
- **The report is written to survive a buyer-side rebuttal.** The buyer's provider will attempt to disallow adjustments the seller included, will add adjustments the seller omitted, and will present its own adjusted-EBITDA number. The seller's provider anticipates this by pre-empting the disputable adjustments — footnoting the seller's rationale, citing the practitioner precedent for each treatment, and defending against the common buyer counter-arguments.

## EBITDA-normalisation adjustments — the six main categories

The adjustment categories are practitioner-standardised. The six main categories for a growth-stage SaaS / AI target:

### 1. Owner and executive compensation normalisation

The target's founder-CEO may be paid $0 base plus equity (typical early-stage) or a below-market $150K salary (typical growth-stage but pre-professional-CEO). The founder's actual market-comp for the role and stage would be $300–500K base plus significant equity. A buyer replacing the founder with a market-comp CEO will incur that incremental cost; the QoE normalisation flips this into a *reduction* of adjusted EBITDA — the reported EBITDA overstates the run-rate operating profit because the target has been running on below-market executive comp.

Conversely, if the target's founder-CEO has been paying themselves at above-market comp (occasional in bootstrapped-then-venture-financed companies), the QoE normalises to the market rate and *adds back* the excess to adjusted EBITDA.

The same normalisation applies to founder-executives in other C-suite roles, to family members on payroll, and to any related-party compensation arrangement that would not survive the change of control.

Practitioner discipline: cite a specific compensation-benchmarking source (Radford, Pave, Option Impact, or a sector-specific study). The buyer's provider will otherwise substitute their own benchmark; anchoring the seller's normalisation to a named third-party benchmark reduces the space for dispute.

### 2. Non-recurring items

Items that hit the historical period but are not expected to recur. Common examples:

- Legal fees for a specific litigation matter now resolved.
- Restructuring charges from a prior workforce reduction.
- Impairment of a prior acquisition.
- Costs of the transaction itself (banker fees, legal fees, QoE fees, accounting-cleanup costs) — these are almost always added back.
- Costs of a prior aborted transaction attempt.
- Executive-severance one-off events.
- Business-interruption events (a specific customer bankruptcy, a data-centre outage, a pandemic-related one-time cost).
- Political-donation or executive-personal expenses run through the P&L.

Practitioner discipline: distinguish carefully between *non-recurring* (a specific past event that will not repeat) and *variable-but-recurring* (a category of cost that varies year-to-year but is not going away). A one-off legal-fee spike from patent-troll litigation now settled is non-recurring; the target's ongoing $200K/year run-rate for IP-portfolio maintenance is variable but recurring and should not be added back.

### 3. Run-rate revenue

The reported historical revenue is typically not the run-rate revenue at the transaction date. Adjustments that walk from historical revenue to run-rate:

- **Full-period annualisation of customers signed mid-period.** A customer signed in June at $500K ACV contributed $250K to the reported year but contributes $500K to run-rate. The adjustment recognises the incremental $250K in run-rate revenue.
- **Removal of one-time revenue.** Migration fees, professional-services engagements that will not repeat, one-time perpetual-licence sales in a company that is transitioning to subscription.
- **Removal of revenue from customers who have churned.** A customer that generated $1M in the trailing twelve months but churned in month 11 is not part of the run-rate; the adjustment removes their contribution from run-rate.
- **Reversal of ASC 606 revenue-recognition timing that does not reflect economic activity.** A three-year contract with $3M paid upfront and $1M recognised per year is $1M of GAAP revenue per year but $3M of contracted revenue in the year of signing. The QoE typically presents both a GAAP revenue and a contracted-revenue view, and depending on the buyer's frame, adjusts run-rate to reflect one or the other.

Practitioner discipline: run-rate adjustments are the most-disputed category. Buyers will discount an aggressive run-rate presentation. The seller's provider anchors run-rate to a specific methodology — typically the "annualised value of active subscriptions as of the transaction reference date" — and defends the methodology rather than defending the adjusted number in isolation.

### 4. Deferred-revenue and revenue-recognition-timing adjustments

Deferred revenue is the liability associated with cash the target has collected for services not yet delivered. It is analytically important for two reasons: (a) buyers typically treat deferred revenue as a debt-like item in the enterprise-value-to-equity-value bridge (because the buyer inherits the delivery obligation without the corresponding future cash collection); (b) the target's deferred-revenue accounting under ASC 606 may or may not match the way a buyer's Q of E provider would present the target's revenue.

The QoE analyses:

- **Deferred-revenue rollforward** — opening balance, additions from bookings, releases from delivery, ending balance.
- **The "fair-value haircut" of deferred revenue in purchase accounting** — under ASC 805, an acquirer restates the target's deferred revenue to its fair value at the acquisition date. Historically this often reduced the acquirer's post-close revenue relative to the target's pre-close revenue. FASB ASU 2021-08 changed this for revenue-contract-with-customer deferred revenue arising under ASC 606, aligning acquirer post-close revenue more closely with the target's pre-close presentation. The QoE walks the buyer through the target's pre-close deferred-revenue balances and the acquirer's post-close revenue exposure.
- **Revenue-recognition posture** — the target's ASC 606 policy across the five-step model (contract identification, performance-obligation identification, transaction-price determination, transaction-price allocation, revenue recognition on performance-obligation satisfaction). Any nonstandard revenue-recognition treatment (bill-and-hold, principal-vs-agent for reseller arrangements, right-of-return provisions, variable consideration, contract-modification treatment) is called out with the target's rationale.

### 5. One-time expenses and cost-basis adjustments

Costs that hit the period but should not be reflected in run-rate:

- **Signing bonuses and executive-onboarding costs** for hires now stable — the ongoing salary is run-rate; the one-time signing bonus is not.
- **Consulting engagements now concluded** — a $500K consulting spend for a specific initiative now shipped is not run-rate.
- **One-time infrastructure migrations** — a cloud-provider migration or a data-centre exit that generated one-time costs.
- **Founder-vehicle, personal-expense, and country-club expenses** run through the P&L (occasional in founder-controlled companies; a QoE-standard add-back with founder disclosure).
- **Related-party-expense normalisations** where the target has been paying above-market for a service provided by a founder-related entity.
- **Stock-based compensation** — a special case. Most QoE presentations exclude stock-based compensation from adjusted EBITDA (following the practitioner convention that treats SBC as a non-cash charge), but some buyer QoE providers add it back as an economic cost. The seller's provider names the treatment explicitly and shows both views.

### 6. Pro-forma adjustments for pending transactions

If the target has closed or committed to a transaction during the historical period, the QoE presents the pro-forma effect:

- **Recent acquisitions** — the pro-forma contribution of the acquired business as if owned for the full historical period.
- **Recent divestitures** — the pro-forma removal of the divested business from historical results.
- **Pending customer wins with contract signed but revenue not yet booked** — sometimes disclosed as a "signed pipeline" adjustment rather than a run-rate adjustment; buyer treatment varies.
- **Pending customer losses** — churned customers whose termination notice has been received but whose contract term has not fully run out.

## The presentation architecture that survives a buyer-side rebuttal

The buyer's QoE provider will read the seller's report with the express intent of disputing adjustments that reduce the pool of enterprise value. Anticipation of this rebuttal shapes the seller's presentation:

**The adjustment bridge is a single-page waterfall.** The reader sees reported EBITDA on the far left, each adjustment as a discrete waterfall bar, and adjusted EBITDA on the far right. Positive adjustments (add-backs) and negative adjustments (subtractions) are colour-differentiated. Each bar has a dollar value.

**Each bar has a paragraph of narrative.** The narrative names the adjustment, cites the specific dollar amount, describes the accounting for the amount, and defends why the treatment is appropriate. The narrative is written for the buyer's QoE provider — with references to practitioner precedent and to the supporting evidence in the data room. A one-line "add-back for one-time legal fees" is not defensible; a paragraph that names the specific matter, cites the settlement filing, notes that the matter is now closed and the risk of a related future matter is discussed in the litigation workstream, and points to the legal-invoice detail in workpaper 3.7 is defensible.

**The workpapers are indexed and cross-referenced.** Every adjustment in the bridge has a workpaper reference (e.g., "See Workpaper 4.2 for owner-comp normalisation methodology"). The workpapers are indexed and provided in the data room's Financial folder. A buyer's QoE provider that wants to re-perform an adjustment reads the workpaper, tests the calculation against the underlying source data (which is also in the data room), and either accepts the seller's treatment or notes a proposed rebuttal.

**The methodology memo is the last line of defence.** For each adjustment category, a methodology memo describes the seller's approach. For owner-comp, the memo cites the compensation benchmark. For non-recurring items, the memo defines the criteria for classifying an item as non-recurring (typically "not expected to occur in the next twelve months" combined with a specific past event). For run-rate revenue, the memo defines the annualisation methodology and the customer-cohort treatment. When the buyer's QoE provider disputes an adjustment, the discussion often collapses to a debate over methodology; a well-written methodology memo pre-empts most of that debate.

**The buyer's likely rebuttal is pre-answered.** For the adjustments most likely to be contested (owner-comp normalisation, run-rate revenue, deferred-revenue treatment, SBC exclusion), the report includes a "considerations" paragraph naming the buyer-favourable alternative treatment and the seller's rationale for the presentation chosen. This does not concede the point; it demonstrates that the seller's provider considered the alternative and had a defensible reason for their approach.

## Working-capital and closing-cash mechanics

The QoE's working-capital analysis feeds directly into the purchase-price-adjustment mechanic in the definitive agreement (mod-103 chapter 4). The buyer and seller need to agree on a *target working capital* — the normalised working-capital amount the seller commits to deliver at closing — and any deviation at closing triggers a dollar-for-dollar adjustment to the purchase price. The QoE's job is to present a defensible target-working-capital number.

The typical presentation:

- **Historical working-capital by month** for the last twenty-four months — showing seasonality and trend.
- **Analysis of any non-recurring working-capital events** that distort the historical picture (a one-time collection surge, a one-time inventory build for a discontinued product).
- **The recommended target-working-capital number**, expressed either as a fixed dollar amount or as a formula (e.g., "median of the trailing 12 months' month-end working-capital"). The formula approach is more common when the target has meaningful seasonality.
- **Any deferred-revenue special treatment** — whether deferred revenue is included in the working-capital calculation, excluded, or partially included (this is a heavily-negotiated point because deferred revenue is both a working-capital-like current liability and a debt-like item in the EV-to-equity bridge).

Adjacent to working capital, the QoE presents the *closing-cash* and *closing-debt* analysis — the target's closing-cash balance is added to the purchase-price bridge, and the target's closing-debt (bank debt, venture debt, capital-lease liabilities, other debt-like items) is subtracted. The QoE identifies each debt-like item and quantifies it.

## The QoE-provider selection decision

The choice of QoE provider matters. The specifics:

- **The Big Four (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC)** — the incumbent providers on larger deals ($100M+ enterprise value). Broadest sector coverage. Highest brand recognition. Highest fees. Best-positioned for transactions where the buyer is a public company and the buyer's audit committee prefers a Big Four sell-side deliverable.
- **QoE-specialist firms (Frank, Rimerman + Co.; Riveron; Kroll; Cherry Bekaert; RSM; Grant Thornton; BDO)** — the deep-bench QoE practices that have grown up alongside the mid-market PE and venture-M&A wave. Often more responsive than the Big Four for mid-market deals ($30M–$300M enterprise value). Frank, Rimerman + Co. in particular has a strong tech and venture practice; Riveron has a strong PE-portfolio practice; Kroll (formerly Duff & Phelps) has broad valuation-and-QoE bench; RSM and Grant Thornton have national practices with sector specialisation.
- **The target's audit firm** — the target's ongoing-audit firm can typically not perform the sell-side QoE for independence-and-conflict reasons (particularly if the buyer's audit firm is going to inherit the audit). Most targets engage a QoE provider distinct from their audit firm.

Selection considerations:

- **Buyer expectation** — for a strategic buyer that is a public company, or a sponsor with a house-standard QoE-provider expectation, matching the buyer's likely expectation reduces friction.
- **Sector fit** — SaaS-metric-normalisation, deferred-revenue-treatment, and ARR-vs-GAAP-revenue reconciliation are specific analytic areas where sector-experienced QoE providers dramatically outperform generalist providers.
- **Timing** — the QoE typically takes six to twelve weeks from engagement to first draft. Engaging late in a process means either the QoE runs after LOI signing (which is disadvantageous — the buyer's QoE will be running in parallel with much of the seller's QoE, defeating the pre-empt purpose) or the QoE is truncated.
- **Cost** — QoE fees for a mid-market transaction typically run $150K–$500K depending on complexity, sector, and provider. Cost is a real consideration but is minor against typical banker fees, legal fees, and R&W-insurance premiums.

Engagement letter watch: the QoE provider's engagement letter should permit the seller to share the final report with prospective buyers (and their advisors) under a reliance-letter mechanism. Without reliance, the buyer's QoE provider will treat the seller's QoE as unreliable input and re-perform every analysis from scratch, defeating most of the value.

## The "sell-side QoE handed to the buyer at LOI" pattern

The modal timing pattern in modern practitioner practice:

- **Pre-marketing phase.** The seller's QoE is engaged three-to-six months before a formal process launch. First-draft QoE completes before the CIM goes to bidders.
- **Round 1 (IOI).** The QoE is not distributed at IOI — it goes only to the shortlisted bidders as they enter round 2.
- **Round 2 shortlist.** The QoE is delivered to shortlisted bidders under NDA, either directly by the sell-side banker or via the data room. Bidders' QoE providers begin their review immediately.
- **LOI signing.** By LOI signing, the winning bidder's QoE provider has typically completed a first-pass review of the seller's QoE and has surfaced any material disputes. The LOI headline number reflects the winning bidder's view of adjusted EBITDA after this preliminary review.
- **Post-LOI diligence.** The buyer's QoE provider performs detailed testing of the seller's QoE and issues a formal QoE report to the buyer. Disputes are worked through the diligence process. Material findings feed into the price-renegotiation choreography (chapter 5).

The pattern speeds diligence by weeks-to-months, reduces the LOI-to-signing price re-trade risk, and materially reduces the probability of a deal-collapse from a late-surfacing financial finding. It is not costless — the seller pays for the QoE — but the ROI in transaction-execution certainty is high enough that it has become the modal practice in mid-market and larger venture-M&A.

## Common QoE-preparation mistakes

- **Under-scoping the engagement.** A QoE engaged for a "quick look" rather than a full report produces a document the buyer's QoE cannot rely on and does not achieve the pre-empt objective. Engage the QoE for a full-scope report.
- **Aggressive add-backs that will not survive.** The seller's provider is tempted by the seller's business logic ("we're planning to change how we do this next year, so the current cost isn't run-rate"). The buyer's provider will reject speculative add-backs. Include only defensible, evidence-supported adjustments.
- **Omission of adjustments that reduce EBITDA.** The seller's provider is tempted to omit add-backs that would reduce EBITDA (e.g., normalising up an owner-comp that is currently above-market, adding a cost the target has committed to but not yet incurred). Omitting these makes the report obviously one-sided and undermines the credibility of every other adjustment.
- **Working-capital-target games.** Setting a target-working-capital number materially below the historical average, hoping the buyer will accept it and the seller will benefit from the closing-adjustment mechanic. The buyer's QoE will surface the discrepancy; the seller's credibility takes a hit.
- **Ignoring debt-like items.** Failure to identify unpaid transaction bonuses, deferred-comp balances, unpaid earn-outs from prior acquisitions, unpaid sales-tax liabilities, or environmental reserves. These will surface in buyer-side diligence; if the seller has not surfaced them first, they read as concealment.
- **Late-engagement.** Engaging the QoE after LOI signing loses most of the pre-empt value. Engage before the CIM ships if possible; before round-2 shortlist at latest.
- **No reliance letter.** Delivering the QoE without a reliance-letter mechanism means the buyer's QoE provider cannot rely on it, so they re-do everything. Structure the engagement so that a reliance letter can be issued to the eventual buyer's QoE provider.

## Summary

The sell-side quality-of-earnings is a specific-purpose transaction document — authored by a Big Four or QoE-specialist firm, aimed at the buyer's QoE provider and buyer's deal team, structured around an adjusted-EBITDA bridge that reconciles reported EBITDA to a run-rate normalised EBITDA. Its six main adjustment categories — owner-comp normalisation, non-recurring items, run-rate revenue, deferred-revenue accounting, one-time expenses, and pro-forma pending transactions — are practitioner-standardised, and its presentation architecture (single-page waterfall, indexed workpapers, methodology memos, pre-answered buyer rebuttals) has evolved to survive a buyer-side rebuttal. The QoE feeds directly into the working-capital-target mechanic in the definitive agreement, into the closing-cash / closing-debt bridge, and into the enterprise-value-to-equity-value walk that anchors the purchase price. Delivered to the winning bidder at LOI, it materially speeds diligence and reduces the LOI-to-signing price re-trade risk.
