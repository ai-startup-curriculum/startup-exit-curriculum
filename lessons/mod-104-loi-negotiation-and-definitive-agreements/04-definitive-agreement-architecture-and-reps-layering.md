# Definitive-Agreement Architecture and the Reps-and-Warranties Layering

## Why this matters

The definitive agreement — Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA), Merger Agreement, or Asset Purchase Agreement (APA), depending on the corporate form from mod-103 chapter 1 — is the contract that actually consummates the transaction. Everything up to LOI signing was a marketing exercise and a preliminary negotiation. Everything after LOI signing until closing is the drafting, red-lining, and negotiation of this document (plus its disclosure schedules, plus the ancillary documents — the escrow agreement, the R&W policy, the employment agreements, the transition services agreement). By deal close, the definitive agreement is typically 100–250 pages of contract text and another 200–1000+ pages of disclosure schedules. It is the single most important artefact of the entire transaction.

The definitive agreement has a well-established modal architecture that has been refined over decades of practice. Buyer's counsel almost always drafts the first cut; seller's counsel red-lines it; the parties negotiate. Learning the modal architecture — what goes in each section, why each section is there, and what the buyer-vs-seller tension is in each — is the entry price to any competent M&A negotiation. This chapter installs that architecture with a focus on the **reps-and-warranties** layering that is where the largest portion of the negotiating time is spent.

The reps-and-warranties (also called "representations and warranties" or just "reps") are the specific factual statements the seller makes about the target company. They are the vehicle by which transaction risk is allocated between buyer and seller: if a rep turns out to be inaccurate at signing or (subject to the bring-down mechanic) at closing, the buyer has a claim against the seller under the indemnification package (see chapter 6) or against the R&W insurer (see chapter 7). Which reps are included, how each one is scoped, what qualifiers ("materiality," "knowledge," "reasonably expected to result in a Material Adverse Effect") apply, and what disclosure schedules the seller attaches — this is where the substantive risk allocation happens. Understanding the reps-and-warranties layering is understanding what the transaction is actually about.

## The definitive-agreement skeleton

A modal Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA) or Merger Agreement is organised into a small number of top-level sections. Names and section numbers vary by drafter (WSGR / Cooley / Orrick / Fried Frank / Wachtell all have house styles); the substantive content is nearly universal.

### Article I — Definitions

The defined-terms glossary. Every capitalised term in the agreement is defined here. Definitions matter — the "Material Adverse Effect" definition is where multi-week negotiations happen; the "Company" definition sets the scope of the reps; the "Environmental Laws" definition scopes the environmental rep; the "Intellectual Property" definition scopes the IP rep. The article is often 20–40 pages by itself for a substantial transaction.

### Article II — Purchase and Sale (or "Merger" in a Merger Agreement)

The mechanical provisions that describe the transaction itself:

- What is being sold or exchanged (stock of Target; assets and assumed liabilities of Target; membership interests; etc.).
- The purchase price and its components (base cash consideration, stock consideration, structured consideration, escrow, holdback).
- The closing mechanics (closing date, closing location, deliverables at closing, wire instructions).
- The purchase-price adjustment mechanics (working-capital adjustment, closing-cash adjustment, closing-debt adjustment, transaction-expenses adjustment). See mod-103 chapter 4 for the design mechanics; this article implements them.
- The treatment of vested and unvested equity, the option pool, RSUs, warrants, ESPP, convertible notes, and any other target-issued securities.
- The escrow and holdback mechanics — how much, in what form, held by whom, released on what schedule and conditions.
- Any earn-out mechanics (see mod-103 chapter 3 for design; this article implements).
- Any rollover-equity mechanics.

### Article III — Representations and Warranties of the Company

The seller's reps about the target company. This is the core risk-allocation section and typically the longest article in the agreement — 30–60 pages of specific statements about the target's corporate existence, capitalisation, financial statements, absence of undisclosed liabilities, contracts, IP, employment, tax, environmental, compliance, litigation, and dozens of other specific topics. The rest of this chapter develops the layering of these reps.

### Article IV — Representations and Warranties of the Stockholders (or Sellers)

For a stock deal where the target's stockholders are the actual sellers (rather than the target itself), the stockholders make individual reps about their ownership of the target stock, their capacity to sell, and specific matters related to the sale. This is separate from Article III (which is company-level) and typically much shorter (2–5 pages).

### Article V — Representations and Warranties of the Buyer

The buyer's reps. Typically much shorter than the seller's reps because the buyer is only warranting a limited set of things — the buyer's corporate existence and capacity, the buyer's authority to enter the agreement, the buyer's financing (for a cash deal, that adequate funds are available; for a stock deal, that the acquirer stock has been duly authorised and issued), the absence of consents or conflicts, litigation, and (for a public-acquirer stock deal) the accuracy of the acquirer's SEC filings.

### Article VI — Covenants

The covenants govern the interim period between signing and closing and, for some, extend post-closing:

- **Interim operating covenants** — how the target must operate the business between signing and closing. See chapter 5.
- **No-shop / non-solicitation covenants** — the target cannot pursue alternative transactions during the interim period. (This is separate from and additional to the LOI-stage no-shop; the definitive-agreement no-shop covers the signing-to-closing period.)
- **Access covenants** — the buyer's access to the target's records, personnel, and facilities during the interim period.
- **Cooperation covenants** — the parties' obligations to cooperate on regulatory filings (HSR, CFIUS, foreign antitrust), third-party consents, and closing preparation.
- **Employee-matters covenants** — treatment of employees, benefits transition, WARN Act compliance (if applicable).
- **Post-closing non-compete / non-solicit covenants** — restrictions on the sellers post-closing.
- **Post-closing tax cooperation, insurance-transition, and other cleanup covenants.**

### Article VII — Closing Conditions

The specific conditions that must be satisfied (or waived) before the parties are obligated to close:

- Bring-down of representations and warranties (see chapter 5).
- Absence of Material Adverse Effect (MAC / MAE — see chapter 5).
- Third-party consents obtained.
- Regulatory clearances obtained (HSR waiting period expired, CFIUS clearance if applicable, foreign-antitrust clearances if applicable).
- No injunction or other legal impediment to closing.
- Delivery of specified closing documents.
- (Sometimes) financing available.

### Article VIII — Termination

The specific circumstances under which the parties can terminate the agreement:

- Mutual written consent.
- Outside date (typically 3–9 months from signing — if the deal has not closed by the outside date, either party can terminate).
- Breach by the other party (subject to cure periods).
- Failure of a closing condition (subject to specific mechanics).
- Legal impediment (an injunction that becomes permanent, or a governmental prohibition).
- (For public-target agreements) the target's board changing its recommendation, or the target accepting a superior proposal.

And the consequences of termination — reverse termination fee, break-up fee, expense reimbursement (see chapter 2), or (for some terminations) specific-performance rights.

### Article IX — Indemnification and Survival

The indemnification package that governs post-closing claims for breach of reps and warranties, breach of covenants, and specified indemnifiable items. Survival periods, caps, baskets, de-minimis, sole-and-exclusive-remedy language, fraud carve-outs. See chapter 6 for the full package design.

### Article X — Miscellaneous

Governing law (typically Delaware for a Delaware target, sometimes New York), forum selection (Delaware Chancery for a Delaware-target M&A dispute), notices, amendment procedures, severability, entire-agreement clause, counterparts, execution mechanics.

### The exhibits and disclosure schedules

The definitive agreement is typically accompanied by:

- **Disclosure schedules** — the seller's specific disclosures against each rep. If Rep 3.7 says "Except as set forth on Schedule 3.7, Target has no material undisclosed liabilities," the actual list of material undisclosed liabilities is on Schedule 3.7. Schedules can run hundreds or thousands of pages for a complex target.
- **Buyer disclosure schedule** — the buyer's specific disclosures against each buyer rep (typically much shorter).
- **Forms of ancillary documents** — the escrow agreement, the R&W policy (or the R&W policy binder), the transition services agreement, the employment agreements, the non-compete agreements, the ESOP treatment plan, the option-conversion certificates.

## The seven-layer reps stack

The reps and warranties are organised into layers that reflect their content and their treatment in the indemnification package (see chapter 6). Different practitioners use slightly different layer names, but the modal seven-layer stack is:

### Layer 1 — Fundamental representations

*Fundamental reps* are the small set of foundational statements that go to the essence of the transaction — if any of them is materially wrong, the entire transaction is compromised. Fundamental reps typically include:

- **Corporate organisation and existence.** Target is validly incorporated, in good standing, has the corporate power to conduct its business.
- **Capitalisation.** The specific stated capitalisation of Target (number of shares of each class outstanding, options, warrants, convertible notes, all of it) is accurate. This rep is where sophisticated buyers spend enormous diligence effort — an inaccurate capitalisation rep means the buyer bought a different company than they thought.
- **Authorisation.** Target has the corporate authority to enter into the agreement and consummate the transaction; the transaction has been duly authorised by all necessary corporate action.
- **Title to shares (in a stock deal) or title to assets (in an asset deal).** The seller has good and valid title to the shares (or assets) being sold, free and clear of liens.
- **No conflicts.** Consummating the transaction does not violate the target's charter, any material contract, or applicable law.

Fundamental reps are treated specially in the indemnification package: they survive longer (typically the full statute of limitations, or 6–7 years), are capped at the full purchase price (not the smaller general-rep cap), and are typically not subject to any basket (first-dollar recovery). Chapter 6 develops the mechanics.

### Layer 2 — Tax representations

*Tax reps* address the target's compliance with all applicable tax laws — federal, state, local, foreign. Standard tax reps cover:

- All tax returns have been filed as required.
- All taxes shown on those returns have been paid.
- No tax audits or proceedings are pending or threatened.
- No tax positions taken are inconsistent with applicable law.
- The target's tax attributes (NOLs, credits, basis) are accurately described.
- Specific reps about IRC §280G analysis (see mod-103 chapter 6), IRC §409A compliance, IRC §162(m) covered-employee identification, transfer-pricing compliance, indirect-tax compliance (sales / use / VAT).

Tax reps are usually given fundamental treatment for indemnification purposes — long survival (statute of limitations or a specifically negotiated 6-year period), high cap (up to purchase price), no basket. Tax exposure is one of the largest historical sources of post-closing indemnification claims.

### Layer 3 — General representations (the "business reps")

*General reps* are the workhorse layer — the specific factual statements about the target's operations, financial position, and business condition. Standard general reps include:

- **Financial statements.** The target's financial statements fairly present the target's financial position and results of operations in accordance with GAAP (with any specifically-noted exceptions for non-GAAP items).
- **Absence of undisclosed liabilities.** Target has no material liabilities except those on the balance sheet, incurred in ordinary course since the balance-sheet date, or specifically disclosed.
- **Absence of certain changes.** Between the balance-sheet date and signing, Target has operated in ordinary course and has not undertaken specified out-of-ordinary-course actions (dividends, material acquisitions, material dispositions, material contracts, material litigation, etc.).
- **Contracts.** Target has disclosed all material contracts; each disclosed contract is valid, binding, and in force; Target is not in material default; no counterparty is in material default; no material contract has been threatened with termination.
- **Real property.** Target owns or leases the real property necessary for its business; the property is in good condition; no material dispute with landlords.
- **Personal property and inventory.** Standard reps about title, condition, insurability.
- **Litigation.** Disclosure of pending and threatened litigation, and rep about no material litigation not disclosed.
- **Employee benefits.** All employee benefit plans are disclosed and are administered in compliance with ERISA and applicable law.
- **Insurance.** All material insurance policies are disclosed and in force.
- **Suppliers and customers.** Disclosure of top suppliers and customers and rep about no material adverse changes in those relationships.

General reps are the most-negotiated layer. Their scope, qualifiers (materiality, knowledge, MAE-qualified), and disclosure-schedule mechanics determine much of the substantive risk allocation.

### Layer 4 — Compliance-with-law and permits representations

*Compliance-with-law reps* address the target's regulatory posture:

- Target is in compliance with all applicable laws (subject to materiality qualifier).
- Target holds all material permits and licenses required to conduct its business.
- No governmental investigation is pending or threatened.
- No notice of violation has been received.

The scope of "applicable laws" is negotiated. For a heavily-regulated target (healthcare, financial services, telecom, aerospace-defense), the compliance-with-law rep is subdivided into specific regulatory sub-reps — HIPAA compliance, HITECH, Stark, Anti-Kickback for healthcare; SEC / FINRA / CFTC / state securities laws for financial services; FCC / state PUC for telecom; ITAR / EAR / OFAC / FCPA for aerospace-defense.

### Layer 5 — Environmental representations

*Environmental reps* address the target's environmental posture — historical contamination, ongoing environmental compliance, environmental permits, environmental litigation, hazardous-substance handling. Environmental exposure is a specific concern in asset-heavy businesses (manufacturing, real estate, mining, agriculture) and is often the subject of specific environmental diligence (Phase I ESA, sometimes Phase II).

For software / SaaS / AI targets, environmental reps are typically brief and low-risk. For asset-heavy targets, environmental reps are extensively negotiated and often supported by environmental representations-and-warranties insurance as a specific coverage.

### Layer 6 — IP representations

*Intellectual-property reps* address the target's IP portfolio and the target's freedom to operate:

- Target owns or has the right to use all IP necessary to conduct its business.
- Target's IP is validly registered / registered where required / capable of enforcement.
- No infringement claim against Target is pending or threatened.
- Target does not knowingly infringe any third-party IP.
- Target has valid licenses (both inbound and outbound).
- Employees and contractors have assigned IP to Target under valid IP-assignment agreements.
- Open-source software incorporated in Target's products has been managed to avoid material license-compliance issues (particularly GPL and other copyleft licenses that could infect proprietary code).
- Target has not disclosed material trade secrets without appropriate confidentiality protection.

IP reps for software / SaaS / AI targets are the *primary* risk-allocation layer. Open-source compliance, IP-assignment completeness, and freedom-to-operate risk are the specific diligence workstream focus areas.

### Layer 7 — Privacy and data-protection representations

*Privacy and data-protection reps* address the target's collection, use, storage, sharing, and disposal of personal data. Standard privacy reps cover:

- Compliance with applicable data-protection laws (US federal and state — GLBA, HIPAA, COPPA, CCPA / CPRA, state privacy laws; EU — GDPR; UK — UK GDPR; other jurisdictions).
- Adherence to published privacy notices.
- Consent mechanisms for personal-data collection where required.
- Adequate data-security controls (contractual — DPA / standard-contractual-clauses; technical — encryption, access controls; organisational — training, incident response).
- Absence of material data breaches (or disclosure of any material breaches).
- Absence of material privacy-related litigation or regulatory investigations.

Privacy reps for consumer-facing targets or targets that process sensitive personal data (health data, financial data, biometric data, children's data) are extensively negotiated and often supported by cyber-liability insurance as a specific coverage.

### Layer 7.5 (emerging) — AI-model representations

For AI targets — including AI-native software targets and traditional software targets with material AI-model components — a new layer of reps is emerging. Practice is still evolving; typical AI-model reps cover:

- The AI models Target uses (or has developed) have been trained on data Target has the right to use for that purpose.
- Third-party AI models (foundation-model APIs, open-source models) are used under valid licenses.
- Target's AI models comply with applicable AI laws where the target operates (EU AI Act, US state AI laws where applicable, sector-specific AI regulations).
- Target has appropriate AI-model documentation, evaluation, and governance in place.
- Target's AI-model outputs do not knowingly infringe third-party IP or generate specific categories of prohibited content.
- No material AI-model-related litigation or regulatory investigation is pending or threatened.

## Rep qualifiers — where the substantive negotiation happens

Each rep is qualified by a combination of the following, and the specific mix is where most of the negotiating time is spent:

### Materiality qualifiers

"Material" and its variants ("in all material respects," "material to Target and its subsidiaries taken as a whole") are used to scope the rep's coverage:

- **Unqualified rep.** "Target is in compliance with all applicable laws." — Any technical non-compliance, however trivial, is a breach.
- **Materiality-qualified rep.** "Target is in compliance with all applicable laws in all material respects." — Only non-compliance rising to the "material" level is a breach.
- **MAE-qualified rep.** "Except as would not reasonably be expected to result in a Material Adverse Effect, Target is in compliance with all applicable laws." — Only non-compliance rising to the MAE level is a breach.

The three levels are progressively more seller-favourable. Buyers push for unqualified reps (so they can catch any exposure); sellers push for materiality or MAE qualifiers (so trivial exposure is not a claim).

The negotiation typically resolves with materiality qualifiers on the general reps (Layer 3), MAE qualifiers on the compliance and environmental reps (Layers 4–5), and unqualified fundamental / tax reps (Layers 1–2).

### Knowledge qualifiers

"Knowledge" qualifiers limit the rep to what the target's specified representatives *know*:

- "To the Knowledge of Target," or
- "To Seller's Knowledge," where "Knowledge" is defined as the actual knowledge (or, in a broader definition, actual and constructive knowledge — what the person *should have known* after reasonable inquiry) of a specified set of individuals (the CEO, CFO, GC, and other named officers).

Knowledge qualifiers are used for reps where the seller cannot reasonably know the underlying fact without specific inquiry — for example, "To Seller's Knowledge, no third party has infringed Target's IP" (the seller cannot warrant no infringement has ever occurred anywhere; the seller can only warrant what the specified officers know).

Buyers push against Knowledge qualifiers (which shift the risk of unknown facts back to the seller); sellers push for them (which limits exposure to what was actually known). The typical resolution: Knowledge qualifiers on reps where the seller genuinely cannot warrant without qualification, and unqualified reps where the seller can warrant based on records or systematic verification.

### Time-scope qualifiers

"Since [date]" or "In the last three years" limits the rep to a specific time window. For a target with a long operating history, a time-scope qualifier is essential — no seller can warrant compliance with, say, environmental laws going back to the target's incorporation 30 years ago.

Standard time-scope windows: 3 years for most operational reps; 6 years or the specific statute of limitations for tax reps; the target's entire history for fundamental reps.

### "Except as set forth on Schedule X" qualifiers

The disclosure-schedule qualifier is the actual mechanism by which specific exceptions to the rep are carved out. If Rep 3.7 says "Except as set forth on Schedule 3.7, Target has no undisclosed liabilities," the seller then attaches Schedule 3.7 listing all the specific undisclosed liabilities that are excepted from the rep.

Disclosure schedules are where the actual risk allocation lives. A rep that looks broad and buyer-favourable in the contract text may be functionally narrow if the corresponding schedule discloses a lot of exceptions. The seller's counsel's job during the definitive-agreement drafting period is to populate the schedules exhaustively with every specific exception the seller wants carved out; the buyer's counsel's job is to review the schedules against the diligence-workstream findings and either accept the disclosure or push back on scope.

**The "10b-5 disclosure schedule" pattern** — for a public-company acquirer, disclosure schedules may include additional protection against securities-law liability by including specific 10b-5-style disclaimers. Practitioners handle this in specific ways depending on the acquirer's public status.

## The bring-down mechanic

The reps and warranties are given as of two dates: **signing** and **closing**. Between signing and closing (the interim period), the target continues to operate the business, and facts can change. The **bring-down** mechanic — a closing condition (see chapter 5) — provides that the reps are true and correct at closing to the specified standard.

The most-negotiated aspect of the bring-down is the **standard** to which the reps must be true at closing:

- **"True and correct in all respects" bring-down.** Any deviation, however small, from the reps as of closing is a failure of the closing condition, letting the buyer walk. This is dangerously buyer-favourable and rarely accepted by sellers except for fundamental reps.
- **"True and correct in all material respects" bring-down.** Deviations must rise to the "material" level to constitute a failure. This is the modal standard for general reps.
- **"True and correct except as would not reasonably be expected to result in a Material Adverse Effect" bring-down.** Only deviations rising to the MAE level constitute a failure. This is the most seller-favourable standard and is the modal standard for compliance / environmental reps.

Fundamental reps typically bring down at the "true and correct in all respects" standard because the fundamental nature of the reps means the buyer needs certainty on them.

The interaction between the rep's original qualifier and the bring-down qualifier can be complex. A rep that is qualified by materiality *and* brought down at the "true and correct in all material respects" standard is effectively "double-material" and can be manipulated in negotiation. Well-drafted agreements handle this with specific language (a "double materiality" concept for the bring-down that reads through the rep's original qualifier to avoid double-counting).

## Disclosure schedules — the substantive risk-allocation artefact

The disclosure schedules are technically part of the definitive agreement but functionally a separate artefact. They are drafted in parallel with the definitive agreement itself; the seller's counsel typically prepares a first draft after the buyer delivers the SPA draft and updates it iteratively as diligence progresses.

### What goes in a schedule

For each rep, the schedule contains:

- **Specific exceptions to the rep** — the enumeration of specific facts the rep does not cover.
- **Cross-references** — where an item disclosed on one schedule is relevant to a different rep, the cross-reference to the other schedule.
- **Supporting documentation** — the underlying contracts, agreements, notices, or other documents that support the disclosed exceptions.

### Schedule discipline

- **Specificity.** A disclosure that is too general does not defeat the rep. "Target has various contracts that may contain change-of-control provisions" is not adequate disclosure of specific change-of-control contracts. The seller's counsel drafts schedules with specific contract citations, specific dates, specific counterparties.
- **Cross-reference discipline.** An item disclosed on one schedule that could also be relevant to another rep should be cross-referenced. In practice, the "General Disclosure" clause (typically in the introductory language of the schedule set) provides that all disclosures on any schedule are deemed disclosures against any other rep to which they are reasonably apparent — the *reasonable apparency* standard. Buyer's counsel may negotiate against the general-disclosure provision or narrow it.
- **Update discipline.** Between signing and closing, if a specific disclosed matter changes materially, the seller may deliver an update to the disclosure schedule. The interaction between schedule updates and the bring-down mechanic is specific and negotiated — well-drafted agreements provide that schedule updates do not cure a breach of the rep for indemnification purposes (i.e., an update does not let the seller escape indemnification for an item that was undisclosed at signing) but do inform the closing-condition analysis.
- **Coordination with diligence.** The schedules should reflect everything the diligence workstreams have surfaced. If diligence has found a customer contract with an aggressive change-of-control provision, the schedule for the change-of-control rep should list that contract; if diligence has found a pending litigation matter, the schedule for the litigation rep should list it.

Drafting schedules well is one of the most time-consuming portions of the seller's counsel's work during the interim period, and is where the risk-allocation between buyer and seller is actually accomplished. Sloppy schedule drafting is one of the leading causes of post-closing indemnification claims.

## Common rep-and-warranty negotiation moves

- **Buyer's SPA draft has an unqualified compliance-with-law rep; seller counters with an MAE-qualified rep.** Standard resolution: materiality-qualified rep on the general compliance rep, with MAE-qualifiers on specific sub-reps.
- **Buyer's SPA draft has a broad "absence of undisclosed liabilities" rep; seller narrows to exceptions on the balance sheet, incurred in ordinary course since the balance sheet, and specifically disclosed.** Standard resolution: seller-favourable narrowing with the specific carve-outs.
- **Buyer's SPA draft has a "10b-5" rep — "no material misstatement or omission in disclosures made to Buyer"; seller pushes to remove or significantly narrow.** 10b-5 reps are aggressive buyer-side language that essentially catches every diligence-material fact. Sellers strongly resist. Standard resolution: 10b-5 reps are removed, or narrowed to specific categories with specific knowledge qualifiers.
- **Buyer's SPA draft has an unqualified capitalisation rep; seller pushes for a materiality qualifier.** Capitalisation is fundamental and materiality-qualifiers are typically not accepted by buyers. Standard resolution: unqualified capitalisation rep with a de-minimis exception for administrative-error items.
- **Buyer's SPA draft has knowledge qualifiers on IP-infringement rep; seller wants broader knowledge scope.** IP-infringement risk is a real seller concern. Standard resolution: knowledge qualifier on "no infringement of third-party IP" rep, with the knowledge definition scoped to specific officers.
- **Buyer's SPA draft has no time-scope qualifier on tax reps; seller pushes for statute-of-limitations time scope.** Tax reps generally survive to statute of limitations; time-scope qualifier is standard.

## Summary

The definitive agreement is the contract that consummates the transaction, structured into a well-established modal architecture: definitions, purchase-and-sale, seller reps, buyer reps, covenants, closing conditions, termination, indemnification, and miscellaneous. The reps-and-warranties layer (Article III) is the core risk-allocation section, organised into seven layers: fundamental, tax, general, compliance-with-law, environmental, IP, privacy, plus an emerging AI-model layer. Each rep is qualified by materiality, knowledge, time-scope, and disclosure-schedule qualifiers that together determine the substantive risk allocation. The bring-down mechanic tests the reps at closing to a negotiated standard. Disclosure schedules are the actual risk-allocation artefact where the specific exceptions to each rep are enumerated. Buyer's counsel drafts the SPA first; seller's counsel red-lines it; the negotiation runs over 30–90 days from LOI signing to signing.

Chapter 5 continues the definitive-agreement layer by covering the covenants (interim-operating and post-closing), closing conditions (including the bring-down mechanic that ties back to Article III's reps), and the MAC / MAE definition negotiation that is often the single most-negotiated portion of the entire agreement.
