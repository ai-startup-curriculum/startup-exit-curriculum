# Auction Management — Choreography, Competitive Tension, Information Parity

## Why this matters

The single largest driver of the headline price a target achieves in a sell-side transaction is the presence, quality, and timing of competing bidders. A target with one interested buyer negotiates from a weak position — the buyer knows there is no alternative and prices accordingly. A target with three credible bidders competing against each other on price, structure, and process posture can extract materially better terms: on the ABA / SRS Acquiom / Cornerstone practitioner data, competitive processes deliver headline prices that meaningfully exceed bilateral-negotiation outcomes for comparable targets.

But an auction is not just "invite everyone and take the highest number." A well-run auction requires a specific choreography: which bidders to invite, in what round, with what information, on what timeline, with what disclosure discipline. Get the choreography wrong and the process leaks (signalling to customers, employees, and competitors that the target is for sale before the target is ready), or the information distribution becomes unequal across bidders (which creates an ex-post fiduciary-duty problem and can result in the "winning" bid being disqualified), or the competitive tension collapses in the final round (which lets the last bidder price aggressively low), or the process drags on so long that the market moves against the target between IOI and LOI.

This chapter installs the auction-management toolkit: the choice among single-track / dual-track / limited-round / broad-auction process shapes; the mechanics of the IOI round and the best-and-final round; information-parity discipline across bidders; and the timing choreography that maintains competitive tension without triggering signalling risk. mod-102 covered the pre-auction machinery (buyer map, banker selection, teaser / CIM / management presentation). This chapter picks up from the moment the teaser goes out to the moment the winning LOI is signed.

## The four modal process shapes

A sell-side process fits one of four modal shapes. The choice is driven by the target's specific facts — buyer-set size, information sensitivity, market-window, seller preferences — and is one of the first decisions the banker and the target's board make together.

### 1. Bilateral negotiation (single-buyer, no formal process)

The target is in negotiation with a single strategic or financial buyer, without any formal marketing to other potential bidders. This is the *default* posture when a buyer approaches the target unsolicited and the target's board decides to engage. It is also common when a specific strategic relationship (a customer, a partner, an investor) evolves into an acquisition conversation.

- **Pros:** Fast; confidential; minimal information leakage; no banker required (though sometimes engaged for defensive analysis and fairness opinion); no competitive tension to manage.
- **Cons:** No competitive tension means no price discovery — the target is at the mercy of the buyer's willingness to pay a fair price. The board's fiduciary duty is more exposed if the target is later challenged for having accepted a below-market price.
- **When appropriate:** targets where the specific buyer has a clearly higher willingness to pay than the market (a strategic buyer with unique synergies, a competitor for whom the target is uniquely valuable), or targets where the seller's board has explicitly decided the single-buyer path is worth the price discount for the process simplicity or confidentiality.

The target's board should still, at minimum, obtain a **market check** — a discreet check with 3–5 other potential bidders to validate that the negotiated price is competitive — and a **fairness opinion** from a qualified financial advisor. mod-109 develops the fiduciary discipline; the market check and fairness opinion are essential defensive artefacts.

### 2. Limited-round auction (3–8 bidders)

The banker selects 3–8 bidders from the buyer map — a mix of strategics and financial sponsors, calibrated to the target's specific fit — and runs a two-round process (IOI round, best-and-final / LOI round) with confidentiality discipline. This is the modal choice for a well-prepared sell-side process where the target has a genuine buyer set of moderate depth.

- **Pros:** Real competitive tension; price discovery; disciplined information flow; the target's counsel can maintain confidentiality across a manageable bidder set; the timeline is predictable.
- **Cons:** More expensive and disruptive than a bilateral negotiation; requires more banker involvement; information leakage risk grows with the bidder count.
- **When appropriate:** targets with a well-mapped buyer set of moderate depth (see mod-102), targets that are process-ready (data room is prepared, management team is available), targets that can absorb the 4–6 month process timeline without material operating disruption.

The limited-round auction is what most competent sell-side bankers default to for venture-backed private-target transactions in the $50M–$1B range.

### 3. Broad auction (10+ bidders)

The banker sends the teaser to a much larger bidder set — sometimes 20–50 potential bidders — and runs multiple rounds (teaser distribution, IOI round, first-round management presentation set, second-round diligence access, best-and-final / LOI round). Broad auctions are more common at the larger end of the transaction-size spectrum, or for targets with genuinely dispersed potential buyer interest.

- **Pros:** Maximum price discovery; strongest competitive tension in the best-and-final round; hardest to leak because the number of bidders makes it difficult to trace any specific leak to a specific bidder.
- **Cons:** Highest process cost; longest timeline; highest information-leakage risk (because more bidders means more parties handling non-public information); risk of "auction fatigue" if the target's management team burns out on process meetings; some strategic buyers will decline to participate in broad auctions because of the perceived risk that the target's information will get to competitors.
- **When appropriate:** larger transactions ($500M+) with genuinely broad buyer interest; targets in sectors with a large potential PE / strategic bidder set; targets where the seller has made a clear decision that the price discovery is worth the process cost.

### 4. Dual-track (IPO + M&A in parallel)

The target simultaneously prepares an IPO filing (see mod-107) and runs an M&A process. The two tracks generate competitive tension against each other — the M&A bidders know they are competing against a live IPO alternative, and the IPO underwriters know they are competing against a live sale alternative. The board makes the final decision at (or near) the IPO pricing / signing timeline.

- **Pros:** Maximum optionality; strongest competitive tension against strategic bidders (who know the target has a live IPO alternative); price discovery across two very different exit markets.
- **Cons:** By far the most expensive and complex process shape; requires two full-scope workstreams; IPO preparation costs are significant even if the IPO does not happen; the S-1 filing (or DRS submission) itself can create information leakage; the market-window for both IPO and M&A must remain open simultaneously.
- **When appropriate:** larger, more-mature targets ($200M+ ARR, plausible IPO candidate) with substantial M&A interest and adequate operating and financial infrastructure to support both tracks.

Dual-track processes are the province of pre-IPO growth-stage companies with strong strategic interest. mod-107 develops the IPO-preparation side; this module develops the M&A side. Dual-track requires close coordination between the M&A banker (managing the auction) and the IPO underwriter (managing the S-1 and roadshow).

## Choosing the process shape

The choice is driven by a small set of factors:

- **Buyer-set depth and quality (from the mod-102 buyer map).** A target with three high-quality strategic buyers and one PE bidder is naturally suited to a limited-round auction. A target with two possible bidders is naturally suited to a bilateral or dual-bilateral negotiation. A target with 20+ credible bidders is a candidate for a broad auction.
- **Information sensitivity.** A target whose confidentiality would be materially damaged by process leakage (a target with a customer concentration risk, a target with a controversial technology, a target with a competitor in the bidder set) should lean toward a narrower process.
- **Market-window signals.** In a compressed multiple environment, a broader process is often necessary to surface adequate buyer demand. In a strong multiple environment, a narrower process can extract high prices with less process cost.
- **Target readiness.** A target that is not yet operationally / financially / legally ready for a broad process should either delay the process or lean toward a narrower shape.
- **Seller preferences.** Some sellers prefer a bilateral negotiation for the process control and confidentiality; some prefer a broad auction for the price discovery. The board should decide explicitly.
- **Fiduciary defensibility.** For a target where the board's fiduciary posture is a substantive concern (a board with recent conflicts, a controlled-company transaction, a related-party bidder), a broader process is more defensible under the fiduciary framework. mod-109 develops.

## The IOI round mechanics

The IOI round is the first written expression of interest from the bidder set. It follows CIM distribution (typically 2–4 weeks after CIM goes out, giving bidders time to complete an initial diligence pass and to prepare a written IOI).

### The process paper

The banker prepares a **process paper** that goes to invited bidders with the CIM. The process paper specifies:

- The IOI due date and format (typically a 2–5 page written letter answering specific questions).
- The specific questions the IOI must answer (indicative price range, form of consideration, sources of financing, key conditions, bidder background, requested exclusivity).
- The confidentiality obligations (typically already covered by the executed NDA).
- The process cadence going forward (approximate dates for management presentation, best-and-final round, target signing date).
- The banker's contact information and any specific submission mechanics.

The process paper is *not* a legal document — it is a process-management artefact. But bidders read it closely to calibrate their bid strategy, and a process paper that signals a specific timeline or a specific target price range shapes the responses.

### The IOI evaluation

Once IOIs are received, the banker prepares an IOI evaluation memo for the target's board that scores each IOI on:

- **Indicative price** — the headline range, benchmarked against public comparables and precedent transactions.
- **Form of consideration** — cash-certainty vs. stock-with-upside; the target's board reads this against its stockholder base.
- **Financing certainty** — cash on balance sheet or executed commitments (strong) vs. subject-to-financing (weak).
- **Bidder profile** — strategic (with specified strategic fit) vs. financial (with specified fund profile and typical hold period).
- **Anticipated regulatory risk** — HSR-only vs. CFIUS-triggering vs. foreign-antitrust-triggering vs. materially horizontal (with likely FTC / DOJ scrutiny). Regulatory complexity is a significant closing-certainty variable that the board should weight.
- **Anticipated conditions** — buyer's stated conditions (financing, board approval, specific diligence workstreams). Materially conditional IOIs are worth less than clean IOIs.
- **Anticipated timing** — buyer's stated timeline for LOI signing and closing. Faster is often better; too fast can signal insufficient diligence rigour.

The board selects a shortlist for the next round — typically 2–4 bidders for a limited-round process, sometimes 5–6 for a broad process, sometimes 1 (moving directly to a best-and-final in a bilateral setup). Bidders not selected receive a courteous decline; the banker documents the rationale for the board's selection.

### The IOI-to-LOI compression

A recurring practitioner observation: bidders often *lower* the indicative price between IOI and LOI. This is not (usually) bad-faith negotiation; it reflects the natural information flow. At IOI, the bidder has seen the CIM but has not conducted diligence. At LOI, the bidder has completed a first-round diligence pass and has surfaced specific issues (customer concentration, technology risk, IP exposure, cap-table complexity) that reduce the bidder's willingness to pay. Bankers refer to this as *IOI-to-LOI compression* and typically anticipate a 5–15% headline discount from IOI midpoint to LOI signed price, depending on the diligence-surface complexity.

The banker's job is to (a) minimise the compression by ensuring the bidder set has adequate CIM-stage information (so the IOI is closer to a real number), and (b) discount the IOI headline appropriately when advising the board on which bidders to shortlist.

## The best-and-final round mechanics

The best-and-final round is where the shortlisted bidders submit their LOIs. The mechanics are more choreographed than the IOI round because the winning bid becomes the LOI and initiates exclusivity.

### The management presentation phase

Between IOI and best-and-final, the shortlisted bidders participate in a **management presentation** with the target's CEO, CFO, and other key operating leaders. The management presentation:

- Deepens the bidder's substantive understanding of the target (product, technology, GTM, financial trajectory, competitive position).
- Gives the bidder access to the target's management team so the bidder can assess the team's quality and cultural fit.
- Gives the target's management team access to the bidder so the target can assess the bidder's operating capability and intent.
- Is not itself a negotiating conversation — price is not discussed in the management presentation; the management team is instructed to redirect any price question to the banker.

The management presentation is typically 2–4 hours per bidder and is scheduled tightly to avoid drift. All shortlisted bidders receive the same management presentation content; information parity is enforced.

### The Q&A round

After management presentation, the banker manages a **Q&A round** where bidders submit written questions and the target answers them through the data room. This is the standard mechanism for handling substantive follow-up diligence. The Q&A round is time-limited (typically 1–2 weeks) and questions and answers are shared with all bidders — information parity again. See mod-105 for the data-room and diligence-workstream mechanics.

### The best-and-final process paper

The banker issues a second process paper to the shortlisted bidders that names:

- The best-and-final due date and format (typically an executed LOI, with markup of the seller's LOI template).
- Any specific structural preferences the seller is signalling (e.g., "the seller prefers reverse-triangular merger structure; bidders proposing asset-deal structures should identify the specific undisclosed-liability rationale").
- The seller's LOI template (often attached as an exhibit; bidders mark up the template rather than drafting from scratch).
- Any specific process elements the seller expects (e.g., "bidders should submit executed R&W insurance letter of intent from a qualified underwriter alongside the LOI").
- The specific submission mechanics.

Providing an **LOI template** at the best-and-final stage is a widely-used seller-side technique. It anchors the negotiation on seller-friendly language; bidders mark up the template to indicate their negotiating positions, which lets the seller immediately compare bidders on non-price terms (exclusivity length, standstill scope, break-up-fee mechanics, fiduciary-out language) side-by-side. This makes the seller's evaluation much easier and preserves seller-side drafting leverage.

### The best-and-final evaluation

The banker prepares a best-and-final evaluation memo scoring each LOI on:

- **Headline price and adjustment mechanics** — the actual dollar number, plus the working-capital / closing-cash / closing-debt / transaction-expenses adjustment mechanics that determine the effective net proceeds.
- **Consideration mix** — cash vs. stock vs. structured, and the specific terms of any CVRs, seller notes, or rollover.
- **Structure** — form (asset vs. stock vs. reverse-triangular merger), any tax elections.
- **Financing certainty** — committed vs. subject to financing.
- **Exclusivity and process obligations** — length, fiduciary-out language, matching-right mechanics.
- **Retention pool** — size, funding source, allocation mechanics.
- **Employee arrangements** — treatment of vested / unvested equity, non-competes, key-executive employment agreements.
- **Regulatory-approval strategy** — anticipated filings, closing-condition posture.
- **Non-price terms in the marked-up LOI** — standstill, break-up-fee, MAC / MAE (where included in the LOI at the buyer's insistence), indemnification (where outlined in the LOI).

The board selects the winning LOI, negotiates a small number of final terms with the winning bidder, and signs. Losing bidders are notified with a courteous decline; the banker documents the rationale.

## Information-parity discipline

The single most important discipline in a well-run auction is **information parity**: every shortlisted bidder receives the same information, at the same time, in the same form, subject to the same conditions.

### Why information parity matters

- **Fiduciary defensibility.** A board that runs a process in which one bidder receives materially better information than others exposes itself to a challenge that the "process" was actually a sham designed to steer the deal to the favoured bidder. Delaware courts have addressed this pattern in multiple opinions — the *Corwin* and *Trulia* lines depend on the process being genuine. mod-109 develops.
- **Competitive tension.** If bidders discover that one bidder has better information, they will discount their bids to protect against being at an information disadvantage — the whole process compresses toward the informed bidder's number. Information parity keeps all bidders competing on the same footing.
- **Legal exposure on securities law.** For a public-target process, information parity is enforced by Regulation FD and by securities-law disclosure rules. For a private-target process, information parity is enforced primarily by fiduciary and contract law rather than by securities law, but the practitioner discipline is the same.

### How information parity is enforced

- **Single data room accessible to all bidders on identical terms.** The virtual data room (Intralinks / Datasite / Firmex / Ansarada / DealRoom) grants each shortlisted bidder identical access to identical documents; any incremental documents added during the process are added for all bidders simultaneously.
- **Q&A questions and answers shared across bidders.** When one bidder asks a question, the question and the target's answer are posted to the shared Q&A section of the data room so all bidders see them. Bidders sometimes negotiate for the ability to ask "private" questions where the identity of the asking bidder is protected but the Q&A itself is still shared; this is a defensible compromise. Truly private questions (question and answer only shared with the asking bidder) are usually not appropriate.
- **Management-presentation content parity.** All shortlisted bidders receive the same management-presentation deck and the same substantive management-team access. Individual bidders may ask different questions in their presentations; the target's management team may answer differently based on the specific questions; but the *content available* is identical.
- **Process-timeline parity.** All shortlisted bidders receive the same process-paper timelines and the same substantive process treatment. A bidder that is given a longer diligence extension than others should be handled carefully — if the extension is offered to all, it maintains parity; if it is offered only to one, it creates a defensibility problem.

### The clean-team exception

One legitimate exception to strict information parity: **clean teams**. For a bidder that is a direct competitor of the target — where full data-room access would expose competitively-sensitive information (customer lists, pricing, product roadmaps) to a competitor — the target may set up a "clean team" of the bidder's outside counsel and advisors who receive the sensitive information under strict confidentiality and are prohibited from sharing it with the bidder's operating personnel. The clean-team mechanism balances information-parity discipline against legitimate competitive-sensitivity concerns.

See mod-105 for the data-room and clean-team mechanics; this chapter notes only that clean teams are a defensible way to handle competitor bidders without violating information-parity discipline.

## Timing choreography

The overall process timeline is one of the banker's most important management decisions. Too fast and bidders cannot complete adequate diligence, which compresses IOI-to-LOI and reduces bid quality. Too slow and the market may move against the target, or bidders lose interest, or the target's operating story degrades between the CIM's baseline and the LOI signing.

### The standard limited-round auction timeline

The modal timeline for a limited-round auction of a venture-backed private target:

- **Weeks 0–4.** Banker engagement, buyer-map finalisation, CIM authoring, teaser drafting. See mod-102.
- **Week 4.** Teaser distribution to invited bidder set.
- **Weeks 4–6.** NDA negotiations with responding bidders. CIM distribution to bidders who sign the NDA.
- **Weeks 6–10.** Bidders review CIM and prepare IOI. IOI due at end of week 10 (or 8, or 12, depending on bidder-set size and CIM complexity).
- **Weeks 10–11.** IOI evaluation. Shortlist selected.
- **Weeks 12–14.** Management presentations with shortlisted bidders. Q&A round.
- **Weeks 14–16.** Bidders complete first-round diligence. Best-and-final LOI due at end of week 16.
- **Weeks 16–17.** Best-and-final evaluation. Winning bidder selected. LOI signed.
- **Weeks 17–24.** Exclusivity period. Definitive-agreement drafting, buyer-side diligence, R&W insurance underwriting. Signing at end of week 24.
- **Weeks 24–32.** Signing-to-closing interim period. Regulatory-filing clearances, third-party consents, closing conditions satisfied.
- **Week 32.** Closing.

Total: ~8 months from banker engagement to closing. This is the modal timeline; specific transactions run faster (bilateral processes, targets that are highly prepared) or slower (broad auctions, dual-track processes, transactions with material regulatory or diligence complexity).

### Compressed timelines

Some transactions run compressed timelines — 2–3 months from teaser to closing. This is common in:

- **Preemptive strategic transactions** where a specific buyer pre-empts a broader process by putting a compelling offer on the table quickly and demanding a fast close.
- **Distressed sales** where the target's liquidity position forces a fast close.
- **Small transactions** where the diligence workstream is limited in scope.

Compressed timelines have specific risk profiles — reduced diligence rigour, less bidder competition, more concessions on non-price terms. The board's decision to accept a compressed timeline should be explicit and grounded in the specific reasons.

### Extended timelines

Some transactions require extended timelines — 12+ months from teaser to closing. This is common in:

- **Dual-track processes** where the IPO-preparation timeline is the binding constraint.
- **Transactions with material regulatory complexity** — CFIUS review, foreign-antitrust review, sector-specific regulatory approvals.
- **Broad auctions with many bidders** where the process depth adds calendar weeks at each round.

Extended timelines expose the process to market-window compression and operating-story drift. The banker manages this through periodic re-teasing (updating the CIM with fresh operating data, hosting periodic bidder-update calls) and through active operating-story management (chapter 5 of mod-101).

## Managing signalling risk

An auction inherently signals to the market that the target is for sale. Some signalling is unavoidable — bidders talk, and information leaks through their teams and their advisors. But well-run auctions minimise signalling:

- **NDA discipline.** All bidders sign an NDA before receiving the CIM. The NDA prohibits disclosure of the target's identity, the target's non-public information, and the fact of the process itself.
- **Sanitised teaser.** The teaser is drafted so that the target's identity is not obvious from the description. This lets the banker widely distribute the teaser without immediately signalling which company is for sale.
- **CIM under NDA.** The CIM — which discloses the target's identity and detailed operating information — is distributed only to bidders who have signed the NDA. The CIM itself is watermarked with the bidder's identity for traceability if it leaks.
- **Management presentations at neutral locations.** Management presentations are typically hosted at the banker's offices or at a neutral hotel, not at the target's headquarters, to reduce the risk that competitors or customers observe the bidder team visiting the target.
- **Data-room activity logs.** The virtual data room logs every access; the banker reviews the logs periodically to identify unusual patterns (a bidder that has ceased reviewing documents; a bidder whose access includes personnel not on the deal team).
- **Employee-communications discipline.** The target's CEO and CFO know about the process; other operating personnel are informed on a need-to-know basis; the broader company is not informed until the target's board has decided that disclosure is appropriate. See mod-110 for the transaction-communications discipline.
- **Customer / partner communications discipline.** Customers and partners are typically not informed of the process until signing (or, for the largest customers, sometimes just before signing under specific NDA protections). The target's account managers should not be told the process is happening until it is time to inform them.

### Handling a leak

If the process leaks — a customer, a partner, an employee, or a journalist learns that the target is for sale — the target's board and banker have to decide how to respond:

- **Confirm nothing publicly.** The target's public posture is typically "we do not comment on rumours or speculation." The banker and target's counsel prepare a specific messaging note for any employee, customer, or partner who asks.
- **Assess the specific leak.** Which bidder's team appears to be the source? Is the leak likely to spread? Is it likely to reach competitors or customers in ways that damage the target?
- **Decide whether to accelerate the process.** If the leak is likely to spread and damage the target, the banker may accelerate the process (compress the IOI-to-LOI timeline, add bidders to the shortlist to increase competition, or move directly to a best-and-final round).
- **Decide whether to fire the leaking bidder.** If a specific bidder can be identified as the source and the leak violates the NDA, the target may remove the bidder from the process. This has downside risk — removing a serious bidder reduces competitive tension — and is done selectively.

Leaks are the single largest process risk in a limited-round or broad auction. The banker's job is to minimise the probability and to have a response plan ready if they occur.

## Common auction-management mistakes

- **Choosing the wrong process shape.** A bilateral negotiation for a target with a rich buyer set forgoes price discovery; a broad auction for a target with a narrow buyer set generates process cost and leakage risk without much competitive benefit.
- **Inadequate IOI-round preparation.** A CIM that does not adequately describe the target's financial trajectory, customer economics, and competitive position generates IOIs that compress heavily by LOI. Investing in CIM quality pays off in IOI quality.
- **Losing information-parity discipline.** Once one bidder has better information than others, the process compresses toward that bidder's number and the fiduciary defensibility is impaired.
- **Timeline drift.** A process that runs 2 weeks past the target timeline is common; a process that runs 6+ weeks past is problematic. Bidder attention wanes, the market may move, the operating story may drift.
- **Under-preparing the best-and-final process paper.** A best-and-final round that is not structured (no LOI template, no specific non-price-term guidance) produces LOIs that are difficult to compare and that leave the seller in a weak negotiating position on non-price terms.
- **Failing to negotiate non-price terms.** The best-and-final round often focuses on price, and the target's counsel signs an LOI with buyer-favourable exclusivity, standstill, and break-up-fee terms because they were not part of the price competition. Chapter 2's discipline applies at LOI signing, not later.
- **Signalling risk under-managed.** A leak that reaches a major customer during the process can cost the target 10–20% of the transaction value if the customer responds by putting the account out to bid or by renegotiating pricing.

## Summary

A well-run auction produces a materially higher price than a bilateral negotiation for a target with adequate buyer-set depth. The four modal process shapes — bilateral negotiation, limited-round auction, broad auction, dual-track — are chosen against the target's specific buyer-set depth, information sensitivity, market-window, readiness, and seller preferences. The IOI round produces a set of nonbinding indications that let the target shortlist for a best-and-final round; the best-and-final round produces LOIs that the target evaluates on price, adjustment mechanics, consideration, structure, financing certainty, and non-price terms. Information parity across bidders is essential for both competitive tension and fiduciary defensibility. Timing choreography — the ~8-month modal timeline for a limited-round auction — is calibrated to allow adequate bidder diligence without letting the market move against the target. Signalling risk is minimised through NDA discipline, sanitised teasers, watermarked CIMs, and controlled employee / customer / partner communications. The output of a well-run auction is a signed LOI from a qualified bidder at a defensible price with defensible non-price terms.
