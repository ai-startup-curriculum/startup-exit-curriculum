# Deal-Scale Negotiation Canon — Freund, Fisher / Ury, Voss

## Why this matters

Chapters 1–7 installed the *content* of an M&A negotiation — what an LOI actually locks in, how exclusivity and standstills reshape leverage, how an auction generates competitive tension, how the reps-and-warranties stack allocates risk, how the MAC / MAE definition is fought over, how the indemnification package is designed, how R&W insurance shifts the retention curve. That content is necessary but not sufficient. Two teams sitting across a conference table (or a video call) with the same LOI in front of them can — and regularly do — reach materially different outcomes on the same document, because deal-scale negotiation is a repeated, multi-issue, multi-party exercise in which the *how* of the conversation drives the *what* of the terms as much as the underlying economics do.

Practitioner M&A negotiation draws on three distinct bodies of writing that together form the working canon. Each was written for a different audience, uses a different vocabulary, and points at a different sub-skill. In combination they cover the ground:

- **James C. Freund — *Anatomy of a Merger* (1975)** — the practitioner's playbook for the specific choreography of a sell-side or buy-side M&A negotiation, written by a Skadden partner who ran deals in the 1960s–1970s formative era of modern M&A practice. The vocabulary is dated and the specific market conventions have moved on; the substantive frame — sequencing, "give-to-get" pattern, the discipline of the closing negotiation — remains the working reference.
- **Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton — *Getting to Yes* (Harvard Negotiation Project, 1981 and subsequent editions)** — the principled-negotiation frame that reshaped how sophisticated commercial negotiators talk about their craft: interests behind positions, objective criteria, invention before decision, BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) discipline. Also foundational: Ury's *Getting Past No* (1991) on responding to hard-bargaining tactics, and Fisher and Shapiro's *Beyond Reason* (2005) on the emotional dimension.
- **Chris Voss — *Never Split the Difference* (2016)** — the hostage-negotiator-turned-business-teacher frame that emphasises calibrated questions, mirroring, labelling, tactical empathy, and the deliberate use of "no" as a tool. Voss and the Harvard frame appear to disagree in places and *are* different — but in deal-scale M&A the frames are complementary rather than contradictory.

This chapter maps each frame onto the actual M&A conversations from chapters 1–7 — the LOI negotiation, the definitive-agreement red-line, the MAC / MAE debate, the indemnification-package negotiation, the closing-condition brinkmanship. The point is not to convert you into a Freund or a Fisher or a Voss disciple. The point is to give you three complementary lenses so that when you face a specific M&A conversation, you can consciously pick the frame that fits the moment and know what tools are on the shelf.

## Freund — *Anatomy of a Merger* — the practitioner sequencing

Freund's contribution is a specific *sequencing* discipline for M&A negotiations. The frame is that deals are won or lost not on any single point but on the choreography — which issue is raised in what order, which concessions are made in which round, when the closing conversation is initiated, how the parties handle the moment when a deal-threatening issue surfaces mid-diligence. Several of Freund's specific frames remain in active practitioner use.

### The sell-side sequencing

Freund's sell-side pattern:

1. **Establish the price and structure early.** The price and structure are the anchors for everything that follows — chapter 1's LOI headline number, chapter 4's definitive-agreement Article II. Freund's discipline: extract the highest defensible headline number *before* diligence begins, because it becomes the ceiling from which negotiation moves. Chapters 1 and 3 develop the auction choreography that produces that number.
2. **Do not concede on price without extracting a corresponding concession.** Every "give" earns a "get." If the buyer proposes a $10M price reduction based on a diligence finding, the seller's response is not "we accept $10M" but "we would accept $5M and a corresponding reduction in the escrow and a shorter general-rep survival period." This is the "give-to-get" pattern that Freund names as the sell-side discipline.
3. **Sequence the fight zones deliberately.** Do not open every fight simultaneously. Sequence the negotiation so that easy issues are resolved first (building momentum and mutual investment), then the moderate issues, then the hardest issues (MAC / MAE, indemnification cap, sandbagging) last — when both sides have too much invested in the deal to walk away over any single point.
4. **Own the closing conversation.** Freund emphasises that the *closing conversation* — the final round of red-line where the last handful of open issues are resolved — is where the largest value swings happen. Prepare specifically for it. Know which of the remaining open issues you can concede on, which you can trade, and which you must hold. Do not let the closing conversation become an accidental one-sided cave-in.

### The buy-side sequencing

Freund's buy-side pattern is the mirror image:

1. **Anchor low on the LOI, subject to diligence.** The LOI headline is the ceiling; the buyer's discipline is to negotiate a headline that leaves room for a diligence-based downward adjustment.
2. **Use diligence findings to justify price re-trades.** Every material diligence finding is an opportunity to reopen price. The discipline: pair each finding with a specific dollar impact and a specific proposed price adjustment. Vague "we're concerned about X" language does not move price; "we identified an $8M receivable-collectability issue that materially reduces run-rate revenue by 3%" does.
3. **Load the definitive agreement in your favour on the first draft.** Buyer's counsel drafts first (chapter 1); the first draft is deliberately loaded with buyer-favourable positions on rep survival, cap, basket, sandbagging, MAC / MAE. The seller's counsel red-lines back to a moderated position. The negotiation converges from the first-draft anchor, so the anchor position determines a substantial portion of the outcome.
4. **Preserve the ability to walk.** The buyer's leverage in the definitive-agreement negotiation depends on the credibility of the walk-away threat. Financing certainty, board approval status, and (in a competitive market) alternative deals under evaluation all shape credibility.

### Freund's specific frames still in active use

Practitioner shorthand from Freund that remains in active vocabulary:

- **"Give to get."** Every concession earns a specific reciprocal concession.
- **"Reserve your ammunition."** Do not fire your strongest arguments early. The strongest arguments are the ones that resolve the hardest issues in the closing conversation; wasting them on easy issues in early rounds is a common first-time-negotiator error.
- **"The bracket close."** When a range of outcomes is possible on a specific issue, propose a bracket (an upper and lower bound) rather than a specific number; let the other side pick within the bracket, which produces psychological ownership of the outcome and often lands the outcome closer to your target.
- **"The dying deal."** Freund's diagnostic for the negotiation posture where one side signals a willingness to walk to extract a final concession. Handled well, it works; handled poorly, it actually kills the deal. Chapters 6 and 5 both have dying-deal moments — the indemnification-cap fight and the MAC / MAE fight — where this posture is a specific practitioner move.
- **"The closing whip."** Freund's frame for the final round where the parties commit to close, exchange final versions of the definitive agreement and disclosure schedules, and hand off to the signing choreography. Not a place for opening new issues; a place for resolving the last handful.

## Fisher / Ury / Patton — the principled-negotiation frame

The Harvard Negotiation Project frame, published as *Getting to Yes* and extended in *Getting Past No* and *Beyond Reason*, offers a set of general principles that apply across negotiation contexts. Four principles are load-bearing at deal-scale.

### Principle 1 — Separate the people from the problem

Distinguish the substantive issue from the relationship between the negotiators. Buyer's counsel red-lining a seller-friendly sandbagging provision is not personally attacking seller's counsel; they are advocating for their client's position. Reciprocally, seller's counsel pushing back is not personally attacking; they are advocating for theirs.

At deal-scale, the "people" side matters more than in a one-shot negotiation because M&A deals are executed over 60–180 days by teams that must continue functioning together throughout. Founders and buyer-side executives who will be working together post-close (in an earn-out, in a rollover-equity structure, in a management continuity role) have an especially strong interest in preserving the relationship. The discipline: attack the problem hard, protect the relationship carefully.

Practical application in the definitive-agreement red-line: use written comments in the red-line for the substantive fight, use direct conversations (video calls, in-person meetings) for the relational elements. The written record captures the substantive positions; the direct conversation calibrates whether the parties are still operating in good faith and preserves the ability to close.

### Principle 2 — Focus on interests, not positions

Positions are stated demands ("we need a 24-month general-rep survival"). Interests are the underlying motivations ("we need protection against post-close discovery of undisclosed liabilities"). Positions are often incompatible; interests often overlap.

Application in the MAC / MAE negotiation (chapter 5): the seller's *position* is a narrow MAC / MAE definition with extensive carve-outs. The seller's *interest* is preventing the buyer from walking away from the deal for reasons outside the target's control. The buyer's *position* is a broad MAC / MAE definition with narrow carve-outs. The buyer's *interest* is preventing the buyer from being forced to close if the target's business materially deteriorates before closing. These interests can be reconciled — the *Akorn v. Fresenius* frame focuses the definition on target-specific durationally-significant adverse events, which addresses the buyer's interest while limiting the seller's exposure to industry-wide or macroeconomic conditions. A position-based negotiation may not find this middle ground; an interests-based negotiation finds it more readily.

Application in the indemnification-cap fight (chapter 6): the buyer's position is a high cap; the buyer's interest is post-close protection. If R&W insurance can provide the post-close protection through insurance rather than seller indemnification, the buyer's interest is served at a lower seller-side cap — and the seller's indemnification exposure is reduced. Chapter 7's R&W insurance mechanic is fundamentally an interests-based solution to a position-based fight.

### Principle 3 — Invent options for mutual gain

Before committing to a specific outcome, generate multiple candidate solutions. The classic Harvard exercise: separate invention from decision. Brainstorm five ways the parties could resolve an issue; then evaluate each.

Application in the earn-out negotiation (mod-103): the buyer wants a large earn-out (aligning seller with post-close performance); the seller wants a small earn-out (converting the earn-out to fixed consideration). Invention: (a) a smaller earn-out with more generous achievement metrics, (b) a larger earn-out with acceleration on specific catalysts, (c) a smaller earn-out plus a rollover-equity component, (d) a fixed-consideration structure with a management incentive plan post-close. Each option addresses different interests; the best fit depends on the specific transaction facts.

### Principle 4 — Insist on objective criteria and know your BATNA

**Objective criteria.** When positions diverge, appeal to objective criteria — ABA Deal Points distributions, SRS Acquiom benchmarks, comparable transactions, published multiples, R&W insurance market reports. "Our general-rep survival is 18 months, which is the median in the current ABA Deal Points Study" is a fact-based argument that is harder to reject than "we want 18 months." Chapters 2, 4, 5, 6, and 7 name the specific practitioner-benchmark sources; the discipline is to use them in the actual negotiation.

**BATNA.** Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement — what happens if this deal does not close. A seller's BATNA in a competitive auction is the second-place bidder (still viable, still credible); a seller's BATNA in a bilateral negotiation may be continued operation, another fundraise, or a wind-down. A buyer's BATNA is whatever alternative uses of the capital exist — an alternative target, a share buyback, an internal build. Every party's negotiating leverage is a function of BATNA quality. The discipline:

- Know your own BATNA specifically and honestly. Not "we could always walk away" but "if this deal doesn't close, we can raise a Series D at $X valuation from Investor Y" — or "we cannot raise, and we will need to cut headcount by 30% by Q2."
- Estimate the other side's BATNA. What is the buyer's alternative use of the capital? What is the seller's alternative to this specific buyer? The parties who understand each other's BATNAs negotiate more efficiently.
- Improve your BATNA during the negotiation. Chapter 3's auction choreography is BATNA cultivation — a live second-place bidder is a functioning BATNA. Even after LOI signing, disciplined bankers keep second-place bidders "warm" so that the BATNA is real if the exclusive-negotiation deal falls apart.

### Ury's *Getting Past No* — five steps against hard bargaining

Ury's follow-up work addresses the specific problem of negotiating against a counterparty that is using positional or hard-bargaining tactics. Five steps:

1. **Go to the balcony** — do not react to provocations; step back mentally, evaluate the tactic, and respond deliberately.
2. **Step to their side** — acknowledge the other side's position (which is different from agreeing with it) to reduce defensiveness.
3. **Reframe** — restate the issue in a way that opens problem-solving space. "You're asking for X. Let me make sure I understand what you're trying to accomplish."
4. **Build them a golden bridge** — make it easy for the other side to say yes by offering a face-saving path.
5. **Use power to educate, not to attack** — when the other side is not moving, demonstrate the cost of not agreeing (typically by clarifying your BATNA) rather than by attacking their position.

Application when a buyer's counsel proposes an aggressive first-draft sandbagging provision: instead of red-lining it to the opposite, use step 3 (reframe): "What are you trying to protect against with this sandbagging provision?" Often the buyer's counsel is proceduring against a specific concern (a diligence-surfaced issue, a specific fact pattern) that can be addressed with a specific indemnity rather than with the general sandbagging language. Reframing surfaces the underlying interest.

### Fisher and Shapiro — *Beyond Reason* — the emotional dimension

Fisher and Shapiro added the emotional layer to the Harvard frame: five "core concerns" that shape emotional engagement in negotiation — appreciation, affiliation, autonomy, status, role. Skilled deal-scale negotiators attend to these deliberately. A founder who feels dismissed (violated appreciation) or excluded from the substantive negotiation (violated autonomy or status) becomes an obstacle to closing; a founder who feels respected and included is a facilitator.

## Voss — *Never Split the Difference* — calibrated questions, mirroring, tactical empathy

Voss's frame — developed as an FBI hostage negotiator and adapted for business negotiations — emphasises a specific set of conversational techniques that reshape how the other side is thinking about the negotiation. Several are directly applicable at deal-scale.

### Calibrated questions

A calibrated question is a "what" or "how" question that requires the other side to think through the problem and produces information without triggering defensiveness. Not "why are you insisting on a 24-month survival?" (defensive) but "how am I supposed to explain a 24-month survival to my board given the ABA median of 18 months?" (invites the other side to solve the problem for you).

Application in the MAC / MAE negotiation:

- "How is your team thinking about the specific carve-outs we've proposed?" (calibrated; produces information)
- "What would need to be true about the definition for you to accept the current draft?" (calibrated; surfaces underlying concerns)
- "How can we structure the definition so that it addresses your concern about post-signing deterioration without exposing us to industry-wide swings?" (calibrated; frames the problem cooperatively)

### Mirroring and labelling

**Mirroring** — repeating the last few words the other side said, with a slight upward inflection. Prompts the other side to elaborate without you having to ask an explicit question. Especially useful when the other side is stating a position without explaining the underlying rationale.

**Labelling** — naming the emotion or motivation you observe in the other side. "It sounds like you're concerned about the retention structure." Or, more strongly: "It seems like there's frustration about how the diligence process has gone." Labelling defuses emotions by surfacing them; unspoken emotions typically grow, spoken emotions typically shrink.

Application when a founder-CEO is emotional about a specific term (a non-compete that they view as personally punitive, a retention structure that they view as insulting): labelling the underlying feeling — "it sounds like this term is landing as a signal that we don't trust you" — often defuses the emotional charge and lets the substantive negotiation continue.

### Tactical empathy

Voss's frame for the deliberate practice of understanding the other side's perspective — not just their position but the specific concerns, pressures, and interests that produced the position. Tactical empathy is not sympathy or agreement; it is the negotiator's discipline to understand the other side sufficiently to design an approach that works.

Application in the indemnification-cap negotiation: the buyer's insistence on a 20% cap may reflect (a) buyer counsel's default first-draft position, (b) buyer's specific diligence-surfaced concerns about a particular exposure category, (c) buyer's institutional risk-committee posture, or (d) buyer's negotiating tactic to anchor high before conceding. Each interpretation calls for a different response. Tactical empathy is the discipline of figuring out which one is in play before responding.

### "No" as a beginning, not an end

Voss's frame: "no" is often the beginning of a real negotiation, not the end of it. A first "no" typically means "I don't understand," "I'm not ready," or "I need more information." The negotiator's discipline is not to press through the "no" but to explore what it means. "What would need to change for that to be a yes?" is a calibrated response to a "no" that keeps the conversation open.

Application when a buyer rejects a specific seller-friendly provision: rather than immediately conceding, explore the "no." Sometimes it turns out to be a soft "no" that yields to a small modification; sometimes it is a hard "no" backed by an institutional constraint; the exploration surfaces which one is in play.

### The "that's right" moment

Voss's marker for the moment when the other side feels genuinely understood — "that's right" (as opposed to "you're right," which is a concessive rather than a comprehension marker). The "that's right" moment is when the substantive negotiation can move forward productively. Getting there requires listening, mirroring, labelling, and calibrating questions until the other side feels seen.

## Mapping the frames onto specific M&A conversations

The three frames are complementary. In practice, a well-negotiated M&A conversation draws on all three at different moments.

### The LOI negotiation (chapters 1–2)

- **Freund frame:** Sequence the LOI drafting deliberately — price and structure first (anchoring), then exclusivity mechanics, then process obligations. Reserve the fiduciary-out carve-out as a hold-firm position; concede on the standstill duration and the expense-reimbursement cap as trade material.
- **Harvard frame:** The buyer's *position* is a 60-day exclusivity with no fiduciary-out; the buyer's *interest* is protection of the diligence investment. Reframe: "we can offer 45 days plus a matching right for any superior proposal — that gives you diligence-investment protection while preserving our fiduciary posture." Interest-based reframing bridges the position gap.
- **Voss frame:** Calibrated question: "how are you thinking about the exclusivity length given our commitment to complete definitive-agreement drafting within 30 days?" — surfaces the buyer's actual concerns and often reveals that the buyer is over-indexed on a specific perceived risk that can be addressed differently.

### The definitive-agreement red-line (chapter 4)

- **Freund frame:** Do not open every rep fight simultaneously. Sequence the red-line so that easy reps (corporate existence, capitalisation) are resolved quickly, moderate reps (financial statements, contracts) are negotiated in the first substantive round, and hard reps (IP, privacy, AI-model, compliance-with-law) are resolved in the closing conversation. Reserve the sandbagging fight for the closing conversation.
- **Harvard frame:** Use ABA Deal Points and SRS Acquiom benchmarks as objective criteria. The 18-month general-rep survival is not a position; it is a data-supported market convention. Framing the negotiation around the objective criteria depersonalises the fight and often accelerates convergence.
- **Voss frame:** Labelling: "it sounds like your team is particularly concerned about the IP reps because of the AI-model exposure in our diligence." Naming the underlying concern often opens the space for a specific-indemnity solution rather than a broader rep-scope fight.

### The MAC / MAE debate (chapter 5)

- **Freund frame:** This is a closing-conversation fight — do not open it in round 1 of the red-line. The MAC / MAE negotiation is where "give-to-get" is most visible; the seller trades broader carve-outs against a shorter cure period or against specific bright-line thresholds.
- **Harvard frame:** The *Akorn v. Fresenius* framework is objective criteria. Both sides' positions align around durationally-significant target-specific adverse events; the fight is about the specific bright lines. Anchor on the case-law framework.
- **Voss frame:** Calibrated question: "what would need to be in the MAC / MAE definition for you to be comfortable that you're not exposed to a target-specific catastrophic event between signing and closing?" — invites the buyer to name the specific fact patterns they are worried about, which typically maps to a small number of specific carve-outs rather than a broad definition.

### The indemnification-package negotiation (chapter 6)

- **Freund frame:** Sequence — de-minimis and basket first (small dollar impact, easier to resolve); cap and survival next (moderate); sandbagging and sole-and-exclusive-remedy carve-outs last (hardest). Reserve the R&W insurance mechanic as a structural pivot that can reshape the entire package.
- **Harvard frame:** Interests — buyer wants post-close protection; seller wants defined exposure. R&W insurance serves both interests better than a traditional package with a large cap and small escrow (which under-collateralises the buyer while over-exposing the seller). Chapter 7's R&W insurance mechanic is fundamentally an interest-based solution to a position-based fight.
- **Voss frame:** Tactical empathy — the buyer's specific-indemnity requests often reflect specific diligence findings that surfaced in the workstreams. Understanding which finding motivates which request lets the seller design a targeted specific indemnity (chapter 6) rather than accepting a broader cap increase.

### The closing-condition brinkmanship (chapter 5)

- **Freund frame:** The closing conversation is where the closing-condition fights (bring-down, MAC / MAE, third-party consents) are resolved. Own it. Prepare specifically. Know your walk-away, know the other side's walk-away, and know which of the remaining conditions is the actual point of contention.
- **Harvard frame:** BATNA discipline is critical here. If the buyer is threatening to walk on a specific closing condition, the seller's response depends on the seller's BATNA — if the second-place bidder is still viable, the buyer's walk-away threat is less credible; if it is not, the seller must find a way to satisfy the condition.
- **Voss frame:** "No" as a beginning — a buyer saying "we cannot close on this basis" often means "we need something specific to change before we can close." Explore what would change the "no" to a "yes." Sometimes it is a small specific-indemnity item; sometimes it is a price adjustment; sometimes it is a genuine deal-killer that requires renegotiation of a headline term.

## Anti-patterns

Several patterns recur across first-time M&A negotiators (and some experienced ones). Naming them helps avoid them.

- **The one-shot concession.** Conceding on a specific term without extracting a reciprocal concession. Freund's discipline: every give earns a get. The one-shot concession trains the other side to expect additional one-shot concessions.
- **The reactive red-line.** Red-lining every buyer-friendly provision to the seller-friendly opposite, without regard to which provisions actually matter. The result is a bloated red-line that dilutes the seller's negotiating leverage on the provisions that do matter (fiduciary-out, MAC / MAE, cap, sandbagging).
- **The personal-attack response.** Treating an aggressive buyer-side red-line as personal aggression rather than as a first-draft anchoring position. Fisher's discipline: separate people from the problem.
- **The relationship-first cave.** Conceding on substantive terms to preserve the relationship. This is a common founder-side error — the founder wants to work with the buyer post-close and doesn't want to strain the relationship. The result is a materially worse deal that the founder later resents.
- **The BATNA bluff.** Pretending to have a stronger BATNA than you actually have. Sophisticated counterparties see through this; discovery of the bluff destroys credibility and materially weakens negotiating leverage for the remaining rounds.
- **The dying-deal miscalibration.** Deploying a walk-away signal too early (when the counterparty is not yet invested enough to be motivated by it) or too late (when the counterparty has already emotionally checked out). Freund's discipline: the dying-deal move is a specific tactic for a specific moment, not a general posture.
- **The frame-switching failure.** Sticking with one negotiating frame throughout the deal. Different phases call for different frames — the LOI is Freund-heavy (sequencing), the definitive-agreement red-line is Harvard-heavy (objective criteria, interests), the closing conversation is Voss-heavy (calibrated questions, tactical empathy). The negotiator who works with only one frame leaves value on the table.

## Practitioner discipline — preparing for a deal-scale conversation

A practical routine before each substantive negotiation session:

1. **Freund preparation.** What are the specific issues on the table this session? In what order will I raise them? Which are my give-to-get positions? Which am I reserving for the closing conversation?
2. **Harvard preparation.** For each substantive issue, what is my position, what is the underlying interest, what is the other side's position, and what is their underlying interest? What objective criteria (ABA / SRS Acquiom / case law / market comparables) support my position? What is my BATNA and what is theirs?
3. **Voss preparation.** For each substantive issue, what calibrated questions would surface the other side's underlying concerns? What labels might defuse emotional charge? Where is the "that's right" moment I am trying to reach?

The preparation is the difference between a session that produces movement and a session that produces frustration. First-time M&A negotiators typically over-invest in preparation on the *content* (the specific reps, the specific dollar figures) and under-invest in preparation on the *conversational architecture*. The conversational architecture is what turns a red-line comment into a resolved issue.

## Summary

Three distinct bodies of writing form the deal-scale negotiation canon. Freund's *Anatomy of a Merger* provides the sell-side and buy-side sequencing discipline — how to order the fights, how to make give-to-get concessions, how to own the closing conversation. The Harvard Negotiation Project (Fisher, Ury, Patton) provides the principled-negotiation frame — separate people from problem, focus on interests behind positions, invent before deciding, insist on objective criteria and know your BATNA. Voss's *Never Split the Difference* provides the calibrated-question, mirroring, labelling, and tactical-empathy toolkit that reshapes how the other side thinks about the conversation. The frames are complementary; a well-negotiated M&A deal draws on all three at different moments — Freund-heavy in the LOI, Harvard-heavy in the definitive-agreement red-line, Voss-heavy in the closing conversation. The practitioner discipline is to consciously pick the frame that fits the moment and know what tools are on the shelf.
