# Covenants, Closing Conditions, and the MAC / MAE Definition

## Why this matters

Between signing and closing — a 30 to 180 day period, depending on regulatory-clearance requirements, third-party-consent workflows, and the specific closing conditions in the deal — the transaction is a bound agreement to close on specified terms *if* specified conditions are satisfied. Everything in that interim period is governed by the covenants and the closing conditions. Covenants define what each party must and must not do; closing conditions define what must be true (and what must not have happened) for the parties to be obligated to close.

The single most-negotiated definition in any modern M&A agreement — sometimes taking weeks of drafting to resolve — is the **Material Adverse Change / Material Adverse Effect** definition. MAC / MAE serves two functions: it qualifies certain reps and covenants (see chapter 4), and it stands alone as a closing condition that lets the buyer walk if the target suffers a MAC / MAE between signing and closing. Delaware Chancery's development of the MAC / MAE doctrine — culminating in *Akorn v. Fresenius* — has installed a highly-specific practitioner discipline around what qualifies as a MAC / MAE, what does not, and how the definition should be drafted.

This chapter installs the covenants and closing-conditions apparatus, with special attention to the MAC / MAE definition negotiation.

## Covenants — types and mechanics

Covenants are the parties' contractual commitments regarding conduct. In an M&A agreement, covenants fall into three broad temporal categories:

- **Interim covenants** — govern the parties' conduct between signing and closing.
- **Closing covenants** — govern the specific mechanical steps at closing.
- **Post-closing covenants** — govern conduct after closing (some for a defined period, some indefinitely).

Substantively, covenants come in a small number of standard categories.

### Interim operating covenants

The interim-operating covenants require the target to continue operating its business in the ordinary course between signing and closing, and to refrain from specified out-of-ordinary-course actions. Standard language:

> "During the period from the date hereof through the earlier of the Closing or the termination of this Agreement, Target shall, and shall cause each of its Subsidiaries to, (a) conduct the business of Target and its Subsidiaries in the ordinary course of business consistent with past practice, (b) use commercially reasonable efforts to preserve intact its business organisation, retain the services of its officers and key employees, and maintain its relations with material customers, suppliers, and other Persons having business relations with Target."

Then a specific list of prohibited actions — things the target must not do without buyer's prior written consent:

- Amend the charter or bylaws.
- Issue, sell, or grant any equity securities (subject to specific carve-outs for ordinary-course option exercises).
- Declare or pay dividends.
- Acquire or dispose of material assets outside ordinary course.
- Incur material indebtedness.
- Enter into, amend, or terminate material contracts.
- Make material capital expenditures outside the approved budget.
- Hire, fire, or materially compensate executives outside ordinary course.
- Adopt or amend employee benefit plans.
- Change accounting methods.
- Settle material litigation.
- Make material tax elections or amend tax returns.

The list is negotiated line by line. Seller's counsel pushes for higher materiality thresholds and more ordinary-course carve-outs; buyer's counsel pushes for lower thresholds and tighter control.

### The "efforts" standards

Covenants often require a party to use a specified standard of "efforts" to achieve a specified outcome:

- **"Reasonable best efforts"** — the highest standard commonly used; requires the party to take all actions necessary to achieve the outcome short of taking actions that would result in a material adverse effect on the party.
- **"Commercially reasonable efforts"** — a moderate standard; the party must take actions that a reasonable business would take under the circumstances.
- **"Reasonable efforts"** — a lower standard; requires only reasonable actions and does not require the party to bear significant cost or risk.

The choice of standard matters most in the regulatory-cooperation covenant and the third-party-consent covenant, where the covenanting party's efforts determine whether closing conditions can be satisfied. The classic example: the buyer's antitrust-cooperation covenant. A buyer that agrees to use "reasonable best efforts" to obtain HSR clearance may be required to divest material assets or businesses if that is what antitrust regulators demand; a buyer that agrees only to "commercially reasonable efforts" has more room to walk if regulators demand substantial divestitures.

Well-drafted covenants specify not just the efforts standard but also any specific actions the party must take (or is not required to take). The "hell or high water" covenant is the most-buyer-adverse — the buyer agrees to take *any* action necessary to obtain regulatory clearance, including divestiture of specified assets. Sellers of substantial targets often insist on hell-or-high-water language; buyers strongly resist.

### The "no shop" / non-solicitation covenant (definitive-agreement version)

Similar to the LOI-stage exclusivity provision (chapter 2), the definitive agreement contains a no-shop covenant that prohibits the target from soliciting or engaging with alternative-transaction proposals during the interim period. The definitive-agreement no-shop is typically stricter than the LOI-stage exclusivity, but similarly includes a fiduciary-out carve-out for the target's board and matching-rights protection for the buyer.

For public-target agreements, the no-shop covenant and its fiduciary-out carve-out are heavily developed and directly tied to Revlon obligations. See mod-109.

### Regulatory-cooperation covenants

The regulatory-cooperation covenants require the parties to jointly prepare and file all required regulatory filings (HSR, CFIUS, foreign antitrust) promptly after signing, to respond promptly to regulator inquiries, to defend the transaction against regulator challenge, and to cooperate on any required remedial actions (divestitures, behavioral commitments, holding-company structures). The covenants define who files what, who pays which filing fees (see mod-103 chapter 9 for the HSR fee tiers), and who bears the risk of specific outcomes.

### Third-party-consent covenants

Many material contracts contain change-of-control or anti-assignment clauses that require third-party consent to the transaction. The consent covenants require the parties to obtain the required consents, and specify who bears the risk if specific consents cannot be obtained (typically the seller, since the seller controls the underlying contracts; but negotiated).

Third-party consent is often the source of specific closing conditions (see below): the parties may condition closing on obtaining consents from a specified subset of material contracts (typically the top 5–10 customer contracts by revenue, the top 5 supplier contracts, any material lender consents, any material real-property landlord consents).

### Employee-matters covenants

The employee-matters covenants govern what happens to Target's employees between signing and closing (typically minimal change; no material headcount actions without consent) and what happens at closing (which employees are being retained, at what compensation, under what employment agreements, with what non-compete / non-solicit terms). WARN Act compliance obligations (if applicable) are addressed here. See mod-110 for the transaction-communications discipline that surrounds employee-matters covenants.

### Non-compete and non-solicit covenants (post-closing)

The sellers — typically including the founders and key executives who are receiving material consideration in the transaction — agree to specified non-compete and non-solicit restrictions for a defined period after closing (typically 2–5 years; sometimes longer for founders who are receiving very large consideration).

Non-compete enforceability varies significantly by jurisdiction. In California, non-competes are generally unenforceable except in specific M&A contexts (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §16601 for the sale-of-goodwill exception). In New York, non-competes are enforceable if reasonable in scope, duration, and geography. In many other US jurisdictions, non-competes are enforceable subject to reasonable-scope standards. The 2024 FTC non-compete ban (currently subject to ongoing litigation) added a federal-level uncertainty; well-drafted agreements handle this with backup provisions.

### Post-closing cooperation covenants

Various post-closing covenants govern the parties' ongoing cooperation: tax-return preparation and audit defence, insurance-transition, records access, retention of specific records, cooperation on litigation defence, cooperation on regulatory matters.

## Closing conditions

Closing conditions are the specific conditions that must be satisfied (or waived) before either party is obligated to close. Closing conditions are typically bifurcated into:

- **Conditions to buyer's obligations** — must be satisfied for the buyer to be obligated to close.
- **Conditions to seller's obligations** — must be satisfied for the seller to be obligated to close.
- **Mutual conditions** — must be satisfied for both parties to be obligated to close.

### The standard closing-conditions set

- **Bring-down of reps and warranties.** Reps must be true and correct at closing to the specified standard (see chapter 4).
- **Compliance with covenants.** Each party must have complied with its covenants through closing.
- **Absence of MAC / MAE.** No MAC / MAE has occurred between signing and closing (see next section).
- **Regulatory clearances.** HSR waiting period expired without action, CFIUS clearance obtained if required, foreign-antitrust clearances obtained if required.
- **Third-party consents obtained.** Consents from specified material third parties (typically top customers, top suppliers, material lenders, material landlords).
- **No injunction or legal impediment.** No court order or other legal impediment prohibits the transaction.
- **Delivery of closing documents.** Specified closing documents delivered (officer certificates, secretary certificates, opinion letters, resolutions, resignations, R&W insurance policy binder).
- **(For financial-sponsor buyers, sometimes) financing available.** Debt financing available on committed terms.

### Certificate mechanics

The bring-down of reps and the compliance-with-covenants conditions are typically satisfied by delivery of an **officer's certificate** at closing — a formal certificate signed by the target's CEO or CFO certifying that the reps are true and correct at closing to the specified standard and that the target has complied with its covenants. The officer's certificate is a substantive artefact — a false certificate exposes the certifying officer to personal liability.

### Financing conditions

Financing conditions have traditionally been a subject of significant negotiation. A financing condition allows the buyer to walk (subject to any reverse termination fee) if the buyer cannot obtain financing on the terms specified in its commitment letters. Sellers strongly resist financing conditions; buyers with genuine financing risk (financial sponsors relying on committed debt financing) sometimes insist. The modal outcome in modern deals: the buyer's obligations are *not* conditioned on financing; the buyer bears the risk of a financing failure, mitigated by a reverse termination fee (chapter 2) payable to the seller if financing fails.

### Concurrent-with-closing versus condition-precedent-to-closing

Some conditions must be satisfied *before* closing (condition-precedent); others are satisfied *concurrently* with closing (delivery of documents at the closing itself). The distinction matters for the mechanics: a condition-precedent that fails prevents closing from occurring; a concurrent-condition failure means the closing cannot be consummated on the specified date but a further closing date may be scheduled.

## The MAC / MAE definition — the central negotiation

The Material Adverse Change (MAC) or Material Adverse Effect (MAE) definition is the single most-negotiated definition in modern M&A agreements. It serves two functions:

1. **As a qualifier for specific reps and covenants** (see chapter 4). A rep qualified by MAE is breached only if the underlying inaccuracy would reasonably be expected to result in a MAE.
2. **As a standalone closing condition** — one of the mutual closing conditions is typically "no MAE has occurred with respect to Target between signing and closing." If a MAE occurs, the buyer is not obligated to close.

The buyer wants the MAE definition broad — many things should count as a MAE, letting the buyer walk if the target's condition deteriorates. The seller wants the MAE definition narrow — few things should count as a MAE, so the buyer is bound to close except in genuinely catastrophic circumstances.

### The typical MAE definition structure

A modal MAE definition has three parts:

1. **The affirmative clause.** Anything that "has, or would reasonably be expected to have, a material adverse effect on the business, financial condition, results of operations, or assets of Target and its Subsidiaries taken as a whole" is a MAE.

2. **The carve-outs.** A specific list of things that shall *not* constitute a MAE:
   - Changes in general economic conditions.
   - Changes affecting the industry in which Target operates (subject to a "disproportionate impact" clawback — if the industry-wide change affects Target disproportionately, that disproportionate portion can be a MAE).
   - Changes in law, GAAP, or other accounting standards.
   - Acts of war, terrorism, natural disasters, pandemics (yes, this carve-out has been extensively litigated since 2020).
   - Failure of Target to meet financial projections (with the underlying causes of the failure potentially still constituting a MAE).
   - Actions taken with the buyer's consent, or actions required by the definitive agreement itself.
   - Any change in the market price or trading volume of the buyer's stock (relevant only for stock-consideration deals).

3. **The disproportionate-impact clawback.** For carve-outs related to general economic, industry, law, or force-majeure changes, if Target is affected *disproportionately* compared to other companies in its industry, the disproportionate portion of the effect can still constitute a MAE.

### The Akorn v. Fresenius lesson

The 2018 Delaware Chancery Court decision in *In re Akorn v. Fresenius* (upheld by the Delaware Supreme Court) is the single most important MAC / MAE case in modern practice. Vice Chancellor Laster's opinion held that Fresenius was entitled to terminate its acquisition of Akorn on MAE grounds, based on a combination of: a massive drop in Akorn's business performance after signing (revenue and EBITDA declines of 25%+ year over year on a sustained basis); specific breaches of Akorn's data-integrity representations related to FDA compliance; and evidence that the deterioration was durationally significant (not a temporary blip).

The *Akorn* decision established several practitioner principles:

- **Duration matters.** A MAE requires deterioration that is significant enough in *duration* to have long-term consequences, not a short-term dip. The court's benchmark: deterioration that has "substantial and lasting" effect measured in years, not quarters.
- **Magnitude matters.** The financial magnitude required to constitute a MAE is very substantial — typically 25%+ decline in key financial metrics on a sustained basis, though the exact threshold depends on the specific business.
- **Breach of specific reps matters.** Where the target breached specific data-integrity or compliance reps, the breach was a separate closing-condition failure independent of the general MAE analysis.
- **Buyer's-perspective analysis.** The MAE analysis is conducted from the perspective of a "reasonable acquirer" evaluating the target's long-term business prospects.

Before *Akorn*, no Delaware court had ever held that a MAE occurred, despite many buyer attempts to invoke MAE clauses to walk from deteriorating deals. *Akorn* changed the practitioner landscape by demonstrating that MAE claims can succeed — but only in cases where the underlying deterioration is genuinely dramatic and lasting.

The *Channel Medsystems v. Boston Scientific* case (2019) is another important reference — the court applied *Akorn* to hold that a breach of specific data-quality reps constituted a MAE independent of the general business-condition MAE analysis.

### The IBP v. Tyson lesson

The earlier *IBP, Inc. v. Tyson Foods* decision (2001) established the pre-*Akorn* baseline: MAE requires a "substantial threat to the earnings power of the target that is durationally significant." *IBP* held that a temporary earnings decline did not constitute a MAE, even though the decline was substantial in the specific quarter — because the decline was expected to be temporary. The *IBP* / *Akorn* line together establishes that MAE requires both substantial magnitude and substantial duration.

### The pandemic-era carve-out lesson

A wave of MAE litigation followed the COVID-19 pandemic, as buyers attempted to walk from deals signed before the pandemic. Delaware Chancery's response was mixed — some MAE claims succeeded, others did not, and specific analysis depended heavily on the specific carve-out language and the specific ordinary-course covenant language. The practitioner lesson: force-majeure carve-outs (natural disasters, pandemics) should be drafted explicitly rather than left to interpretation, and ordinary-course covenants should be carefully drafted to avoid creating a separate closing-condition failure independent of the MAE analysis.

Specifically, *AB Stable VIII LLC v. MAPS Hotels and Resorts One LLC* (2020) held that the target's response to the COVID-19 pandemic — closing hotels and reducing operations — was outside the "ordinary course of business" contemplated by the ordinary-course covenant, giving the buyer a separate closing-condition failure independent of the MAE analysis. This decision underscored that ordinary-course covenants and MAE clauses are analytically separate — a target may satisfy the MAE clause (no MAE has occurred) while still failing the ordinary-course covenant.

### Drafting the MAE definition

Given the *Akorn* framework, the seller's negotiating position on the MAE definition is:

- **Narrow the affirmative clause.** Focus on effects on "business, financial condition, or results of operations" — not on broader concepts like "prospects" (which could import forward-looking assumptions that the seller cannot warrant).
- **Extensive carve-outs.** Include broad carve-outs for general economic, industry, legal, and force-majeure changes. Include carve-outs for the deal itself (announcement effects, transition costs).
- **Narrow the disproportionate-impact clawback.** Limit the clawback to specific effects and specific comparator groups.
- **No standalone MAC / MAE closing condition.** In some deals, the seller can push to eliminate the standalone MAE closing condition entirely (the MAE would then only qualify specific reps). Buyers strongly resist.

The buyer's negotiating position:

- **Broad affirmative clause.** Include "prospects" in the affirmative clause. Include effects on Target's key employees, customers, and business relationships.
- **Narrow carve-outs.** Carve-outs should be limited to clearly-defined categories; force-majeure carve-outs should be limited to specifically-listed events (not open-ended).
- **Broad disproportionate-impact clawback.** The clawback should apply broadly to any carve-out where Target is affected differently from comparators.
- **Standalone MAC / MAE closing condition.** The MAE clause should be a standalone closing condition (in addition to qualifying specific reps).

The modal outcome for a competitive process where the seller has leverage: a MAE definition that focuses on business, financial condition, and results of operations; extensive carve-outs (with the modern list of specifically-listed force-majeure events including pandemics); a disproportionate-impact clawback limited to specific categories; and a standalone MAE closing condition with the *Akorn* duration and magnitude expectations built in.

## Ordinary-course covenants — the Akorn / AB Stable teaching

The ordinary-course covenant is a separate substantive covenant (see above) but interacts directly with the MAE clause. *AB Stable* teaches that a target's response to changing circumstances — even a reasonable, defensive response — can breach the ordinary-course covenant and create a closing-condition failure independent of the MAE clause.

The practitioner response:

- **Precise definition of "ordinary course."** Well-drafted agreements define "ordinary course of business" with reference to past practice, industry practice, and specific contemplated categories of action.
- **Consent mechanics for unusual actions.** The ordinary-course covenant should include a mechanism for the target to seek buyer consent for actions that are outside ordinary course but are appropriate in the circumstances. The buyer's consent should not be unreasonably withheld.
- **Force-majeure response carve-outs.** For force-majeure events (pandemics, natural disasters), the ordinary-course covenant should carve out reasonable actions taken in response.
- **Interaction with MAE carve-outs.** The ordinary-course covenant and the MAE clause should be drafted consistently — if the MAE clause carves out pandemic effects, the ordinary-course covenant should not create a separate closing-condition failure for pandemic-response actions.

## Interim-period risk management

Given the closing-conditions apparatus, the interim period is a substantive period of risk management for both parties:

- **The seller's job.** Continue operating the business in ordinary course. Avoid actions that would breach the interim-operating covenants. Prosecute closing diligently. Prepare for closing.
- **The buyer's job.** Complete post-signing diligence. Prosecute regulatory filings. Monitor the target's operations. Prepare for closing. If material adverse developments occur, evaluate MAE / covenant-breach analysis.
- **Communication discipline.** Both parties communicate through counsel; substantive disputes go to principals only after counsel-to-counsel efforts. This is important because premature accusations of MAE or covenant breach can themselves become the subject of dispute.

If the buyer believes an MAE or covenant breach has occurred, the buyer's typical playbook is:

1. **Send a notice of concern.** Counsel-to-counsel notice specifying the alleged breach and requesting information.
2. **Enter into discussions.** Discussions to understand the seller's position; opportunity to remedy or for the seller to provide additional context.
3. **Attempt price re-trade.** If the alleged breach is genuine but the buyer does not want to walk, the buyer may propose a price re-trade — a reduction in the purchase price in exchange for waiving the alleged breach.
4. **File for termination and (if necessary) litigate.** If the parties cannot resolve, the buyer terminates the agreement and (if the seller disputes the termination) faces Delaware Chancery litigation.

The seller's playbook mirrors this — deny the alleged breach if disputed, provide information to defuse concerns, negotiate price re-trade if necessary to preserve the deal, and (if necessary) sue for specific performance to force the buyer to close.

Specific performance is the seller's primary remedy in a Delaware M&A agreement — the seller can obtain a court order forcing the buyer to close. Delaware courts have been willing to grant specific performance in appropriate cases (*Snow Phipps Group v. KCake Acquisition*, 2021), which shifts the leverage balance in interim-period disputes toward the seller.

## Common negotiation moves

- **Buyer's draft has "Material Adverse Effect" defined to include effects on "prospects"; seller pushes to remove.** "Prospects" is buyer-favourable because it imports forward-looking assumptions. Standard resolution: remove "prospects" or narrowly define.
- **Buyer's draft has ordinary-course covenant defined as "past practice"; seller pushes for "past practice or as required by law or as reasonably necessary in response to changed circumstances."** Buyer resists; standard resolution is a modest expansion of the definition with specific carve-outs.
- **Buyer's draft has "hell or high water" antitrust covenant; seller pushes for "commercially reasonable efforts" with specified divestiture limits.** Standard resolution: seller specifies the maximum divestitures the buyer is required to accept.
- **Buyer's draft has broad third-party-consent condition (any material consent); seller pushes for a specific list of required consents (top 5 customers, top 3 suppliers).** Standard resolution: specific list with dollar-threshold criteria.
- **Buyer's draft has broad standalone MAE closing condition; seller pushes for MAE that only qualifies specific reps.** Standard resolution: standalone MAE closing condition with extensive carve-outs and disproportionate-impact clawback.

## Summary

Covenants govern the parties' conduct during the interim period between signing and closing (interim-operating covenants, no-shop, regulatory cooperation, third-party consents, employee matters) and post-closing (non-compete, non-solicit, cooperation). Closing conditions define what must be satisfied for the parties to close (bring-down of reps, compliance with covenants, MAC / MAE absence, regulatory clearances, third-party consents, no legal impediment, closing deliverables). The MAC / MAE definition is the single most-negotiated definition in the agreement; the *Akorn v. Fresenius* line establishes that MAE requires substantial magnitude and substantial duration, and specific rep breaches can constitute a separate closing-condition failure. The *AB Stable* line establishes that ordinary-course covenants can create closing-condition failures independent of the MAE analysis, and force-majeure response carve-outs should be drafted explicitly. Specific performance is available to the seller and has been granted in appropriate cases, shifting interim-period leverage toward the seller.
