# R&W Insurance — Mechanics, Underwriting, and the Traditional-Indemnity Trade-Off

## Why this matters

Representations-and-warranties (R&W) insurance is a specialised insurance product that indemnifies the insured (typically the buyer, sometimes the seller) for losses arising from breaches of the seller's representations and warranties in a definitive M&A agreement. Over the past decade, R&W insurance has moved from a niche product to a routine feature of mid-market and larger private-target M&A. In current practice, the substantial majority of private-target transactions above ~$50M enterprise value are R&W-insurance-supported, and the traditional-indemnification-only structure has become the exception rather than the rule at that size range.

R&W insurance changes the transaction economics materially. The seller's indemnification exposure is dramatically reduced — often to a small escrow (0.5–1% of purchase price for the retention gap) plus specific carve-outs — and the buyer's protection comes from an insurance policy with a defined limit (typically 10–20% of enterprise value). The escrow reduction alone can free 10–20% of the purchase price for the seller at closing rather than 12–24 months later. The R&W-insurance-underwriting process becomes a parallel workstream to the definitive-agreement drafting, requiring specific coordination between the parties, their counsel, the broker, and the underwriter.

This chapter installs the R&W insurance mechanics: market conditions and the broker set (Marsh / Aon / Lockton), the underwriting process from broker engagement to policy binding, the retention economics and how the retention interacts with the indemnification package, the buyer-side vs. seller-side placement decision, the standard exclusions and specific-diligence exclusions, and the substantive trade-off analysis between traditional indemnification and R&W insurance for a specific transaction.

## Market conditions and the broker set

R&W insurance is distributed almost exclusively through specialised M&A insurance brokers. The dominant brokers in the US market are:

- **Marsh McLennan (Marsh)** — the largest global M&A insurance broker; extensive R&W insurance practice with dedicated M&A insurance groups across US, EMEA, and APAC.
- **Aon** — the second-largest global broker; extensive R&W practice.
- **Lockton** — private, US-headquartered broker with substantial R&W practice.
- **WTW (Willis Towers Watson)** — global broker with R&W practice.
- **Woodruff Sawyer, HUB International, USI, Alliant** — mid-tier brokers with R&W practice.

Each broker has a dedicated M&A insurance team that manages underwriter relationships, coordinates the underwriting process, and negotiates policy terms on behalf of the client (buyer or seller).

The underwriter market is separately structured. Primary underwriters in the US R&W market include:

- **AIG / American International Group**
- **Liberty GTS (Liberty Global Transaction Solutions)**
- **Beazley**
- **Euclid Transactional**
- **Vale Insurance Partners**
- **Concord Speciality Risk**
- **CFC**

Multiple underwriters typically bid on a single deal; the broker manages a competitive process to obtain the best combination of premium, retention, and policy terms. Larger policies (limits above ~$50M) are often placed with a "primary" underwriter plus one or more "excess" underwriters providing coverage above the primary layer.

Broker-published annual reports (Marsh's R&W Insurance Report, Aon's M&A Insurance Market Update, Lockton's M&A Insurance Report) are the practitioner reference for current market conditions — premium rates, retention levels, claim frequency and severity, coverage trends, and market capacity. These reports should be consulted for the current-cycle market read.

## Retention economics

The **retention** (also called "attachment point" or "deductible") is the amount of loss the insured must bear before the insurance coverage attaches. In R&W insurance, the retention is typically expressed as a percentage of enterprise value:

- **Primary retention.** Typical range 0.75% to 1.5% of enterprise value, with 1% as a modal anchor.
- **Retention drop.** The retention often drops after 12 months from closing to a lower level (typically 0.5% of enterprise value). The drop reflects the reduced probability of undiscovered breaches surfacing after 12 months of buyer ownership.
- **Retention structure.** The retention can be structured as (i) buyer-side self-insured, (ii) seller-side escrow, or (iii) a hybrid split. See "The retention gap" below.

Retention interacts with the indemnification package as follows:

- **Losses below the retention.** The buyer bears the loss up to the retention level (from escrow, from seller recourse, or from self-insurance).
- **Losses within the coverage layer.** The insurance policy covers losses above the retention and up to the policy limit.
- **Losses above the policy limit.** The buyer bears the excess loss, unless the buyer has purchased additional excess coverage.

For a $200M enterprise-value transaction with a 1% primary retention and a $20M (10% of EV) policy limit, the coverage structure is:
- $0–$2M: buyer bears loss (retention).
- $2M–$22M: insurance policy covers.
- Above $22M: buyer bears excess loss.

## Policy limits

The policy limit is negotiated based on the buyer's assessment of transaction risk, the size of the transaction, and market pricing. Typical policy limits:

- **10% of enterprise value** — the most common single-policy limit for mid-market transactions.
- **15–20% of enterprise value** — common for transactions with elevated diligence-surfaced risk or specific rep exposures.
- **Above 20% of enterprise value** — less common but available through excess layers, typically for very large transactions or transactions with specific high-risk rep exposures.

Limits typically range from a few million dollars (for small transactions) to hundreds of millions (for large transactions). Policy limits above ~$50M are typically syndicated across multiple underwriters.

## Premium economics

The premium is the fee the insured pays for the R&W policy. Premium is typically expressed as a percentage of the policy limit (the "rate on line") and typically ranges from 2% to 5% of the policy limit, with 2.5%–3.5% as a modal range in recent cycles. For a $20M policy limit, the premium would typically be $500K–$700K.

Premium is affected by:

- **Transaction size and complexity.** Larger transactions have lower rates on line; smaller transactions have higher rates on line (reflecting fixed underwriting costs).
- **Diligence quality.** Well-diligenced transactions with clean rep exposure command lower rates; transactions with elevated diligence risk command higher rates.
- **Rep scope and quality.** Comprehensive, well-drafted reps command lower rates; narrower or weaker reps command higher rates (or specific exclusions).
- **Target industry.** Some industries (healthcare, technology, financial services) have industry-specific risk profiles that affect underwriter pricing.
- **Market conditions.** R&W insurance market pricing fluctuates with underwriter capacity, claim frequency, and macro-market conditions. In tight markets (2019–2021), rates were compressed and terms were competitive; in looser markets (post-2022), rates have normalised.

Premium is a one-time payment at policy binding (typically at closing). The premium is typically paid by the buyer for a buyer-side policy or by the seller for a seller-side policy; the transaction economics can allocate the premium as a shared cost (seller pays out of the purchase price, effectively).

## The underwriting process

R&W insurance underwriting is a specific process that runs in parallel with the definitive-agreement drafting. The modal underwriting timeline is 3–5 weeks from broker engagement to policy binding.

### Week 0 — broker engagement

The buyer (or the seller, for a seller-side policy) engages an R&W insurance broker. The broker gathers the initial transaction information:

- Transaction size and structure.
- Target industry and general profile.
- Anticipated diligence workstreams.
- Target legal counsel and financial-diligence provider identities.
- Anticipated closing timeline.

The broker prepares a **broker submission** — a summary of the transaction that goes to multiple underwriters for indicative quotes.

### Week 1 — indicative quotes

Underwriters review the broker submission and provide indicative quotes:

- Indicative premium (as a rate on line).
- Indicative retention.
- Standard exclusions.
- Any preliminary comments on the transaction (specific due-diligence requirements, likely specific exclusions).

Indicative quotes are non-binding but represent the underwriter's initial assessment. The buyer (or seller) selects a primary underwriter based on the quote and the underwriter's reputation and claims-handling track record.

### Weeks 1–3 — underwriting due diligence

The selected underwriter conducts substantive due diligence on the transaction:

- **Diligence-memo review.** The underwriter reviews the buyer's legal-diligence memo, financial-diligence memo, tax-diligence memo, and other workstream memos. This is why diligence memo quality is critical — the underwriter is essentially underwriting the buyer's diligence work.
- **Data-room review.** The underwriter reviews the data-room contents to assess the diligence-surface risk.
- **Underwriting call.** The underwriter holds a call (typically 2–4 hours) with the buyer's diligence team, the buyer's counsel, and (sometimes) the target's counsel to discuss specific diligence findings, exposures, and the buyer's assessment of each.
- **Draft SPA review.** The underwriter reviews the near-final draft SPA to assess the specific rep scope and any specific language issues.

### Week 3–4 — underwriter comments and exclusions

The underwriter delivers a **preliminary comment memo** listing any specific concerns and any specific exclusions the underwriter intends to include in the policy. Common categories of exclusions:

- **Known matters.** Any matter the buyer has actual knowledge of before closing is excluded (unless specifically negotiated as covered — see "Known-matter coverage" below).
- **Diligence-surfaced items.** Specific diligence findings that the underwriter has flagged as elevated-risk (typically 2–5 items per transaction) may be excluded or specifically-carve-out.
- **Industry-specific exclusions.** Sector-specific exclusions may apply (e.g., specific product-liability exclusions for manufacturers; specific data-privacy exclusions for consumer-data-heavy targets).
- **Standard exclusions.** Purchase-price adjustments, forward-looking statements, specific tax matters (transfer pricing, transaction-tax exposure), pension-underfunding, environmental (unless specifically covered), specific-performance obligations, IP infringement in certain contexts.

The buyer's counsel and broker negotiate the exclusions with the underwriter, typically successfully narrowing some and unable to remove others. The final exclusions are documented in the policy.

### Week 4–5 — policy binding

The policy is finalised and bound. Binding typically occurs at closing of the transaction (the policy incepts at closing). The premium is paid; the policy documents are signed; coverage is in force.

### Post-closing — claim handling

If a claim arises post-closing, the buyer submits the claim to the R&W insurer. The insurer reviews the claim, assesses coverage, and (if coverage attaches) pays the claim above the retention up to the policy limit. Claim disputes go to arbitration (typical) or litigation, per the policy terms.

## Buyer-side versus seller-side placement

R&W insurance can be placed with the buyer as the insured (buyer-side policy) or with the seller as the insured (seller-side policy). The distinction is substantive:

### Buyer-side policy (dominant modern pattern)

- The **buyer** is the insured party. If a rep is breached, the buyer claims against the insurance policy for the loss (after the retention).
- The seller has no rights or obligations under the policy. The seller's indemnification obligation to the buyer is capped by the retention (subject to the specific structure).
- Buyer-side is the dominant modern pattern. It aligns the insured party (buyer) with the party bearing the claim risk (buyer).

### Seller-side policy (less common)

- The **seller** is the insured party. The buyer brings a traditional indemnification claim against the seller; the seller then claims against its insurance policy for reimbursement.
- The buyer has no direct rights against the insurer. The buyer's protection depends on the seller's indemnification obligation, which is (indirectly) backed by the insurance.
- Seller-side policies are less common because they introduce an extra layer of dispute (buyer vs. seller, then seller vs. insurer) and because they are less attractive to buyers (who prefer direct-recourse against an insurer).

### Placement mechanics

Placement can be initiated by either party:

- **Buyer initiates.** The buyer decides to place a buyer-side policy as part of its diligence workstream planning. The seller may agree to pay some or all of the premium as part of the transaction economics.
- **Seller initiates.** The seller decides to place a seller-side policy to reduce its post-closing indemnification exposure. Less common in modern practice.
- **Seller-facilitated buyer-side.** The seller identifies R&W insurance as part of the transaction structure, engages a broker, obtains indicative quotes, and delivers them to the buyer at LOI stage or at diligence launch. The buyer then places the policy. This "seller-facilitated buyer-side" pattern is increasingly common as it lets the seller signal the transaction economics (reduced escrow, reduced indemnification) early.

### Premium allocation

- **Buyer pays.** Buyer bears the premium as a transaction cost.
- **Seller pays.** Seller pays the premium out of the purchase price (effectively reducing net proceeds).
- **Split.** Common allocation: buyer pays 50%, seller pays 50% (or buyer pays the premium and seller reimburses through a purchase-price adjustment).

The specific allocation is negotiated based on the parties' respective assessments of who benefits from the insurance — typically both parties benefit (buyer gets protection; seller gets reduced indemnification exposure), so a shared cost is defensible.

## Standard exclusions and specific-diligence exclusions

### Standard exclusions (typical in every R&W policy)

- **Purchase-price adjustments.** Working-capital adjustment, closing-cash / closing-debt adjustments, transaction-expense adjustments are not covered (they are addressed by the SPA's specific adjustment mechanics).
- **Forward-looking statements.** Projections, budgets, and other forward-looking statements are not covered (the reps typically do not extend to forward-looking statements; the policy follows the reps).
- **Specific tax items.** Transfer-pricing exposure, transaction-tax exposure (§280G, §338(h)(10) elections), and specific pre-closing tax exposures are typically excluded or specifically-carve-out (with separate tax insurance available as a supplemental product).
- **Environmental (sometimes).** Environmental exposure is typically excluded from the general R&W policy but can be covered through separate environmental insurance.
- **Pension underfunding.** Multi-employer pension withdrawal liability and single-employer defined-benefit pension underfunding are typically excluded.
- **Fraud.** Fraud is excluded (though the definition of fraud may vary — the policy language should be reviewed against the SPA's fraud definition).
- **Specific-performance obligations.** Failure to perform specific closing conditions (deliverable failures) is not covered.

### Specific-diligence exclusions

Beyond the standard exclusions, the underwriter typically imposes specific exclusions based on the diligence findings for the specific transaction. Common examples:

- **Named litigation matters.** A specific pending or threatened litigation matter identified in diligence may be excluded.
- **Named regulatory matters.** A specific pending or threatened regulatory matter may be excluded.
- **Named contract disputes.** A specific known contract dispute may be excluded.
- **Named IP matters.** A specific known IP infringement claim or exposure may be excluded.
- **Named privacy matters.** A specific known data-breach investigation or privacy exposure may be excluded.
- **Industry-specific exclusions.** For example, product-liability exclusions for manufacturers, medical-device exclusions for medical-device targets, PFAS / environmental-tort exclusions for chemical or materials targets.

The buyer's counsel and broker negotiate these exclusions with the underwriter, sometimes successfully narrowing them (e.g., limiting the exclusion to a specific known matter rather than the broader rep category) and sometimes not. Exclusions the buyer cannot remove may need to be addressed through separate coverage (specific tax insurance, environmental insurance) or through a specific indemnification carve-out in the SPA (the seller directly indemnifies for the excluded matter, funded through a special-indemnity escrow).

### Known-matter coverage

Standard R&W policies exclude matters the buyer has actual knowledge of before closing (the "known-matter" exclusion). For some specifically-identified matters, the buyer can purchase **known-matter coverage** as a supplemental product — the underwriter agrees to cover a specific known matter (e.g., a specific tax exposure the buyer has identified in diligence and quantified) up to a specific limit for a specific additional premium. This is common for specific tax exposures that the buyer wants to cover without leaving the exposure on the seller's indemnification.

## The trade-off — R&W insurance vs. traditional indemnification

The choice between R&W-insurance-supported and traditional-indemnification-only depends on the specific transaction facts. The trade-off analysis:

### Where R&W insurance shines

- **Transactions above ~$50M enterprise value.** Below that size, R&W insurance premium and retention economics become unattractive relative to a traditional escrow structure.
- **Dispersed stockholder bases.** For a target with dozens or hundreds of stockholders (venture-backed targets with multiple financing rounds, employee-owned stockholder bases), direct-recourse against individual stockholders is impractical. R&W insurance provides a single-source recovery.
- **Financial-buyer / private-equity acquisitions.** PE buyers typically prefer R&W insurance because it lets them exit their acquisition cleanly (the target's former sellers are typically not available for post-closing indemnification claims).
- **Speed to closing.** R&W insurance can reduce the negotiating time on the indemnification package because the parties can defer the traditional cap / basket / survival negotiation to the retention-and-policy-limit negotiation.
- **Seller optics.** For a seller, R&W insurance dramatically reduces post-closing indemnification exposure — most or all of the purchase price is received clean at closing.
- **Transactions with mixed buyer-side comfort.** If some portion of the buyer's team is uncomfortable with specific rep exposures, R&W insurance provides an insurance-backed source of recovery that is independent of the seller's willingness or ability to pay.

### Where traditional indemnification shines

- **Small transactions.** Below ~$50M enterprise value, R&W insurance minimum-premium and fixed-cost economics are unattractive; traditional escrow is cheaper and simpler.
- **Concentrated seller bases.** If the seller is one or two large-stake stockholders (a founder-controlled target with a small stockholder set), direct-recourse against the sellers is practical and R&W insurance is less necessary.
- **Very-high-risk transactions.** For transactions with material identified risks that R&W insurance would exclude, traditional indemnification (with a specific special-indemnity for the identified risks) may be the only workable structure.
- **Strategic buyers with long relationships.** In transactions between a strategic buyer and a target the buyer has known for years, both parties may prefer the direct-recourse-plus-relationship-management approach of traditional indemnification.
- **Some cross-border transactions.** R&W insurance markets are less developed in some non-US jurisdictions; local-market practice may favour traditional indemnification.

### The hybrid approach

Many modern transactions combine R&W insurance with residual traditional indemnification:

- **R&W policy** covers general reps up to the policy limit (typically 10% of EV).
- **Small retention escrow** (typically 0.5–1% of EV) held for 12–18 months to cover the retention.
- **Special indemnity escrow** for identified diligence issues that the R&W policy excludes.
- **Seller indemnification** for fraud, and (sometimes) for specific-carve-out categories not covered by the R&W policy.
- **Fundamental / tax reps** typically remain seller-indemnified up to the full purchase price (the R&W policy may or may not cover fundamental / tax reps at the policy limit).

The hybrid approach captures most of the R&W benefits (reduced escrow, insurance-backed recovery for general reps) while preserving specific-carve-out and fundamental / tax protection through traditional mechanisms.

## Common R&W insurance mistakes

- **Late broker engagement.** Engaging the broker too late in the process (e.g., week 4 of a 6-week diligence sprint) compresses the underwriting timeline and forces suboptimal exclusion negotiation. Engage the broker at the start of diligence, ideally at or before LOI signing.
- **Weak diligence documentation.** Underwriters underwrite the diligence memos, not the underlying facts. Weak or poorly-documented diligence memos result in more exclusions and higher premiums.
- **Failure to negotiate exclusions.** The initial exclusion list is a starting point. Aggressive negotiation (through the broker) can typically narrow specific exclusions or convert broad exclusions into specific-known-matter exclusions.
- **Not coordinating the policy with the SPA.** The policy language and the SPA language should be coordinated — the reps in the SPA drive the coverage in the policy, so any specific SPA language matters. Broker and buyer's counsel should coordinate closely.
- **Under-sizing the policy limit.** A policy limit of 10% of EV is standard but may be inadequate for some transactions. The buyer should analyse the specific risk profile and consider excess layers for elevated-risk transactions.
- **Ignoring the retention gap.** The retention gap (the 0.5–1% of EV the buyer bears before insurance attaches) must be funded through some combination of escrow, seller recourse, or self-insurance. Failing to fund the retention gap leaves the buyer exposed to the first-dollar-loss risk.

## Interaction with the SPA

The R&W policy is a *separate* contract between the insured and the insurer, but its terms interact with the SPA:

- **The reps in the SPA drive coverage.** The policy typically references the SPA reps and provides coverage for breaches of those reps. Changes to the reps in the SPA can affect the policy coverage.
- **The definition of "Loss" in the SPA and the policy should align.** Damages calculation methodologies (e.g., diminution in value, out-of-pocket losses, multiplier-based losses) should be consistent between the SPA indemnification provision and the policy.
- **Sandbagging and knowledge qualifiers should align.** The policy's knowledge exclusions and the SPA's sandbagging provisions should be coordinated to avoid gap or overlap.
- **The policy's exclusions should be reflected in the SPA structure.** If the policy excludes a specific matter, that matter may need to be separately indemnified by the seller (or funded through a special-indemnity escrow).

## Summary

R&W insurance is a specialised insurance product that has moved from niche to standard in mid-market and larger private-target M&A. The market is served by specialised brokers (Marsh / Aon / Lockton / WTW) and a handful of primary underwriters (AIG / Liberty GTS / Beazley / Euclid / Vale / others). Retentions are typically 1% of enterprise value dropping to 0.5% after 12 months; policy limits are typically 10–20% of enterprise value; premiums are typically 2–5% of the policy limit. The underwriting process runs 3–5 weeks in parallel with the definitive-agreement drafting and requires substantive coordination between broker, buyer's counsel, and underwriter. Buyer-side policies dominate modern practice. Standard exclusions and specific-diligence exclusions must be negotiated carefully; excluded items may need separate treatment through the SPA. The trade-off between R&W insurance and traditional indemnification depends on transaction size, stockholder concentration, buyer type, and specific risk profile — a hybrid approach (R&W insurance for general reps, traditional indemnification for fundamental / tax reps and specific-carve-out categories) is now the modal structure for transactions above ~$50M enterprise value.
