# exercise-04: SPA reps-and-warranties red-line drill

**Estimated effort:** 4–5 hours

## Objective

Red-line a Stock Purchase Agreement's representations-and-warranties article — layer by layer through the seven-layer reps stack — from the opposite side of whichever seat you are more comfortable in. If you approach the exercise most naturally as a seller, red-line a buyer-friendly SPA rep package from a seller position. If more naturally as a buyer, red-line a seller-friendly SPA rep package from a buyer position. Either way the finished artefact is a full-length redline of Article III (Company Representations and Warranties) with margin comments explaining each material change, plus a 2–3 page issues list that a partner-level M&A lawyer would take into the next negotiation round.

By the end you should be able to defend every material qualifier addition or removal, every scope narrowing or broadening, every disclosure-schedule reference, and every knowledge-and-materiality qualifier — and to negotiate the resulting redline with a sophisticated counterparty.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 4 — Definitive-Agreement Architecture and the Reps-and-Warranties Layering](../04-definitive-agreement-architecture-and-reps-layering.md) as the primary reference — the seven-layer reps stack, disclosure schedules as risk-allocation artefact, the specific rep categories (fundamental / general / tax / environmental / compliance / IP / privacy / AI-model).
- [Chapter 6 — Indemnification Package Design](../06-indemnification-package-design.md) as supporting reference — the survival / cap / basket structure that the rep-scope negotiation feeds into.
- [Chapter 8 — Deal-Scale Negotiation Canon](../08-deal-scale-negotiation-canon.md) for the Freund sequencing on which reps to fight first vs. last.

## Prerequisites

- The same hypothetical transaction one-pager from exercises 01–02.
- The deal-structure choice from mod-103 chapter 1 (SPA / Merger Agreement / APA — this exercise assumes an SPA; adapt if your structure is a Merger Agreement or APA).
- Access to a full-length SPA template. Suitable starting points: the ABA Model Stock Purchase Agreement (with Commentary), the Cooley GO SPA template, the ABA Deal Points Study reference documents, or any recently-signed SPA from a public-target definitive-agreement filing on SEC EDGAR. Do NOT use a specific-transaction SPA verbatim — extract the structure and re-derive.
- The disclosure-schedules discipline from chapter 4 — schedules as the actual risk-allocation artefact.

## Tasks

### 1. Set the negotiating seat and starting draft

Declare your seat: **seller-side redline of a buyer-friendly first draft**, or **buyer-side redline of a seller-friendly counter-draft**. Both seats are valid; pick the one that stretches you more.

Then obtain (or draft) the starting rep package. If you selected seller-side, start with a maximally-buyer-friendly first draft: broad rep scopes, few materiality qualifiers, few knowledge qualifiers, aggressive disclosure-schedule discipline (no cross-references, hard-to-find disclosure requirement), pro-sandbagging language. If you selected buyer-side, start with a maximally-seller-friendly counter-draft: narrow rep scopes, extensive materiality qualifiers, extensive knowledge qualifiers, loose disclosure-schedule discipline, anti-sandbagging language.

Write a 1-paragraph description of the starting draft — where it sits on the buyer-vs-seller spectrum on each of the seven layers.

### 2. Red-line the fundamental reps (layer 1)

Fundamental reps: corporate organisation, capitalisation, authorisation to enter the agreement, no consents needed (or consents named specifically), no conflicts with charter / bylaws / material contracts, title to shares (for a stock deal).

Sub-tasks:

- **Corporate organisation.** Redline the specific corporate-existence rep. Add / remove qualifiers ("duly organised and validly existing," "good standing"). Consider the specific jurisdictions in which good-standing is required — Delaware (for a Delaware C-corp target), each state of qualification-to-do-business.
- **Capitalisation.** This is the most-negotiated fundamental rep and often the source of the largest fundamental-rep breach exposure. Redline the specific cap-table statement — Common outstanding, Preferred outstanding by series, options and warrants and convertible-note conversions. Add specific disclosure-schedule references. Consider the treatment of vested / unvested / early-exercised shares. Consider the treatment of RSUs.
- **Authorisation.** Redline the "duly authorised" language. Consider whether the target's board approval and (for a stock deal) stockholder approval are conditions to signing or to closing.
- **No conflicts.** Redline the "no conflicts with charter / bylaws / material contracts" rep. Consider the specific material-contract change-of-control provisions that would be triggered (see chapter 5's third-party-consent covenants).
- **Title (for a stock deal).** Redline the individual-stockholder rep on title. Consider whether individual stockholders are joined as parties.

For each redline, add a margin comment (1–2 sentences) explaining the position.

### 3. Red-line the general reps (layer 2)

General reps: financial statements, absence of undisclosed liabilities, absence of certain changes since a specified date, contracts, real property, personal property, litigation, employment matters, employee benefits, insurance, permits, absence of conflicts of interest.

For each general rep, walk through:

- **Scope.** What specifically is being warranted?
- **Materiality qualifier.** Is a materiality qualifier appropriate? Where? At what threshold?
- **Knowledge qualifier.** Is a knowledge qualifier appropriate? Whose knowledge — the CEO, the CFO, specific named officers, all directors and officers?
- **Bring-down mechanic (chapter 5).** Does the rep survive signing to closing subject to a bring-down, and if so, at what materiality threshold (usually MAC / MAE for general reps)?
- **Disclosure-schedule reference.** Which specific disclosure schedule does the rep tie to, and how granular is the required disclosure?

Focus specifically on:

- **Financial-statements rep.** The single most-important general rep. Redline the specific language on presentation ("in accordance with GAAP consistently applied," "fairly presents in all material respects"). Consider unaudited-financials treatment.
- **Absence-of-undisclosed-liabilities rep.** Consider the specific formulation — "except as set forth on the Company Financial Statements or on Schedule X" vs. broader language. Consider knowledge and materiality qualifiers.
- **Absence-of-changes-since-date rep.** Consider the specific list of prohibited events (asset dispositions, indebtedness incurrences, dividend declarations, benefit-plan changes, litigation initiations). Match to chapter 5's interim-operating covenants.
- **Contracts rep.** Consider the specific definition of "Material Contract" — dollar-threshold-based, category-based, or hybrid. Draft the specific categories (top-N customer contracts by revenue, top-N supplier contracts, all indebtedness, all material real property, all IP licenses in and out, all severance / retention arrangements, all joint ventures, all non-competes binding the target).
- **Litigation rep.** Consider the scope of "pending" vs. "threatened" litigation. Consider the specific de-minimis threshold below which litigation need not be disclosed.

### 4. Red-line the tax reps (layer 3)

Tax reps are often given "fundamental" treatment for survival and cap purposes but are structurally general reps. Redline:

- Filing of all required tax returns.
- Payment of all taxes due.
- No pending or threatened tax examinations, audits, or disputes.
- Consistency of tax positions with prior-period returns.
- No specific tax elections outside the ordinary course (§338 elections, S-corp status, foreign-entity classification).
- Compliance with transfer-pricing rules (for a multi-jurisdictional target).
- No obligation under a tax-sharing agreement or similar arrangement.
- Specific state-tax nexus and sales-tax compliance (particularly for a SaaS target with multi-state nexus exposure).

Consider the specific state-and-local tax risk profile of the target. A SaaS target with dispersed customer geography has meaningful sales-tax-nexus exposure; the tax rep must be drafted to allocate this risk.

### 5. Red-line the environmental reps (layer 4)

Environmental reps: compliance with environmental laws, absence of environmental claims, absence of releases of hazardous substances, environmental permits.

For a technology target with limited physical footprint, the environmental rep can be narrower and more heavily-qualified. For a target with a manufacturing footprint or physical-inventory operations, the rep is more substantive.

Consider knowledge qualifiers (typically CEO / CFO / General Counsel / any employee with environmental-compliance responsibility) and materiality qualifiers.

### 6. Red-line the compliance-with-law reps (layer 5)

Compliance-with-law reps: general compliance, specific-regulatory compliance (sector-specific — healthcare for a healthtech target, financial-services for a fintech target, education for an edtech target), no ongoing government investigations or enforcement actions, no material past violations.

Consider the specific regulatory-body list — SEC, DOJ, FTC, HHS-OIG, CFPB, state regulators, foreign regulators. Consider the specific sanctions-and-anti-corruption reps (OFAC, FCPA, UK Bribery Act) — increasingly standard even for smaller targets.

For an AI-first target, consider whether the compliance-with-law rep needs a specific AI-regulation carve-in — EU AI Act, state-level AI regulations (California, Colorado, others), NIST AI RMF alignment.

### 7. Red-line the IP reps (layer 6)

IP reps: ownership of registered IP, ownership of material unregistered IP, no infringement, no IP-related claims, adequate IP protection, employee IP-assignment discipline, open-source-software compliance.

The IP rep is where the buyer's counsel invests the most attention for a technology target. Redline specifically:

- **Ownership.** Distinguish "Owns" from "has valid and enforceable rights to use." Consider joint-development arrangements, work-for-hire treatment, university-collaboration IP.
- **No infringement.** Distinguish "does not infringe" (broad) from "to the knowledge of the Company, does not infringe" (narrower). Consider whether the rep covers past infringement or only current infringement.
- **IP-related claims.** Consider "pending" vs. "threatened." Consider the specific de-minimis threshold.
- **Employee IP assignment.** Consider whether the rep covers all current employees, all former employees, all consultants, and all contractors. Consider the "assignor estoppel" hazard where a former employee retains rights.
- **Open-source-software compliance.** Consider the specific OSS-license categories — permissive (MIT, BSD, Apache), weak-copyleft (LGPL, MPL), strong-copyleft (GPL, AGPL). Consider whether the target has any GPL-contaminated code paths that could trigger copyleft obligations for the buyer's derivative products.

For an AI-first target, add specific AI-training-data reps:

- Rights to training data used (owned, licensed with sufficient scope, publicly available with appropriate rights).
- Absence of infringement claims related to training data (increasingly relevant with pending litigation on training-data copyright and derivative rights).
- Compliance with third-party terms-of-service on scraped training data.
- Documentation of the training-data provenance for regulatory compliance (EU AI Act general-purpose AI transparency).

### 8. Red-line the privacy reps (layer 7 — part a)

Privacy reps: compliance with applicable privacy laws (GDPR, CCPA / CPRA, state-level privacy laws, HIPAA if applicable, COPPA if applicable), absence of privacy-related incidents (breaches, unauthorised access, regulatory investigations), execution of required data-processing agreements, adequacy of data-security controls.

Consider the specific jurisdictional scope — the target's customers' geography drives the applicable privacy-law list. A SaaS target with EU customers is subject to GDPR regardless of the target's own incorporation. A target with California residents' data is subject to CCPA / CPRA.

Consider the specific breach-notification rep — has the target ever had a breach requiring notification? If yes, the disclosure schedule must catalog each.

### 9. Red-line the AI-model reps (layer 7 — part b)

AI-model reps are increasingly standard for technology targets with AI/ML capabilities. Redline (as applicable to your target):

- **Model ownership and rights.** Ownership of proprietary models, licensed rights to third-party foundation models (with license scope confirmation), rights to fine-tuning data.
- **Model training compliance.** Compliance with training-data-source terms, compliance with regulatory obligations (EU AI Act obligations for general-purpose models, transparency obligations).
- **Model performance.** Absence of undisclosed material model-performance issues, absence of undisclosed material bias / fairness issues, absence of undisclosed material safety issues.
- **Model deployment.** Compliance with regulatory obligations for high-risk AI systems (EU AI Act classification), documentation of model deployment (model cards, evaluation results).
- **Third-party model provider dependencies.** Disclosure of foundation-model provider relationships (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, others), terms-of-service compliance, dependency-continuity risk.

For an AI-first target, the AI-model rep may be the single most-negotiated rep given the current state of AI regulation and litigation.

### 10. Draft the disclosure-schedules discipline

The disclosure schedules are the actual risk-allocation artefact — the reps are general statements, and the schedules are where the seller discloses the specific exceptions. Draft a 1-page disclosure-schedules-discipline memo covering:

- **General disclosure-schedules-cross-reference clause.** Whether a disclosure on one schedule is deemed to be a disclosure against other reps to the extent it is "reasonably apparent" from the disclosure. This is a heavily-negotiated point — buyer-friendly draft rejects cross-reference; seller-friendly draft includes it.
- **Schedule updating.** Whether the seller can update the schedules between signing and closing, and if so, whether updates constitute breaches that trigger closing-condition failure.
- **Materiality threshold for disclosure.** Whether the seller must disclose every exception or only material exceptions.
- **Schedule delivery discipline.** Timing of schedule delivery (typically 5–10 days before signing), the review process, and the interaction with the definitive-agreement negotiation.

### 11. Draft the issues list

Write a 2–3 page issues list that a partner-level M&A lawyer would take into the next negotiation round. Cover:

- The 5–10 most-important open issues (rep by rep) with a specific position statement, a specific fallback, and a specific interaction with the indemnification package (chapter 6).
- The specific reps that are candidate "hold-firm" positions (from your seat) — reps you will not concede on without a specific compensating trade elsewhere.
- The specific reps that are candidate "trade-material" positions — reps you can concede on if the other side gives you something else in return.
- The specific reps that are candidate "closing-conversation" reps — reps you will defer resolution on until the final round.
- The disclosure-schedules-discipline positions and their interaction with the reps.
- The chapter-8 negotiation-canon frames to apply at each juncture.

## Starter guidance

Common rep-redline errors to avoid:

- **The scope-materiality double-negative.** Adding both a "material" qualifier and a knowledge qualifier to a rep that is already narrowly scoped. The seller ends up with an unbreachable rep; the buyer's counsel will red-line hard.
- **The disclosure-schedule under-population.** Failing to fully populate the disclosure schedules from the outset. The seller who under-populates the schedules is either (a) creating rep-breach exposure post-close, or (b) creating a rushed-signing pathology where the schedules balloon in the final week and the buyer's counsel has no time to review.
- **The cross-reference bomb.** Buyer-friendly drafts that reject cross-reference between schedules force the seller to duplicate disclosures. Seller-friendly drafts that permit cross-reference create ambiguity about what has actually been disclosed. Both sides need to think through the cross-reference discipline explicitly.
- **The knowledge-qualifier-scope trap.** Defining "knowledge" as the actual knowledge of a specific list of named individuals is narrower than defining "knowledge" as constructive knowledge after due inquiry. Sellers should push for actual-knowledge; buyers should push for constructive-knowledge-after-inquiry.
- **The over-negotiated rep.** Fighting for weeks on a rep that has small dollar exposure in the indemnification package. Chapter 8's Freund sequencing — pick the fights that matter.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- The negotiating seat is declared and the starting draft is characterised.
- The redline covers all seven layers with margin comments explaining each material change.
- Fundamental reps (layer 1) are redlined with specific attention to capitalisation and title.
- General reps (layer 2) are redlined with specific attention to financial statements, undisclosed liabilities, absence of changes, contracts (with material-contract definition), and litigation.
- Tax reps (layer 3) address state-and-local tax exposure and specific target-jurisdiction risks.
- Environmental reps (layer 4) are calibrated to the target's specific environmental risk profile.
- Compliance-with-law reps (layer 5) address sector-specific regulatory scope and AI-regulation carve-ins for an AI target.
- IP reps (layer 6) address ownership, non-infringement, employee IP assignment, OSS compliance, and (for an AI target) AI-training-data reps.
- Privacy reps (layer 7a) address jurisdictional scope and breach-notification history.
- AI-model reps (layer 7b) address model ownership, training compliance, performance, deployment, and foundation-model dependencies.
- Disclosure-schedules discipline is documented with specific positions on cross-reference, schedule updating, materiality thresholds, and schedule-delivery timing.
- Issues list is drafted with hold-firm, trade-material, and closing-conversation reps identified.

## Stretch goals

- **Deal-side swap.** After completing the redline from one seat, swap seats and redraft the other side's counter-response. This exercises both sides' thinking and often surfaces provisions you accepted too easily from the first seat.
- **Public-target overlay.** If your target is (or the transaction resembles) a public-target M&A, redraft with the additional Regulation M-A disclosures, the specific 10-Q / 10-K bring-down mechanics, and the public-company-specific-rep set (SEC filings, internal-controls, sub-certifications).
- **AI-model rep deep-dive.** For an AI-first target, expand the AI-model rep beyond the standard package to cover specific scenarios: (a) a model that was trained on data that turned out to be improperly licensed, (b) a model that produces biased outputs in specific demographic contexts, (c) a model that has a foundation-model-provider terms-of-service breach, (d) a model that requires ongoing fine-tuning that depends on continued access to third-party data.
- **Historical-transaction diagnostic.** Retrieve a specific publicly-filed SPA (SEC EDGAR — merger-vote proxies contain the SPA as an exhibit). Diagnose which of the seven layers is heaviest, which knowledge and materiality qualifiers are used, and how the disclosure schedules are structured. Compare against your redline.
- **Chapter-8 frame overlay.** Annotate each redline with the specific Freund / Harvard / Voss frame you would deploy to defend or negotiate the position. Prep for exercise 8.
