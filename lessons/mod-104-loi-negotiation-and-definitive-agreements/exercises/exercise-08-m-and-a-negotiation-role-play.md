# exercise-08: M&A negotiation role play

**Estimated effort:** 4–6 hours (best done in a single sitting; the specific role-play sessions run 60–90 minutes each with 30-minute debriefs between)

## Objective

Exercise the deal-scale negotiation canon (Freund, Fisher / Ury / Patton, Voss) through a two-sided role play covering three specific negotiation moments in a hypothetical M&A transaction: (1) the LOI negotiation between buyer-side and sell-side counsel, (2) a mid-diligence price-renegotiation moment initiated by the buyer, and (3) a definitive-agreement red-line stand-off on the MAC / MAE definition and the indemnification cap. Between and after the role-play sessions, debrief with structured attention to which negotiation frames were deployed, which succeeded, which failed, and where a different frame would have produced a better outcome.

The finished artefacts are: (a) role-play preparation memos for each side (buyer and seller) covering the three moments, (b) role-play conversation transcripts or notes covering the actual negotiation moves, (c) a structured debrief memo with specific chapter-8 frame analysis, and (d) a set of specific negotiation-craft observations you would carry into a real live M&A negotiation.

By the end you should be able to consciously pick and deploy a negotiation frame that fits the moment, recognise when the other side is deploying a specific frame against you, and hold the negotiating position under adversarial pressure without either capitulating or breaking the deal.

## Tasks

### 1. Set up the role-play mechanics

**1.1 — Roles.** Assign roles:

- **Seller-side team.** Founder-CEO, CFO, sell-side M&A counsel, sell-side banker (if the banker is at the table for the specific moment). One person can play multiple roles for a small session; three people is ideal.
- **Buyer-side team.** Corp-Dev VP (or PE deal partner), buyer-side M&A counsel, buyer-side deal team lead. Similar sizing.

If you have only one live partner: partner plays buyer, you play seller (for two moments), then swap for the third. If you have no live partner: use an LLM as the counterparty with explicit-seat instructions.

**1.2 — Rules of engagement.**

- Each moment runs 60–90 minutes.
- Each moment has a specific starting state (documents, prior negotiations, timing pressures) that is documented in the role-play preparation memo.
- The moment ends when either side calls it (typically at a point of resolution, deadlock, or planned adjournment).
- Between moments there is a 30-minute debrief.
- The final debrief covers all three moments together.

**1.3 — Recording discipline.**

- If in person, appoint a note-taker who is not a negotiator (or record audio if all parties consent).
- If remote, record the video session (with consent) for later debrief review.
- Track the specific negotiation moves — who raised what issue, who conceded on what, who deployed which frame.

### 2. Draft the seller-side role-play preparation memo

Write a 4–6 page seller-side preparation memo covering all three moments. For each moment:

- **Starting state.** Where is the transaction? What has been agreed? What is on the table for negotiation?
- **Seller-side objectives.** Specific outcomes the seller-side team is trying to achieve.
- **Seller-side BATNA.** What happens if this specific negotiation fails? Chapter 8's BATNA discipline.
- **Seller-side priority-and-hold-hard positions.** Which specific positions is the seller-side team willing to walk on? Which is trade-material?
- **Anticipated buyer-side positions.** What the seller-side team expects the buyer to argue.
- **Prepared calibrated questions.** Specific Voss-frame calibrated questions to deploy in the conversation.
- **Prepared objective-criteria references.** Specific ABA / SRS Acquiom / WSGR benchmarks and Delaware case law that support seller-side positions.
- **Prepared Freund-frame moves.** Specific sequencing decisions (which issues to raise first, which to hold for closing).
- **Emotional-preparation notes.** Specific labels or tactical-empathy moves that address anticipated buyer-side emotional dynamics.

### 3. Draft the buyer-side role-play preparation memo

Same structure as task 2 but from the buyer-side seat. Write a 4–6 page buyer-side preparation memo covering all three moments.

### 4. Run moment 1 — the LOI negotiation

**Starting state.** The buyer-side team has sent the seller-side team a first-draft LOI (based on exercise 2). The seller-side team has red-lined it (based on exercise 1). The parties are meeting to negotiate the remaining open issues. The specific open issues:

- Exclusivity length (buyer: 60 days; seller: 45 days).
- Fiduciary-out mechanics (buyer: narrow; seller: broad, with matching-rights compromise).
- Standstill duration (buyer: 24 months; seller: 12 months or NDA-standstill only).
- Expense-reimbursement ladder (buyer: full ladder with break-up fee; seller: expense-reimbursement only, no break-up fee for private-target).
- Retention-pool source (buyer: seller proceeds; seller: buyer's transaction budget).
- Any other open issues from exercises 1 and 2.

**Run the negotiation.** 60–90 minutes. Take notes on:

- Who deployed which frames (Freund sequencing, Harvard interests, Voss calibrated questions).
- Where the negotiation converged, where it deadlocked, where it advanced.
- Any specific moves that shifted the dynamic — successful calibrated questions, unsuccessful position-based demands, effective use of objective criteria.

**Debrief (30 minutes).** Walk through:

- Which specific frames each side deployed and when.
- Which frames succeeded and which failed.
- What surprised each side about the other side's approach.
- Where the interest-based middle ground was — did the parties find it?
- Which specific negotiation move each side would repeat in a real live LOI negotiation.

### 5. Run moment 2 — the mid-diligence price-renegotiation moment

**Starting state.** The LOI has been signed at a specific headline enterprise value. Exclusivity is in place. Buyer-side diligence is 60% complete and has surfaced two specific findings:

- **Finding 1 — customer concentration exposure.** The target's top-3 customer accounts, which represent 55% of ARR, are on annual contracts. One of the three has been signalling (through the diligence-workstream calls) that a competitor is bidding for their renewal. If the top customer is lost at renewal, the target's ARR drops by 15%.
- **Finding 2 — sales-tax nexus exposure.** The state-tax diligence has surfaced a specific SaaS sales-tax nexus exposure in a specific state (e.g., California) that the target has not been collecting. Estimated exposure: $4M in unpaid tax plus interest and penalties.

The buyer-side team is asking to reopen price. The specific ask: a $15M reduction from the LOI headline enterprise value ($10M for the customer-concentration finding, $5M for the tax exposure) — approximately 5% of transaction value.

The seller-side team has three options: (a) accept the re-trade, (b) reject the re-trade and force the buyer to walk (risking loss of the transaction plus the loss of second-place bidders who have moved on), (c) negotiate a specific middle-ground (partial price reduction, specific-indemnity carve-out, purchase-price-adjustment mechanic).

**Run the negotiation.** 60–90 minutes.

**Debrief (30 minutes).** Walk through:

- How did the seller-side team handle the buyer's re-trade? Did they push back on the specific dollar amount, negotiate a specific-indemnity structure, or accept?
- How did the buyer-side team frame the re-trade? Was it presented as diligence-driven-price-discovery (per Freund's buy-side sequencing) or as opportunistic re-trade?
- What role did BATNA discipline play? What did each side know (or bluff) about the other side's BATNA?
- What specific Voss-frame calibrated questions surfaced the other side's underlying interests?
- Where did the interest-based middle ground actually land?

### 6. Run moment 3 — the definitive-agreement red-line stand-off

**Starting state.** Definitive-agreement drafting is in round 3 of red-line. Two specific issues remain deadlocked:

- **MAC / MAE definition.** Buyer's position: broad MAC / MAE with narrow carve-outs and no quantitative threshold. Seller's position: narrow MAC / MAE with broad carve-outs (industry, pandemic, general economic, changes in law) and a specific quantitative threshold (25% EBITDA decline over 6 months). Reference exercise 5 for the specific fact-pattern positions.
- **Indemnification cap and sandbagging.** Buyer's position: 20% general-rep cap, tipping basket, pro-sandbagging. Seller's position: 10% general-rep cap, deductible basket, anti-sandbagging. Reference exercise 6.

The transaction has a specific signing target 3 weeks away. The buyer's team has indicated that if these issues are not resolved in the current round, they will need to escalate to the buyer's investment committee (or executive team) for a decision. The seller's team knows that escalation typically means the buyer walks unless they get their positions.

**Run the negotiation.** 60–90 minutes.

**Debrief (30 minutes).** Walk through:

- How did the parties handle the escalation threat? Was it treated as a "dying-deal" move (Freund frame), as a genuine walk-away signal (BATNA-based analysis), or as tactical pressure?
- What specific carve-outs, qualifiers, or trade-material provisions did each side propose to bridge the gap?
- Did either side deploy R&W insurance as a structural pivot to reshape the fight? Chapter 6/7 interaction.
- Where did the negotiation land? Which side gave more ground?
- What specific Voss-frame calibrated questions surfaced the buyer's underlying concerns on cap size and sandbagging? What labels defused emotional charge?

### 7. Cross-moment debrief

Write a 3–4 page cross-moment debrief covering:

- **Frame deployment across the three moments.** How did the specific frames deployed change between LOI, mid-diligence, and closing-conversation red-line? Which frames were most effective at each moment?
- **Anti-pattern observations.** Which of the chapter-8 anti-patterns showed up in your role play? (The one-shot concession, the reactive red-line, the personal-attack response, the relationship-first cave, the BATNA bluff, the dying-deal miscalibration, the frame-switching failure.)
- **Personal observations.** What specific negotiation move did *you* execute that you are proud of? What move did you execute that you would take back? What specific frame or tool did the *other side* use that impressed you and that you would incorporate into your own toolkit?
- **Chapter-8-canon-integration.** For each frame (Freund, Harvard, Voss), name one specific moment where deploying it changed the outcome — and one specific moment where you should have deployed it but did not.

### 8. Draft the negotiation-craft observations

Write a 1-page set of specific negotiation-craft observations you would carry into a real live M&A negotiation. Cover:

- Specific opening moves.
- Specific responses to hard-ball tactics.
- Specific calibrated questions that consistently produced information.
- Specific escalation-response patterns.
- Specific closing-conversation moves.

This is the artefact you would keep in your negotiation-prep folder for future deals.

## Starter guidance

Common role-play errors to avoid:

- **The over-scripted role play.** Reading from the preparation memo without engaging with what the other side is actually saying. Chapter 8's discipline is that the frames are tools, not scripts.
- **The under-preparation.** Skipping the preparation memos and running the role play as an improvisation. The frames are more effective when consciously deployed; conscious deployment requires preparation.
- **The zero-emotion role play.** Treating the role play as a purely intellectual exercise. Real M&A negotiations have emotional dynamics — founder-CEO frustration with buyer-side diligence intrusiveness, PE deal-partner impatience with seller-side counsel obstruction, mutual investment-committee anxiety about deal-closure timing. The role play should include these dynamics.
- **The debrief-lite.** Running the role play without the structured debrief. The learning is in the debrief, not in the negotiation itself.
- **The single-lens debrief.** Debriefing only through one frame (Freund, or Harvard, or Voss). Chapter 8's discipline is that the three frames are complementary; the debrief should cover all three.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- Role-play mechanics are set up with clear roles, rules, and recording discipline.
- Seller-side preparation memo is written at 4–6 page depth covering all three moments with objectives, BATNA, priority-and-hold-hard positions, anticipated buyer-side positions, prepared calibrated questions, prepared objective-criteria references, and prepared frame moves.
- Buyer-side preparation memo is written at 4–6 page depth with the same structure.
- Moment 1 (LOI negotiation) is run for 60–90 minutes with notes on frame deployment.
- Moment 2 (mid-diligence price re-negotiation) is run for 60–90 minutes with notes on frame deployment.
- Moment 3 (definitive-agreement red-line stand-off on MAC / MAE and indemnification cap) is run for 60–90 minutes with notes on frame deployment.
- Between-moment debriefs cover frame deployment and specific-move observations.
- Cross-moment debrief is written at 3–4 page depth covering frame deployment across moments, anti-pattern observations, personal observations, and chapter-8-canon integration.
- Negotiation-craft observations are written as a 1-page set of specific moves.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. Which of the three frames (Freund, Harvard, Voss) turned out to be most useful for you specifically? What does that say about your natural negotiation style?
2. Which of the three moments (LOI, mid-diligence, red-line stand-off) exposed the widest gap between how you *thought* you would negotiate and how you *actually* negotiated? What was the gap?
3. If you had to run the entire exercise again, which single specific preparation-step would you invest more time in?
4. What did the role play surface about your emotional response to hard-ball tactics? Where does that response help you, and where does it hurt you?
5. In a real live M&A negotiation of the transaction you have been carrying through the module, which of the three moments do you think will actually happen, and which are you now specifically prepared for?

## Stretch goals

- **Third-side observer.** Recruit a third participant to observe the role play (playing no role) and provide a specific-observation debrief. Fresh-eyes observation often catches frame-deployment patterns the negotiators miss.
- **Alternative-scenario extension.** Run a fourth moment — the closing-condition brinkmanship (chapter 5 / mod-105) where the buyer asserts MAC / MAE post-signing and the seller must decide whether to litigate, renegotiate, or walk. This is the highest-stakes M&A negotiation moment and is worth exercising if time permits.
- **Recorded-replay analysis.** Watch or listen to the recorded role play. Identify at least 3 specific moments where a different frame or move would have produced a better outcome. What prevented you from making that move in real time?
- **Frame-swap drill.** Rerun one of the three moments with a frame constraint — e.g., the seller-side must use only Voss-frame calibrated questions and mirroring; no Harvard-frame objective-criteria arguments and no Freund-frame sequencing. What does the constraint expose about the frame's specific strengths and limitations?
- **Cross-transaction generalisation.** Take the negotiation-craft observations from your 1-page set and apply them mentally to a different transaction (a startup fundraising negotiation, a customer-contract negotiation, a hire negotiation). Which observations generalise, and which are M&A-specific?
- **Study-partner drill.** If you have a study partner working through the module, swap seats and run the same three moments with the roles reversed. Debrief on how the same objective conditions produced different negotiation dynamics depending on the seat.
