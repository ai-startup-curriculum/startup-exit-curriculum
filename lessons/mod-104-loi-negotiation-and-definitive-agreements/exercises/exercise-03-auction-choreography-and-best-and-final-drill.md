# exercise-03: Auction choreography and best-and-final drill

**Estimated effort:** 3–4 hours

## Objective

Design the full auction choreography for a specific hypothetical sell-side process — from teaser distribution through best-and-final LOI selection — and then stress-test the choreography under three adversarial scenarios that a real process regularly encounters. The finished artefact is a process paper, an IOI-round evaluation memo, a best-and-final round process paper, a bidder-communication script for the moments of highest signalling risk, and a written debrief on what specifically survived the stress test.

By the end you should be able to defend the process-shape choice, the specific timing of each round, the information-parity discipline, and the specific choreography that maintains competitive tension into the best-and-final round — and to explain how you would recover if the process leaks or a bidder walks mid-process.

## Tasks

### 1. Select the process shape

Using chapter 3's four modal shapes (bilateral / limited-round auction / broad auction / dual-track), select the process shape for your transaction and defend the choice. Write a 1-page shape-selection memo covering:

- **Buyer-set depth and quality** — the shape must fit the number of credible bidders on your buyer map.
- **Information sensitivity** — customer-concentration exposure, competitor bidders, controversial technology, regulator-visible activity.
- **Market-window signals** — sector-specific multiple compression / expansion, macroeconomic environment, buyer-set specific-capacity signals (fund-deployment status for PE, recent M&A activity for strategics).
- **Target readiness** — data-room readiness, financial-audit status, management-team availability.
- **Seller preferences** — board-and-investor tolerance for process cost, timeline, confidentiality risk.
- **Fiduciary defensibility** — is a broader process required to defend the board's fiduciary posture (mod-109)?

The output is a defended choice — bilateral, limited-round (3–8 bidders), broad (10+ bidders), or dual-track.

### 2. Author the process paper (round-1 / IOI round)

Draft the round-1 process paper that goes to the invited bidder set with the CIM. The process paper is a 2–4 page document covering:

- **IOI due date and format** — specific date, page limit, delivery mechanism (secure email to banker, upload to data room).
- **Specific IOI questions** — draft the actual questions the IOI must answer:
  - Indicative price range (range, not point).
  - Form of consideration.
  - Sources of financing (with commitment-letter status for PE).
  - Assumptions the price is based on (specifically ask for the bidder's stated synergy assumptions for a strategic).
  - Key conditions and closing-certainty variables.
  - Bidder background and strategic rationale.
  - Any requested exclusivity (chapter 1 discipline — expect most bidders to defer this).
  - Anticipated regulatory risks and clearance timeline.
- **Process cadence forward** — approximate dates for the shortlist announcement, the management presentation window, the second-round diligence access, the best-and-final due date, the target-signing date.
- **Confidentiality obligations** — reference the executed NDA; explicit reminder about disclosure discipline.
- **Contact information and submission mechanics.**

### 3. Design the round-1 timing choreography

Draft the specific calendar for the IOI round:

- CIM distribution date.
- Bidder Q&A window (typical: 1–2 weeks after CIM distribution).
- IOI due date (typical: 2–4 weeks after CIM distribution).
- IOI evaluation and shortlist selection (typical: 5–10 business days after IOI due date).
- Shortlist notification (with a courteous decline to bidders not selected).

Defend each date against the specific transaction facts. Consider: are your invited bidders public strategics with quarterly-close constraints? PE bidders with investment-committee calendar constraints? Cross-border bidders with time-zone-and-holiday constraints? The calendar must accommodate these.

### 4. Author the IOI evaluation memo (worked example)

Assume you have received IOIs from 5 of your 8 invited bidders. Fabricate a plausible IOI-response set — a mix of strategics and financials, a mix of price ranges, a mix of consideration-mix proposals, a mix of financing-certainty positions. Write a 3-page IOI evaluation memo that:

- Scores each IOI on: indicative price, form of consideration, financing certainty, bidder profile, anticipated regulatory risk, anticipated conditions, anticipated timing.
- Ranks the IOIs on an overall-fit basis, noting the specific dimensions where each ranks high vs. low.
- Recommends the shortlist for the best-and-final round (typical: 3–4 for a limited-round process, 5–6 for a broad process, 1 for a bilateral setup).
- Applies the IOI-to-LOI compression discount (chapter 3 — typically 5–15%) when reading the headline numbers.
- Documents the rationale for including and excluding specific bidders.
- Notes any specific process-management concerns (leakage risk from a specific bidder, cultural-fit concerns, integration-appetite mismatch) that would inform how the shortlist is managed forward.

The memo is the artefact that the target's board would review before approving the shortlist.

### 5. Design the round-2 best-and-final round mechanics

Draft the round-2 process paper and calendar covering:

- **Management presentation window** — 2–4 hours per bidder, scheduled tightly to avoid drift. Information-parity discipline — all bidders receive the same presentation content. Specific management-presentation preparation checklist for the CEO / CFO / other key operating leaders.
- **Q&A round mechanics** — written questions submitted to the data room, answers posted to all bidders (or answered only to the submitting bidder if commercially sensitive — with the sensitive-question protocol documented).
- **Diligence access mechanics** — specific data-room permissioning for round-2 bidders (deeper access than round-1), specific expert-diligence sessions (technology deep dive, IP counsel session, HR / retention session, security / privacy / AI-model session — see mod-105 for the workstream mechanics).
- **Best-and-final process paper** — the round-2 process paper that goes to shortlisted bidders. Draft it explicitly. Cover: LOI due date, LOI format (2–5 pages minimum with specific enumerated items), the specific items the LOI must address (specific price, specific structure, specific consideration mix, financing confirmation, exclusivity request, definitive-agreement drafting-responsibility commitment, retention-pool sizing, regulatory-clearance strategy, closing-condition outline).
- **Timing** — window from round-1 shortlist announcement to LOI due date (typical: 4–8 weeks depending on process complexity).

### 6. Draft the bidder-communication scripts for high-signalling moments

Four moments in the auction carry outsized signalling risk. Draft scripts (2–4 sentences each) for handling each moment:

**6.1 — Bidder asks about competitive tension.** "How many other bidders are you talking to?" The wrong answer signals weakness (either too few bidders and you look like the auction is failing, or too many and you look desperate). Draft the specific script.

**6.2 — Bidder asks about specific competitors.** "Is [Named Competitor] in the process?" The wrong answer breaches confidentiality obligations to other bidders. Draft the specific script.

**6.3 — Bidder asks about price expectations.** "What price range are the other bids in?" The wrong answer either anchors the current bidder low (giving them a target to just-clear) or anchors them high (potentially chilling their bid). Draft the specific script.

**6.4 — Bidder asks about timing flexibility.** "Can we have an extra 2 weeks on diligence?" A yes signals process is soft; a no signals process is inflexible. Draft the specific script.

Then draft the target-management-team briefing — what the CEO, CFO, and other management-presentation participants should and should not say when they get any of these questions off-script.

### 7. Stress-test — three scenarios

Work through each scenario as a written response. Each response covers: what happened, what the process-management move is, what the board-communication is, what the recovery path is.

**Scenario A — Process leakage during IOI round.** A trade-press article surfaces during the IOI round citing an "unnamed source close to the target" and naming the target as being in play. Customers, employees, and one specific top-3 competitor of the target reach out with questions. One of your invited bidders emails the banker asking whether the article changes anything.

**Scenario B — Best-and-final bidder walks 48 hours before LOI due.** Your top-ranked round-1 bidder — a public strategic with a compelling headline number and solid financing certainty — emails the banker 48 hours before the LOI due date to say they will not be submitting a bid. They cite "internal capital-allocation priorities." Your remaining shortlisted bidders are two PE bidders and one weaker strategic. The board is expecting the top-ranked bidder's LOI.

**Scenario C — Superior-Proposal-shape signal from an uninvited bidder.** During the round-2 diligence window, an uninvited bidder — a strategic that was on your original buyer map but not invited to the process because of a specific concern (competitive overlap, past-relationship damage) — reaches out directly to the CEO to express interest at a price meaningfully above your round-1 IOI midpoint. They do not know the details of your process. Your existing exclusivity discipline (per chapter 3) is not yet in place — no LOI has been signed.

For each scenario, the response should demonstrate: awareness of the specific tension between bidder-management, board fiduciary posture (mod-109), and process-integrity; specific script or action; documented rationale.

### 8. Design the information-parity discipline

Draft a 1-page information-parity policy that covers:

- What information all round-1 bidders receive (CIM, process paper, Q&A responses).
- What information all round-2 shortlisted bidders receive (management presentation, deeper data-room access, written Q&A responses).
- What information is bidder-specific vs. bidder-common (bidder-specific: any diligence expert session that goes deep on a bidder-specific topic, e.g., a technical integration diligence that only a specific strategic would care about).
- How commercially-sensitive questions are handled (answered only to the submitting bidder, or answered to all, or redirected to the banker).
- The documentation discipline for the banker to prove information-parity if later challenged.
- The specific hazard: an ex-post fiduciary-duty challenge alleging that one bidder received better information than the others.

### 9. Debrief

Write a 1-page debrief covering:

- Which of the process-management design decisions is the one you are least confident about?
- Which of the three stress-test scenarios felt most likely to happen in your specific transaction, and why?
- What is the single specific process-management move that a first-time seller would most likely get wrong in your scenario A, B, or C response?
- How does your process-shape choice interact with the fiduciary framework (mod-109)? Specifically, is your process-shape choice defensible against a stockholder challenge asserting that a different process would have produced a materially higher price?

## Starter guidance

Common auction-management errors to avoid:

- **The over-inviting round-1.** Inviting too many bidders in an attempt to maximise competitive tension. The result is diluted management-team attention, elevated leakage risk, and reduced-quality individual-bidder relationships. Chapter 3's limited-round default (3–8 bidders) exists for a reason.
- **The under-differentiated IOI evaluation.** Ranking IOIs primarily on headline price without adjusting for financing certainty, regulatory risk, and closing-certainty variables. A $500M IOI from a bidder with a financing condition and CFIUS-triggering ownership is not equivalent to a $475M IOI from a bidder with cash-on-balance-sheet and no regulatory friction.
- **The information-parity slip.** Answering a commercially-sensitive question for one bidder that is not answered for the others. Even if the slip is minor, it can be later cited as evidence of process-integrity failure.
- **The management-presentation drift.** Letting the CEO or CFO answer price questions in the management presentation. Chapter 3's discipline is that price is negotiated through the banker, not through the management-presentation window. Coach the management team specifically on this.
- **The bad-news-slow disclosure.** Waiting to disclose to the board that a top-ranked bidder has walked, or that the process has leaked. Chapter 3's implicit discipline — surface bad news fast, give the board time to adjust.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- Process-shape choice is defended in a 1-page memo referencing the six shape-selection variables.
- Round-1 process paper is drafted with all elements in the chapter-3 list.
- Round-1 calendar accommodates specific transaction facts (bidder-type calendar constraints, cross-border considerations).
- IOI evaluation memo is drafted on a plausible fabricated IOI-response set with quantitative scoring and defended shortlist recommendation.
- Round-2 process paper and calendar are drafted with all elements.
- Bidder-communication scripts are drafted for all four high-signalling moments.
- Three stress-test scenarios each have a written response with specific process-management move, board-communication, and recovery path.
- Information-parity policy is drafted at 1-page depth.
- Debrief covers the four required reflection points.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. If you had to run the process with 2 fewer bidders than your shortlist, which specific 2 would you cut and why? What does that tell you about the bidders you value most?
2. In your stress-test scenario B (top bidder walks), how much of the recovery depended on having a well-managed round-2 shortlist vs. having a strong banker vs. having a specific relationship? Which is the single most-important asset?
3. What is the single choreography decision (round timing, information distribution, bidder-communication script) that you think would matter most for the final headline price of the transaction?

## Stretch goals

- **Dual-track overlay.** If your process shape is dual-track (M&A + IPO), design the specific coordination choreography between the M&A auction and the IPO preparation — specifically, the S-1-drafting timing, the DRS submission timing, the roadshow timing, and the specific dual-track decision-point calendar.
- **Broad-auction overlay.** If your process shape is broad (10+ bidders), design the multi-round diligence-access mechanism — how do you handle 15 bidders wanting expert-diligence sessions? Prioritisation, batching, staggered access.
- **PE-buyer-heavy overlay.** If your buyer map is PE-buyer-heavy, add the specific PE-buyer-management overlays — the sponsor's investment-committee calendar, the specific diligence workstreams PE bidders run (LBO model validation, management-continuity assessment, exit-thesis stress-test), the specific closing-certainty variables (equity commitment letter status, debt commitment status).
- **Regulator-visible-transaction overlay.** If your transaction is likely to attract regulator attention (horizontal antitrust, CFIUS-triggering ownership), design the pre-filing preparation timeline and the process choreography that runs in parallel to the auction.
- **Mock-signalling drill.** With a partner (or a debate-partner AI), role-play the four high-signalling-moment scripts as live conversations. Record the responses. Debrief on which scripts held under adversarial pressure and which did not.
