# Exclusivity, No-Shop, Standstill, and the Break-Up-Fee Ladder

## Why this matters

The exclusivity / no-shop / standstill / expense-reimbursement / break-up-fee package is where the sell-side and buy-side leverage positions swap places. Before LOI signing, the target has multiple bidders, information asymmetry favours the target's banker, and the buyer is competing for the opportunity to underwrite the deal. After LOI signing, one bidder has been chosen, the target contractually cannot talk to the others, information asymmetry starts to favour the buyer (who now owns the diligence process), and the target's negotiating leverage begins to depend on the credibility of the threat to walk away. Every provision in this package regulates that leverage swap.

Most first-time sellers underweight this package. They read the exclusivity provision as procedural boilerplate and focus their negotiation on price. That is a mistake. The exclusivity mechanics — the length of the period, the automatic extensions, the fiduciary-out carve-out, the standstill scope — determine whether the target's board still has room to run a real process if the buyer starts re-trading price in week 6 of a 60-day exclusivity window. The break-up-fee ladder — expense reimbursement, break-up fee, reverse termination fee — determines what the target owes the buyer if the target walks and what the buyer owes the target if the buyer walks. These provisions collectively decide how much protection each side has against the other's bad-faith conduct after LOI signing.

This chapter installs the mechanics: the exclusivity / no-shop provision and its fiduciary-out carve-out; the standstill provision and its acquirer-defence purpose; the expense-reimbursement / break-up-fee ladder benchmarked against ABA Deal Points Study and WSGR practitioner data; matching rights; and the specific interactions with fiduciary duty (developed in mod-109) that shape what the target's counsel *must* insist on in a well-run process.

## Exclusivity — the mechanics

An exclusivity provision (also called a "no-shop" provision or an "exclusive negotiation" provision) binds the target — and typically the target's directors, officers, financial advisors, and other representatives — not to solicit, initiate, encourage, engage in, or continue any discussions with any third party regarding an acquisition of the target during the exclusivity period. Standard drafting reads approximately:

> "During the Exclusivity Period, Target and its Representatives shall not, directly or indirectly, (a) solicit, initiate, encourage, facilitate, or engage in any discussions or negotiations with any Person other than Buyer regarding any Alternative Transaction; (b) furnish any non-public information regarding Target to any Person other than Buyer or its Representatives in connection with any Alternative Transaction; (c) enter into any letter of intent, term sheet, memorandum of understanding, or definitive agreement with any Person other than Buyer regarding any Alternative Transaction; or (d) provide access to any physical or electronic data room to any Person other than Buyer or its Representatives."

Two provisions define the mechanic: what counts as an "Alternative Transaction," and what the "Exclusivity Period" is.

### What counts as an "Alternative Transaction"

The definition of Alternative Transaction (also called a "Competing Proposal" or "Acquisition Proposal") determines the scope of the target's no-shop obligation. A well-drafted definition covers:

- Any acquisition of the target — stock, asset, merger, tender offer, exchange offer, business combination.
- Any acquisition of a *material* portion of the target's assets or equity (typically defined as 15% or more, sometimes 20%; the specific threshold is negotiated).
- Any financing transaction that would materially alter the target's capital structure (equity issuance above a threshold, refinancing of debt above a threshold).
- Any joint venture, strategic partnership, or license agreement that would materially transfer the target's business.

The target's counsel will typically push to narrow the definition — for example, to exclude ordinary-course financing transactions, ordinary-course commercial licenses, and small-scale acqui-hire discussions where the target is the *acquirer*. The buyer's counsel will typically push to broaden it — for example, to include any transaction that could impair the buyer's exclusivity value.

The threshold matters. If the Alternative Transaction definition covers "any acquisition of any portion of Target's equity or assets," then the target is prevented from doing any transaction of any size — an ordinary-course convertible-note bridge financing at 3% of the cap table is technically prohibited. If the definition covers "any acquisition of 15% or more of Target's equity or a material portion of Target's assets," then the target retains flexibility for ordinary-course activity. The 15% threshold aligns roughly with the "poison-pill trigger" concept in public-company practice and is a defensible compromise.

### The Exclusivity Period

A typical exclusivity period runs 30 to 60 days from LOI signing. The specific length is driven by:

- **Definitive-agreement drafting timeline** — 30 days for a straightforward transaction; 45–60 for a more complex one; 60+ for transactions with material regulatory-filing complexity or lengthy diligence.
- **Diligence workstream depth** — a strategic buyer doing full-scope legal, financial, tax, commercial, technology, IP, HR, privacy, security, and AI-model diligence needs longer than a financial buyer doing a lighter workstream package.
- **R&W insurance underwriting timeline** — a buyer-side R&W policy typically requires 3–5 weeks of underwriting time from insurer engagement to policy binding; a bound policy is usually a closing precedent (see chapter 7).
- **Regulatory-filing timeline** — if HSR is filed at signing (rather than pre-signing), the exclusivity period only needs to run to signing; if the parties want HSR clearance before signing (less common), exclusivity needs to accommodate the full HSR waiting period.

The ABA Private Target M&A Deal Points Study — the practitioner-standard empirical reference on private-target M&A terms — publishes distributions of exclusivity length across the transaction sample; the modal length across recent cycles is approximately 45 days.

The target's negotiating asks on the exclusivity-period length:

- **Shorter period.** The shorter the exclusivity, the less time the target is tied to a single bidder and the sooner the target can restart the process if diligence goes poorly. Target's counsel typically pushes for 30–45 days as the primary period.
- **Extension only by mutual agreement, or unilaterally by the target.** The extension clause should not let the buyer unilaterally extend the exclusivity — that would let the buyer keep the target locked up indefinitely while dragging its feet. Standard drafting: extensions require mutual written agreement, and the target may unilaterally allow the period to lapse.
- **Automatic termination if the buyer materially breaches the LOI.** If the buyer stops prosecuting the deal, materially reduces the offered price, or otherwise materially breaches, the target should be free to walk. Language: "The Exclusivity Period shall automatically terminate upon Buyer's material breach of this Letter of Intent, including any material reduction in the proposed Purchase Price."
- **Fiduciary-out carve-out (below).**

The buyer's negotiating asks:

- **Longer period.** More time to complete diligence and draft the definitive agreement without competing bidders in the picture.
- **Automatic extension if regulatory filings are pending.** If the buyer is required to file HSR and the waiting period runs beyond the exclusivity date, the buyer wants automatic extension.
- **Extension by buyer's unilateral notice.** Aggressive buyers propose unilateral-extension language; sellers should reject.

## The fiduciary-out carve-out

For a target whose board owes Delaware fiduciary duties in a change-of-control context — which is *every* Delaware corporation with directors under the standard fiduciary framework — the board's exclusivity commitment cannot lawfully contradict its fiduciary obligation to consider unsolicited superior proposals in good faith. The specific application depends on whether the target is public or private and on the specific fiduciary-duty framework that applies.

### Revlon and the public-target context

For a public-target board that has affirmatively decided to sell control of the company, the *Revlon* line of Delaware cases imposes an obligation to obtain "the best price reasonably available" for stockholders. A blanket no-shop that prevents the board from engaging with an unsolicited superior proposal violates the *Revlon* duty. The response: **a fiduciary-out carve-out** that allows the board to consider, and if appropriate accept, an unsolicited superior proposal — subject to specific procedural protections for the initial bidder (typically a matching right, below).

Standard drafting of a Revlon-compliant fiduciary-out reads approximately:

> "Notwithstanding [Section X: No-Shop], if Target's Board of Directors, after consultation with its outside legal counsel and financial advisor, determines in good faith that (a) it has received an unsolicited written Acquisition Proposal that would reasonably be expected to result in a Superior Proposal, and (b) failure to engage in discussions or negotiations with such Person, or to furnish non-public information to such Person, would be inconsistent with the directors' fiduciary duties under applicable law, Target and its Representatives may (i) engage in discussions or negotiations with such Person, and (ii) furnish non-public information to such Person pursuant to a confidentiality agreement with terms no less favourable to Target than those in [the confidentiality agreement with Buyer]."

The definition of "Superior Proposal" is negotiated and typically includes both a price threshold (materially higher price) and a certainty threshold (comparable or better closing certainty, including financing and regulatory certainty). Buyer's counsel typically pushes for a high price threshold (e.g., "materially higher") and a strict certainty threshold; seller's counsel pushes for a lower threshold and more flexibility.

### The private-target context

For a private-target board, *Revlon* does not directly apply — *Revlon* was a public-company case, and private-company boards owe fiduciary duties primarily to the corporation and its stockholders under the general fiduciary framework rather than under the *Revlon* enhanced-scrutiny standard. But private-target boards still owe fiduciary duties, and the practitioner discipline is to include a fiduciary-out carve-out even in private-target LOIs to protect the board against a stockholder challenge if an unsolicited superior proposal emerges during exclusivity and the board is legally prevented from considering it.

The private-target fiduciary-out is typically narrower than the public-target one — it may require the board to consider only "materially superior" proposals with a heightened threshold — but its presence is a defensive protection for the board rather than a substantive erosion of exclusivity.

### The "no-fiduciary-out" LOI and its risk

Aggressive buyer's counsel will sometimes propose an LOI without a fiduciary-out carve-out. The target's counsel *must* insist on the carve-out. An LOI without a fiduciary-out puts the target's board in the position of choosing between honouring the LOI's exclusivity (which arguably breaches fiduciary duty) and honouring the fiduciary duty (which breaches the LOI). The buyer, of course, would rather the target's board have no choice. But the target's board's counsel, protecting the board's fiduciary posture, cannot allow this.

The fiduciary-out carve-out is one of the very small number of provisions where the target's counsel *cannot* concede. mod-109 develops the fiduciary framework at depth.

## The matching right

If the fiduciary-out allows the target to consider a superior proposal, the buyer typically insists on a **matching right** — a contractual right to be notified of any superior proposal and to submit a matching or better proposal within a defined period (typically 3–5 business days) before the target can terminate the LOI to accept the superior proposal.

Standard drafting:

> "Before Target enters into any definitive agreement in respect of a Superior Proposal, Target shall (a) notify Buyer in writing of the identity of the Person making the Superior Proposal and the material terms of the Superior Proposal, (b) provide Buyer with a copy of the definitive agreement being proposed with respect to the Superior Proposal, and (c) allow Buyer a period of not less than [three / four / five] Business Days to submit a revised proposal that is at least as favourable to Target as the Superior Proposal. If Buyer submits such a revised proposal, Target's Board of Directors shall consider the revised proposal in good faith and, if the Board determines the revised proposal is at least as favourable as the Superior Proposal, Target shall continue to be bound by [Section X: No-Shop]."

Matching rights add friction to the target's ability to accept a superior proposal — the target must formally notify the buyer, share the terms, and wait 3–5 days for a response. In practice, matching rights are meaningful because a rational third-party bidder anticipates the matching mechanic and prices their proposal to overcome the friction — a proposal that is only marginally better than the current LOI will typically be matched, so a serious third-party bidder must propose materially better terms to disrupt the deal. Matching rights are standard in public-target M&A and common in private-target M&A where the initial buyer has substantial negotiating leverage.

## The standstill provision

Standstill provisions restrict a party (typically the buyer) from taking specified hostile or unsolicited actions with respect to the target for a defined period. Standstills exist in two contexts in the LOI-negotiation workflow: the **pre-LOI standstill** in the NDA that the buyer signed at CIM delivery, and the **LOI-embedded standstill** that continues or modifies the NDA standstill.

The pre-LOI standstill (from the NDA) typically prohibits the buyer, during the standstill period, from:

- Acquiring any securities of the target (other than through the contemplated transaction).
- Making or supporting any tender offer, exchange offer, merger, or business combination proposal.
- Soliciting proxies or consents from the target's stockholders.
- Forming or joining a "group" (Rule 13d-5) with respect to the target's securities.
- Requesting the target to waive or amend the standstill.
- Otherwise engaging in any unsolicited activity that would compete with, or attempt to gain control of, the target.

The specific list of prohibited actions and the specific carve-outs (for the contemplated transaction, for ordinary-course investment activity below a threshold, for actions permitted by law) vary widely.

### Standstill duration

Standstill durations in ABA and SRS Acquiom data typically range from 12 to 24 months from NDA signing (or from LOI signing where the LOI extends the NDA standstill). The buyer typically wants shorter; the target wants longer. In practice:

- **Shorter standstill (12 months or less)** — for a friendly, cooperative process with a target that anticipates the possibility of a future relationship with the bidder even if this deal does not close.
- **Longer standstill (18–24 months or more)** — for a competitive process where the target's board wants extended protection against the bidder pivoting to a hostile posture if the friendly negotiation fails.

### Standstill purpose — acquirer defence

The standstill's primary purpose is *acquirer defence*: it prevents a bidder that participates in a friendly sale process, gains access to the target's non-public information, and then loses (or walks away from) the negotiation from turning around and using the information advantage to launch a hostile bid or to accumulate a toehold position in the target's securities. Without the standstill, the target has no protection against a "look inside and then go hostile" pattern.

For a public target, the standstill is essential — the buyer's access to non-public information during the process would otherwise give the buyer an insider-trading exposure or a proxy-contest launching pad. For a private target, the standstill is still valuable but less mechanical — the private target's securities are not publicly traded and the "hostile bid" concept applies differently.

### The "fall-away" trigger

Standstills typically contain **fall-away** provisions that terminate the standstill on specified events — for example:

- The target enters into a definitive agreement with a third party for an Acquisition Transaction.
- The target's Board of Directors publicly recommends acceptance of a third-party tender offer.
- A third party acquires more than a specified percentage of the target's outstanding equity through a tender offer or open-market accumulation.

Fall-away provisions let the buyer respond to a third-party transaction proposal without waiting for the standstill to expire — a legitimate protection for the buyer that has invested in the process. But they also expose the target: an overly-broad fall-away trigger can be interpreted as freeing the buyer to launch a hostile bid the moment the target considers any third-party proposal, defeating the standstill's protective purpose. Well-drafted fall-away triggers specify that the standstill terminates only on a *consummation* or *definitive-agreement* event, not on the mere emergence of a competing proposal.

## The break-up-fee ladder

If the parties agree that walking away should have a monetary consequence, the LOI (or the definitive agreement) includes some combination of expense reimbursement, break-up fee, and reverse termination fee. These sit on a ladder from smallest to largest:

### Expense reimbursement

Expense reimbursement covers the *out-of-pocket transaction expenses* of the non-terminating party — legal fees, financial-advisor fees, accountant fees, filing fees, R&W insurance premium, and similar direct costs. Expense reimbursement is typically capped at a specific dollar amount (a few hundred thousand dollars to a few million for a mid-market transaction) and is triggered by specific events (target termination of the LOI or definitive agreement other than for the buyer's material breach; target acceptance of a superior proposal; target board withdrawal of recommendation).

Expense reimbursement is common in private-target LOIs as the *minimum* deterrent against the target using the buyer's diligence investment as free due-diligence for a subsequent deal. A well-drafted expense-reimbursement provision:

- Names the reimbursable expense categories.
- Caps the reimbursement at a specific dollar amount.
- Names the triggering events (target termination in specified circumstances).
- Excludes the buyer's *internal* costs (buyer's own personnel time; buyer's own overhead).
- Requires reasonable documentation of the claimed expenses.

### Break-up fee

A break-up fee (sometimes called a "termination fee") is a larger, defined dollar amount payable by one party to the other on specified termination events. In public-target M&A, break-up fees are typically 2–4% of transaction value; in private-target M&A, they are less common and typically smaller (0.5–2% of transaction value where present). The break-up-fee provision in a private-target LOI is usually payable by the target only, in favour of the buyer, on target acceptance of a superior proposal — the fee compensates the buyer for the diligence-and-drafting investment lost when the target pivots to a competing bidder.

Break-up-fee size is negotiated against the *chilling effect* concern: a break-up fee that is too large can chill competing bidders (a third-party bidder that must pay a break-up fee out of the transaction proceeds effectively bids for the target *plus* the fee, which raises the third-party bidder's effective cost). Delaware courts have addressed this in the public-target context — most notably in the *Brazen v. Bell Atlantic* line — and the practitioner consensus is that break-up fees of approximately 3% of equity value are within the range of reasonableness for public targets. Higher fees may face fiduciary-duty challenge.

### Reverse termination fee

A reverse termination fee (RTF) is payable by the *buyer* to the *target* on specified termination events — typically buyer's failure to obtain regulatory clearance (an antitrust-block RTF), buyer's failure to obtain financing (a financing-failure RTF), or buyer's breach of the definitive agreement. RTFs are common in transactions where the buyer is a financial sponsor (PE fund) whose ability to close depends on debt financing that may not materialise, or in transactions where the antitrust risk is material (large horizontal transactions, transactions with foreign-antitrust exposure).

RTFs are typically larger than break-up fees — commonly 4–8% of transaction value, sometimes higher. The RTF's purpose is to price the buyer's closing risk; a buyer that will not agree to a meaningful RTF is signalling either that closing risk is low or that the buyer does not want to underwrite it, and either interpretation should be tested in negotiation.

## Practitioner benchmarks

The ABA Private Target M&A Deal Points Study and the SRS Acquiom annual deal-terms study together provide the empirical baseline for exclusivity, standstill, and break-up-fee terms across US private-target M&A. Both studies are published on multi-year cycles (ABA typically biennially; SRS Acquiom annually) and are freely available to practitioners.

Recent-cycle representative distributions (verify current cycle before citing to a live matter):

- **Exclusivity period.** Modal length ~45 days; the middle 50% typically falls in the 30–60 day range.
- **Standstill period.** Modal length ~18–24 months from NDA / LOI signing; range from 12 to 36 months.
- **Fiduciary-out carve-out.** Nearly universal in public-target LOIs; increasingly common in private-target LOIs.
- **Matching rights.** Universal in public-target M&A; common in private-target M&A where the initial buyer has substantial leverage.
- **Expense-reimbursement caps.** Vary widely by transaction size; typically 0.5–1.5% of equity value.
- **Break-up fees.** Median ~3% of equity value in public-target M&A; lower and less common in private-target M&A.
- **Reverse termination fees.** Median ~5–7% of equity value in transactions with material antitrust or financing risk; smaller or absent where risk is low.

## Common negotiation moves

Some patterns worth naming:

- **Buyer proposes 60-day exclusivity; seller counters with 30 days + automatic 15-day extension upon buyer's material progress on diligence.** This is a common compromise that gives the buyer time to complete diligence but conditions the extension on the buyer prosecuting the deal actively.
- **Buyer proposes no fiduciary-out; seller counters with a standard fiduciary-out carve-out and a matching right.** Non-negotiable for the seller's board; the buyer will typically accept in exchange for the matching right.
- **Buyer proposes a 24-month standstill; seller counters with 12 months and a fall-away trigger on target's public announcement of intent to be sold.** The compromise is a shorter standstill with a specific fall-away that protects the buyer against the target using the standstill to lock the buyer out of a future process.
- **Buyer proposes a large expense-reimbursement cap ($2M on a $150M transaction); seller counters with a smaller cap and a tighter definition of reimbursable expenses.** Expense reimbursement is negotiated as a cap and as a category; both matter.
- **Seller proposes a break-up fee that would be payable by both parties symmetrically; buyer counters with an asymmetric break-up fee that is payable only by the target.** Symmetric break-up fees are unusual in private-target M&A; buyer typically insists on asymmetric.
- **Financial-sponsor buyer proposes a $10M cap on the reverse termination fee for a $200M transaction; seller counters with a $25M cap.** Both anchors are defensible; the negotiation resolves around the specific antitrust and financing risk assessments.

## Common LOI-drafting mistakes on this package

- **No definition of Alternative Transaction.** A no-shop that prohibits "any transaction" without a defined Alternative Transaction is either overbroad (prohibits ordinary-course activity) or underspecified (leaves the buyer with a claim on any transaction the target does, however small).
- **Buyer's unilateral extension of the Exclusivity Period.** Leaves the target locked up indefinitely. Extension should require mutual agreement or unilateral election by the target.
- **No fiduciary-out.** Puts the board in a fiduciary bind; non-negotiable for the target's counsel.
- **Standstill without a fall-away trigger.** Leaves the buyer unable to respond to a competing definitive-agreement transaction even after the target has publicly recommended it.
- **Break-up fee that is not tied to specific triggering events.** A break-up fee that is triggered by "any termination" gives the buyer a free option to walk while collecting the fee; the trigger should be tightly defined.
- **Expense reimbursement without a cap.** Uncapped expense reimbursement is a blank check; always cap.
- **No specification of the mechanism for the fiduciary-out review.** The target's counsel should specify that the Board consults with outside legal counsel and financial advisor before exercising the fiduciary-out, so that any subsequent challenge can point to a documented process.

## Interaction with fiduciary duty (mod-109 preview)

The exclusivity / no-shop / standstill / break-up-fee package sits inside the Delaware fiduciary-duty framework that mod-109 develops at depth. Two touchpoints worth naming here:

**Revlon and the change-of-control decision.** Once a public-target board has affirmatively decided to sell control (either by seeking an acquirer, or by responding to an unsolicited proposal in a way that constitutes a "sale-of-control" decision), *Revlon* imposes an obligation to obtain the best price reasonably available. The exclusivity / no-shop / fiduciary-out package must be drafted to comply with *Revlon* — meaning the fiduciary-out carve-out must be meaningful, the matching right must not chill superior proposals, and the break-up fee must not deter competing bidders.

**The Corwin gate.** For a change-of-control transaction that is approved by a fully-informed, uncoerced vote of the disinterested stockholders (following the Corwin doctrine), the transaction is reviewed under the business-judgement standard rather than under enhanced scrutiny — dramatically reducing litigation risk. The exclusivity / no-shop package must not impair the ability of the stockholders to receive full information and make an uncoerced vote.

mod-109 develops these frameworks at depth. For this module's purposes, the drafting discipline is: the exclusivity / no-shop / fiduciary-out / matching-right / break-up-fee package must be drafted to be *defensible* under the fiduciary framework, which means the fiduciary-out carve-out and the matching-right mechanics must be substantive rather than merely token, and the break-up fee must not be so large as to deter competing bidders.

## Summary

The exclusivity / no-shop / standstill / expense-reimbursement / break-up-fee package regulates the leverage swap that happens at LOI signing. A standard exclusivity period of 30–60 days, with a fiduciary-out carve-out for the target's board, a matching right for the buyer, and expense-reimbursement or break-up-fee protection for the buyer against a target that walks. A standstill of 12–24 months protects the target against a bidder that gains access to non-public information and then goes hostile. Break-up-fee sizing (0.5–3% of equity value for private targets; 2–4% for public targets) is negotiated against the chilling-effect concern. Every provision in the package is negotiated against ABA / SRS Acquiom empirical benchmarks and against the fiduciary-duty framework mod-109 develops. The target's counsel's non-negotiable is the fiduciary-out carve-out; the buyer's non-negotiable is meaningful exclusivity with adequate protection against the target walking.
