# Deal-Structure Form Selection — Asset, Stock, Merger Variants, Reverse Morris Trust

## Why this matters

The first substantive fight after an LOI is signed is almost always about *form*. The seller's counsel says "we want a stock deal with §368 tax-free treatment on the stock portion"; the buyer's tax counsel says "we want an asset deal with a §338(h)(10) election"; the seller's founders point out that an asset deal breaks their §1202 QSBS treatment and destroys $10M+ of tax-free-gain per founder; the buyer's M&A counsel points out that a stock deal drags all of the target's undisclosed liabilities into the acquiring entity and that the R&W insurer has flagged three of them; the acquirer's board sub-committee says the CFO's tax memo requires a step-up in asset basis to keep the acquisition accretive; the seller's minority preferred investor points out that a straight statutory merger triggers DGCL §262 appraisal rights that could stretch closing by six months. Every one of those objections is grounded in something real. None of them can be resolved by picking a form off a list; the form has to be chosen against the specific facts, and the choice cascades into every other structural decision in the transaction.

This chapter installs the six modal corporate forms an M&A transaction can take, the buyer-vs-seller tension at first pass on each, and the fact patterns that push a specific deal toward one form over the others. The tax mechanics (§368 tax-free reorg types, §338(h)(10) election, §1202 QSBS preservation) get their own chapters — this chapter names the corporate forms and their non-tax consequences; chapters 5 and 8 return to the tax layer once the corporate-form vocabulary is in place.

## The six modal forms

Any M&A transaction of a US target — regardless of whether the acquirer is US, a US subsidiary of a foreign parent, or a foreign entity — will ultimately be structured as one of six forms. The forms are not interchangeable; each has a distinct corporate-law footprint under the target's state of incorporation (Delaware for most venture-backed companies), a distinct treatment of the target's assets and liabilities, a distinct set of consents and votes required, and a distinct set of tax and regulatory downstream consequences.

### 1. Asset purchase

The buyer forms (or uses) an acquiring entity and purchases specified assets from the target — a defined asset list, sometimes with an assumed-liabilities list. The selling entity (the target) survives as a corporate shell after closing with the sale proceeds as its only material asset; it then either distributes those proceeds to its shareholders and dissolves, or continues to exist as a wind-down vehicle for retained liabilities.

- **Assets:** only the specified assets transfer. Contracts require third-party consent to assign (subject to the target's contract-by-contract "anti-assignment" clauses). Regulatory permits, licenses, and rights may require re-issuance to the acquirer.
- **Liabilities:** only assumed liabilities transfer. Non-assumed liabilities stay with the target. This is the buyer-preferred feature: the acquirer takes the assets clean, and undisclosed liabilities (product liability, tax exposure, employment claims, IP infringement) that were not scheduled remain with the shell.
- **Corporate approvals:** target's board approves; target's shareholders approve if the sale is of "all or substantially all" of the target's assets (DGCL §271 in Delaware; comparable provisions in other states).
- **Tax at target level:** taxable transaction — the target recognises gain or loss on the sale of its assets. For a C-corp target, this creates the classic *two-level tax* problem: the target pays corporate tax on the asset-sale gain, and shareholders then pay dividend / capital-gains tax on the distribution of after-tax proceeds. For an S-corp or partnership, the tax passes through to the owners and the two-level problem does not arise (this is why asset deals are common for S-corp targets).
- **Tax at buyer level:** the buyer takes a *step-up* in the tax basis of the acquired assets to the purchase price — future depreciation and amortization deductions run against the stepped-up basis. This is the buyer-preferred feature that motivates the asset-deal preference.
- **§1202 QSBS impact:** an asset sale does *not* preserve QSBS treatment for the target's shareholders on the underlying company sale — the founders and early employees who held qualified stock lose the opportunity to sell their qualifying stock for §1202 treatment in this transaction. This is a very large seller-side objection when the target has substantial §1202-eligible stock outstanding.

**When the modal outcome is an asset deal:** small-to-mid market transactions, S-corp or LLC targets, deals where the buyer has identified specific undisclosed-liability risks (particularly product liability, environmental, tax) that they will not underwrite, distressed asset sales, and carve-outs of a business line from a larger seller. For C-corp venture-backed targets with substantial §1202 QSBS at the founder / early-employee level, an asset deal is almost always the seller's *worst* option and will trigger material push-back at first pass.

### 2. Stock purchase

The buyer purchases the outstanding shares of the target directly from the target's shareholders. The target continues to exist as a legal entity, now with the buyer (or a buyer subsidiary) as its shareholder.

- **Assets:** all of the target's assets remain in the target entity. No asset-by-asset transfer, no anti-assignment consent problem for most contracts (though "change-of-control" provisions may still trigger — chapter 2 develops the interaction with the consideration structure).
- **Liabilities:** all of the target's liabilities — disclosed, undisclosed, contingent — remain in the target entity, which the buyer now owns. This is the buyer-disfavoured feature: the buyer inherits everything. R&W insurance and indemnification structure (mod-104 mechanics) are the primary mitigants.
- **Corporate approvals:** each shareholder must sell their shares individually. In practice, the mechanism is a shareholder-level SPA with drag-along-rights invocation from the preferred and majority-common consent under the target's stockholders' agreement (mod-101 chapter 5's audit surfaces the drag-along-rights terms; those mechanics show up here). A tender-offer structure may be used for public targets or for private targets with widely-dispersed shareholders, but for the typical venture-backed private target, drag-along and stockholder-consent solicitation carry the vote.
- **Tax at target level:** no gain / loss at the target entity level. The target's tax attributes (NOLs, tax basis in assets) generally carry over subject to §382 limitation on NOL utilisation.
- **Tax at buyer level:** the buyer takes the target's stock at cost basis; the target's *inside* asset basis is unchanged (no step-up). Absent a §338(h)(10) or §336(e) election, there is no depreciation / amortization benefit from the acquisition price above the target's historical basis.
- **Tax at shareholder level:** each selling shareholder recognises gain or loss on the sale of their shares. For a shareholder who held qualifying §1202 QSBS for at least five years, the §1202 exclusion applies — this is the QSBS-preservation seller preference.
- **§338(h)(10) election:** for S-corp targets (and 80%-owned subsidiaries of consolidated groups), a joint election under §338(h)(10) treats the stock deal as an asset deal *for tax purposes only* — buyer gets the step-up, seller pays asset-sale-level tax. §336(e) is a related election for asset-deal treatment where §338(h)(10) is not available. Chapter 5 develops the mechanics.

**When the modal outcome is a stock deal:** venture-backed C-corp targets where founder / early-employee §1202 QSBS preservation is a substantive negotiating point; targets with numerous permits / licenses / contracts that would be expensive or slow to re-consent; targets where the change-of-control provision in commercial contracts is more permissive than the assignment clause; targets where a §338(h)(10) election is available to reconcile the buyer's step-up preference with the seller's stock-deal preference.

### 3. Reverse-triangular merger

The buyer forms a wholly-owned merger subsidiary ("MergerSub") and merges MergerSub with-and-into the target. The target survives as a wholly-owned subsidiary of the buyer; MergerSub disappears; the target's former shareholders receive the merger consideration in exchange for their target shares.

- **Assets and liabilities:** as with a stock deal, the target continues to hold all its assets and liabilities; the buyer now owns the target. The reverse-triangular merger is functionally *equivalent* to a stock deal in its post-close balance-sheet effect, and is generally treated the same way for corporate-law-consequences purposes (contracts stay with the target; consents are needed only where a change-of-control clause is triggered).
- **Corporate approvals:** target board approves the merger agreement; target shareholders approve by the vote required under the target's charter and DGCL §251 (typically a majority of outstanding shares, or the higher threshold specified in the charter). Dissenting shareholders may have DGCL §262 appraisal rights depending on the consideration structure (all-stock in a public-company acquirer may be exempt; cash or mixed consideration typically preserves appraisal rights).
- **Compared with a straight stock purchase:** the reverse-triangular merger *replaces the need for each individual shareholder to sign an SPA* — the merger, once approved, cashes out (or converts) all outstanding target shares by operation of law under the merger statute. This is the practical reason it is used far more often than a direct stock purchase for private-company M&A: it is much easier to close cleanly.
- **§1202 QSBS impact:** the reverse-triangular merger is treated as a stock deal for tax purposes (shareholders receive consideration for their target stock). QSBS treatment is preserved for the qualifying stock exchanged.
- **§368 tax-free reorg treatment:** if the merger consideration is at least 80% qualifying stock of the acquirer, and the other §368(a)(2)(E) requirements are met (target is the surviving corporation; substantially all of target's assets remain in target post-merger), the reverse-triangular merger qualifies as a §368(a)(1)(A) statutory merger that is tax-free to the extent of the stock consideration. Chapter 5 develops the mechanics.

**When the modal outcome is a reverse-triangular merger:** the modal structure for a private-company acquisition of a C-corp Delaware target where the acquirer wants a stock-deal balance-sheet effect and the mechanical simplicity of a merger-statute closing. Also the modal structure when the transaction is intended to qualify as a §368 tax-free reorg on the stock consideration.

### 4. Forward-triangular merger

The buyer forms MergerSub as above, but this time the *target* merges into MergerSub, with MergerSub surviving. The target disappears; MergerSub (renamed) survives as a wholly-owned subsidiary of the buyer holding the target's former assets.

- **Assets and liabilities:** all target assets and liabilities transfer to MergerSub by operation of the merger statute. This is where the corporate-law-consequences diverge from the reverse-triangular: because the target disappears and MergerSub is the surviving entity, contracts that require consent on "assignment" or "transfer" may be interpreted as having been assigned. Whether specific contracts are triggered depends on the contract's language and case law in the relevant jurisdiction. Forward-triangular mergers therefore carry more contract-consent risk than reverse-triangular mergers.
- **Corporate approvals:** target board and shareholder approval as in a reverse-triangular merger, plus MergerSub-parent approval (the buyer's approval as sole shareholder of MergerSub).
- **§368 tax-free reorg treatment:** the forward-triangular structure can qualify as a §368(a)(1)(A) statutory merger that is tax-free to the extent of qualifying stock consideration under §368(a)(2)(D). The consideration must be at least 50% qualifying stock (compared to 80% for the reverse-triangular under §368(a)(2)(E)), and "substantially all" of the target's assets must be held by MergerSub post-merger.

**When the modal outcome is a forward-triangular merger:** transactions where the consideration mix falls in the 50–80% stock range (too much stock for a taxable structure to make sense, but too little for the reverse-triangular §368(a)(2)(E) test); transactions where the buyer specifically wants to integrate the target's operations into a merger-sub structure post-close (e.g., because the target's legal-entity structure is being simplified as part of the deal); transactions where the target's contracts have permissive assignment provisions and the contract-consent risk is low.

### 5. Forward merger (direct statutory merger)

The buyer merges with-and-into the target, or the target merges with-and-into the buyer, with the surviving entity holding all of the combined assets and liabilities. No MergerSub is used; the merger is directly between the two operating entities.

- **Assets and liabilities:** all assets and liabilities of the disappearing entity transfer to the surviving entity by operation of the merger statute. As in the forward-triangular structure, the "assignment" question on contracts is live.
- **Corporate approvals:** both parties' boards and shareholders approve (subject to certain small-merger exceptions under DGCL §251(f) where the surviving entity's shareholders' approval is not required if the merger does not materially affect them). If the buyer is a public company, the merger may or may not require the buyer's own shareholder vote depending on the size of the transaction under NYSE / Nasdaq listing rules (20% dilution threshold triggers a shareholder vote under NYSE Listed Company Manual §312.03 and Nasdaq Rule 5635).
- **§368 tax-free reorg treatment:** the direct statutory merger is the classic §368(a)(1)(A) "A reorg" and can be tax-free to the extent of qualifying stock consideration under the general continuity-of-interest doctrine (developed in chapter 5). No specific consideration-mix percentage threshold applies to the A reorg itself (unlike the triangular variants), though other reorg requirements do.

**When the modal outcome is a forward merger:** rare in modern private-company M&A because the buyer typically wants to isolate the target's liabilities in a separate subsidiary rather than merge them into the parent. More common in public-company M&A of similar-sized entities, in de-SPAC transactions, and in specific tax-driven structures (Reverse Morris Trust and F-reorg drop-downs discussed below).

### 6. Reverse Morris Trust (spin-merge)

A specialised structure that combines a *spin-off* of a business unit from a parent with an immediate merger of the spun-off entity into a third-party acquirer's subsidiary. The historical spinning parent's shareholders must own more than 50% of the combined entity post-merger for the spin to remain tax-free under IRC §355.

- **Corporate mechanics:** in one legal transaction sequence, the parent distributes the shares of the spun-off subsidiary to its shareholders (the spin-off), and the spun-off entity simultaneously merges with the third-party acquirer's subsidiary (the merger). The spun-off entity's former shareholders — who are now the parent's shareholders because of the spin-off — end up as majority owners of the combined entity.
- **Tax:** if the transaction meets the §355(a) requirements for a tax-free spin (five-year active-trade-or-business, business-purpose, continuity of interest, no device for distribution of E&P) *and* the reverse-Morris-Trust structural requirements (parent shareholders own more than 50% of the combined entity post-merger), the entire transaction is tax-free at the parent level. The parent avoids the corporate-level tax that a taxable sale of the subsidiary would trigger.
- **Complexity:** RMT structures are among the most complex in the M&A toolkit, requiring extensive tax counsel, private letter rulings (in some cases), and multi-year planning. They are essentially unavailable to venture-backed startup transactions — they exist because a large parent with a mature business unit wants to divest it without paying corporate-level tax.

**When the modal outcome is a Reverse Morris Trust:** carve-outs of a business unit from a large public-company parent to a third-party acquirer where the parent-level corporate tax on a straight sale would be prohibitive. Not relevant to venture-backed private-company M&A; included in the taxonomy because a comprehensive form catalogue is otherwise incomplete, and because CFOs and general counsels transitioning from venture-backed startup-exit contexts into corporate-strategy or corporate-development roles at larger companies will encounter it.

## The buyer-vs-seller tension at first pass

For a typical venture-backed C-corp Delaware target being acquired by a strategic or financial buyer, the first-pass form preferences look like this:

**Seller preferences (target founders and early employees):**
- *Stock deal or reverse-triangular merger*, preserving the shareholders' §1202 QSBS treatment on the qualifying stock they hold.
- *Tax-free treatment* to the extent of stock consideration under §368 (specifically §368(a)(1)(A) for a reverse-triangular merger with at least 80% acquirer stock).
- *Simplicity of closing* through the merger-statute mechanism rather than individual SPAs.
- *Rollover equity treatment* on any portion the founders / senior team roll into the acquirer's stock, with tax-free treatment on the rollover portion.
- *No two-level tax* on the corporate exit.

**Buyer preferences (buyer M&A and tax counsel):**
- *Asset deal or §338(h)(10) stock deal with step-up*, giving the buyer amortisation deductions on the goodwill and intangible-asset portion of the purchase price (typically 15-year amortisation under §197).
- *Isolation of pre-close liabilities* — either through an asset-deal carve-out or through a subsidiary structure that keeps the target's historical liabilities from crossing into the parent's operating balance sheet.
- *Minimisation of assumed obligations* — pension liabilities, litigation exposure, environmental exposure, employment claims, deferred-comp obligations.
- *Clean tax attributes* — the buyer does not want the target's NOL history if it comes with §382 complications.

**Where the tension resolves:**
- **Reverse-triangular merger** is the modal compromise for a venture-backed C-corp target where founders have material QSBS. It gives the buyer the closing simplicity and (via subsidiary structure) the liability-isolation benefits of a merger while preserving the shareholders' stock-deal tax posture.
- **Stock deal with a §338(h)(10) election** works for S-corp targets and 80%-owned subsidiaries of consolidated groups. It gives the buyer the asset-deal tax treatment (step-up) while giving the sellers the stock-deal corporate treatment (no asset-by-asset transfer). The election requires the joint consent of the target and its selling shareholders — the buyer *cannot make the election unilaterally* — and the tax cost to the sellers of the asset-deal-level tax typically has to be "grossed up" in the purchase price for the sellers to be indifferent. Chapter 5 develops the mechanics.
- **Asset deal** is unavoidable when the buyer is unwilling to underwrite specific undisclosed liabilities (product liability, environmental, tax exposure) and the R&W insurance market does not clear at a price the buyer will pay. The seller loses QSBS treatment and pays the two-level tax; this is the *worst* outcome for a C-corp target with material QSBS, and its cost has to be compensated in the headline number.
- **Forward-triangular merger** is a middle ground when consideration mix falls in the 50–80% stock range and the target's contracts are permissive on assignment.

The negotiation is not "seller wants stock, buyer wants assets, meet in the middle." It is: "The target is a C-corp Delaware entity with $180M of preference stack and founders who hold $15M each of qualifying §1202 stock; the buyer is a public strategic paying $400M with 60% cash and 40% acquirer stock; the buyer's tax director wants $50M of amortisable goodwill; the R&W insurer will underwrite disclosed liabilities but has flagged three items in the diligence Q&A that they will not underwrite. Which of the six forms lets all of these constraints resolve without breaking the deal?"

## The corporate-form mechanics that determine what the transaction *can* be

Not every form is available for every transaction. The following mechanics constrain the choice:

- **Target's entity type.** C-corp, S-corp, LLC (partnership-taxed or single-member disregarded), LP. §338(h)(10) requires an S-corp or an 80%-owned corporate subsidiary. §1202 QSBS requires C-corp target and qualifying stock. A single-member LLC drop-down enables an F-reorg pattern that is discussed in chapter 5.
- **Target's state of incorporation.** Delaware's DGCL is the modal reference and is well-developed on merger mechanics; other states' merger statutes may differ on notice, appraisal, and consent requirements. Cross-border implications are covered when the transaction is Cross-Border (see below).
- **Number and dispersion of target shareholders.** A stock deal that requires each shareholder to sign an SPA is impractical for a target with 200+ shareholders; a merger-statute structure that cashes out all shareholders by operation of law is essential in that case. Drag-along rights and stockholder-consent solicitation mechanics carry the required vote.
- **Preferred-stock terms.** The target's charter may specify class votes, protective provisions, or a class-specific consent required for a change-of-control transaction. A charter that requires a separate class vote of Series A / B / C / D preferred for approval of the merger is a serious complication if any single class has a blocking position.
- **Change-of-control provisions in commercial contracts.** Some contracts define "change of control" broadly enough that even a reverse-triangular merger (which does not, technically, cause the target to change ownership at the *contract* level) triggers a consent requirement. The target's commercial-contract library needs to be assessed (mod-105 diligence-workstream depth) to identify the consents required under each form.
- **Regulatory permits and licenses.** Some permits (broadcast licenses under FCC rules, insurance-carrier licenses under state insurance-department rules, healthcare-provider certifications under state and federal rules, defense-contractor security clearances under DoD rules) require re-application or notification on any form of change-of-control. The specific permit's rule determines what is required.
- **Antitrust and CFIUS filing thresholds.** The corporate-form choice does not change the filing threshold analysis (chapter 9), but the choice of form can affect the filing party (who files) and the closing sequence (some form-specific closing steps are more easily conditioned on filing clearance).
- **Cross-border considerations.** A US target being acquired by a foreign parent may require an inversion-resistant structure (§7874), foreign-antitrust filings on top of HSR (chapter 9), CFIUS review depending on the target's business (chapter 9), and may face foreign-shareholder-rollover complications on any stock consideration in the acquirer. These considerations often push the transaction toward a specific form.

## Common misalignments at LOI signing that later cost the deal

Some patterns to watch for at LOI:

- **LOI silent on form.** An LOI that says "cash purchase of the equity of Target at $X" without specifying reverse-triangular vs. direct stock deal vs. stock deal with §338(h)(10) leaves 30% of the tax and consent structure unresolved. The parties may spend weeks after LOI arguing about it while exclusivity ticks away. Best practice: LOI names the form.
- **LOI proposes a form the target cannot use.** "Reverse-triangular merger with §338(h)(10) election" is incoherent — §338(h)(10) requires the sale to be structured as a purchase of stock of an S-corp or a subsidiary of a consolidated group; the reverse-triangular merger of a C-corp target does not qualify. LOIs written by junior corp-dev associates sometimes mix these; a target's counsel who reads carefully catches the error early.
- **Buyer assumes seller will accept an asset deal without QSBS compensation.** For a target with material §1202 QSBS, an asset deal is worth 20–28% less per-founder than an equivalent stock deal (the QSBS-eligible capital gain that would have been federally-tax-free is now taxed at the top capital-gains rate). If the buyer wants the asset-deal structure, the seller expects the headline price to compensate — sometimes by 10–15% or more depending on the QSBS-eligible fraction of the equity being sold. A buyer who insists on asset structure without price compensation is negotiating against a founding team who will walk.
- **§338(h)(10) election proposed without gross-up.** The election converts a stock deal into an asset deal for tax purposes; the seller's tax bill goes up (the seller pays asset-sale-level tax rather than capital-gains-on-stock tax). Unless the sellers are indifferent (which they generally are not), the seller expects a price gross-up to cover the incremental tax. Proposing a §338(h)(10) election without addressing the gross-up in the LOI is the same negotiating error as the asset-deal-without-QSBS-compensation pattern.
- **Foreign-shareholder rollover complications ignored.** If the target has a non-US shareholder base of any material size (common for AI startups with international founding teams), stock consideration in a US acquirer may trigger tax withholding, FIRPTA implications, and rollover complications that make the "tax-free stock consideration" less attractive to that shareholder cohort than it looks on paper. A cash-carve-out for those shareholders may be needed.

## Summary

An M&A transaction is one of six modal forms: asset purchase, stock purchase, reverse-triangular merger, forward-triangular merger, forward merger, or Reverse Morris Trust. Each has distinct corporate, tax, and regulatory consequences, and the choice cascades into every other structural decision in the deal. For a typical venture-backed C-corp target, the reverse-triangular merger is the modal outcome because it combines the buyer-friendly liability-isolation subsidiary structure with the seller-friendly stock-deal tax posture (including §1202 QSBS preservation). Asset deals recur when the buyer will not underwrite specific undisclosed liabilities or when the target is an S-corp / LLC. Forward-triangular mergers show up when the consideration mix falls in a specific band. The Reverse Morris Trust is essentially unavailable to venture-backed startup exits and appears in the taxonomy for completeness. The next chapter installs the consideration-mix design that sits inside whichever form has been chosen, and chapters 5 and 8 return to the tax layer once the corporate-form vocabulary is fixed.
