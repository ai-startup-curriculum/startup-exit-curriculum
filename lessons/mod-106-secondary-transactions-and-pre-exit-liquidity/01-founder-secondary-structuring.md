# Founder Secondary — Rules-of-the-Road, Board-Approval Choreography, Tax, Signalling

## Why this matters

A **founder secondary** is a transaction in which a founder (or occasionally another meaningful common-stock holder — an early key employee, an angel, a departed co-founder) sells a portion of their common shares to an existing investor, a new investor coming into a priced round, or a purpose-built secondary-market buyer, before the company itself is sold or goes public. Twenty years ago the pattern was rare and mildly disreputable — "if the founder wants out, the company must be broken." Today, at any growth-stage priced round of consequence, some form of founder-secondary discussion is standard: primary-round-only financings are the exception, not the rule, once the company crosses roughly Series C. The reason is straightforward. A founder who is five, seven, or ten years into building the company, who has taken a below-market salary throughout, whose entire net worth is in illiquid common stock, and whose personal circumstances (mortgage, children entering college, a spouse's business venture, a health situation) demand *some* liquidity is a founder who benefits materially from a small, structured cash-out — and a founder relieved of household-cash-flow anxiety is a demonstrably better founder-CEO than one who is not. The lead investor who insists the founder take *no* liquidity in a $150M Series D is often the same investor who complains six months later that the founder is distracted by acquisition inbound.

But the transaction is not free. Every founder secondary is watched by the current investor syndicate, is watched by any acquirer or IPO underwriter that later reads the transaction ledger, is watched by employees who see the founder taking cash while they hold illiquid common, and is watched by the tax authorities in every jurisdiction the founder resides. It carries specific *tax* consequences (long-term-capital-gains treatment, holding-period preservation, the interaction with IRC §1202 QSBS covered in mod-103 chapter 8), specific *governance* consequences (board approval, ROFR waiver, disclosure to the round, the disclosure schedule in a future definitive agreement), and specific *signalling* consequences (perception by remaining investors, by the market, and — critically — by acquirers who read a large founder cash-out as an "exit-intent signal" and adjust their offers accordingly).

This chapter installs the founder-secondary framework as a *repeatable* discipline. It covers what board approval a founder secondary actually needs, what size the market accepts before the transaction becomes governance-toxic, how the tax treatment interacts with the founder's holding period and QSBS position, and how to time and communicate a secondary so it does not signal an exit intent the founder does not have.

> **Reminder: education, not legal, tax, or investment advice.** A live founder secondary requires transaction counsel, the founder's personal tax advisor, and — where the round is material — the finance function's fund-administration and 409A counsel. This chapter installs the M&A / transaction-execution vocabulary; the specifics come from qualified counsel.

## The three canonical shapes of a founder secondary

Founder-secondary transactions fall into three shapes, each with its own approval mechanic and its own signalling implication.

### Shape A — Secondary alongside a primary round ("secondary sleeve on a round")

By far the most common pattern at growth stage. The company runs a primary financing round — Series C, Series D, Series E — and the lead investor (or the round more broadly) allocates some fraction of the round's committed capital to purchasing existing common shares from the founder team, and sometimes from early employees. The lead investor puts $60M of new capital into the company (the primary component) and $15M into buying founder / early-employee common (the secondary sleeve). The primary component funds the company; the secondary component provides personal liquidity without diluting the round's per-share economics beyond what the primary component already does.

Signalling implication: relatively mild. A secondary sleeve embedded in a priced round is understood by the market as a founder-comfort mechanism the round has explicitly blessed. It is disclosed on the round's cap-table impact page but does not typically make its way into external discussion. The lead investor's willingness to fund it is itself a positive signal — the lead trusts the founder enough to keep them in the seat with additional liquidity.

Governance mechanic: the secondary sleeve is negotiated and papered *inside* the primary round's stock-purchase-agreement or a companion secondary-purchase-agreement executed contemporaneously. Board approval, ROFR waiver, and stockholder-consent choreography all fold into the round's approval package.

### Shape B — Standalone secondary between rounds ("off-cycle secondary")

Less common but not rare. A founder wants liquidity six months to a year after a priced round has closed, without triggering a new primary round. An existing investor (typically the lead or a co-investor) or a purpose-built secondary buyer (Industry Ventures, StepStone, Coller, Lexington, Hamilton Lane's secondary practice) acquires common shares directly from the founder in a standalone transaction. No new capital enters the company.

Signalling implication: material. The market reads a standalone founder secondary as "the founder wanted out of some position badly enough not to wait for the next round." The lead investor's *reaction* becomes part of the transaction — the lead who buys the shares is signalling comfort; the lead who refuses is signalling concern. A founder who runs a standalone secondary without discussing it with the lead first is running a governance risk that will show up in the next round.

Governance mechanic: the transaction runs as a common-stock secondary sale, subject to the company's Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement (chapter 6). Board approval is required for the ROFR waiver even where the buyer is an existing investor; the board must formally decline (or exercise) the company's ROFR before the transaction can close.

### Shape C — Tender-participation as founder liquidity ("founder participation in employee tender")

When the company runs an employee tender offer (chapter 2), the founder is *typically excluded* from participating — the tender is designed for the rank-and-file, and executive participation is a separate governance question. But some companies structure a tender with a specific executive tranche, or with a smaller founder tranche embedded in the tender, that lets the founder sell a bounded number of shares at the tender price alongside the employee population.

Signalling implication: depends on how the tender is disclosed to employees. A tender that lets executives cash out significantly more than the rank-and-file employees is a morale event; a tender that caps executive participation to (for example) 50% of the highest per-employee cap for the tender is understood as founder-alongside-team liquidity.

Governance mechanic: the tender's overall approval package (chapter 2) covers this. Executive participation is a specific board and (typically) compensation-committee decision that is disclosed to participating employees and reflected in the tender's public documentation.

## The 10–20% common-holding sale ceiling

The single most-cited market-practitioner rule of thumb for founder-secondary sizing is the **10–20% of common holdings** ceiling. The specific interpretation:

- A founder secondary that sells up to roughly **10%** of the founder's common-stock holdings (calculated on the founder's fully-vested-plus-projected-vest position through some near-term horizon) is broadly acceptable and requires only the standard governance package.
- A founder secondary sizing between **10% and 20%** of the founder's holdings is negotiable but expected to be discussed with the lead investor in the primary round (Shape A) or the lead investor of the most recent round (Shape B or C). The board — meaning the specific investor directors, in practice — will want to understand the founder's use of the proceeds at some general level.
- A founder secondary that reaches or exceeds **20%** of the founder's holdings is a *material* transaction that most boards will require a specific fiduciary-and-alignment discussion around. At 30%+, most boards read the transaction as an alignment-of-interest concern and will either refuse the ROFR waiver or negotiate the size down.

Two important qualifiers on this rule of thumb.

First, the denominator is the *founder's holdings*, not the total common outstanding. A founder secondary of 5% of the common outstanding could be 50% of a specific founder's position (if the founder holds only 10% of the common); or 8% of a founder's position (if the founder holds 60% of the common). The rule is applied against the *founder's* position because the alignment question is about the founder's remaining stake, not about the aggregate common in circulation.

Second, the rule of thumb is stated in cash-out-*proportion* terms, not in dollar terms. A founder selling 10% of their position at a $2B valuation is realising much more cash than a founder selling 15% at a $200M valuation. The market's read of the alignment question is proportional, not absolute — a $50M cash-out at a $5B valuation with the founder retaining $450M of equity reads as sensible household-diversification; a $10M cash-out at a $100M valuation with the founder retaining $30M reads as material lightening.

Practitioner discipline: never state a founder-secondary size as a dollar number without also stating the ratio to the founder's remaining position. The board conversation is much cleaner when the framing is "the founder is selling 12% of their fully-vested position, retaining 88% and continuing to vest into the rest" rather than "the founder is selling $18M of common."

## Alignment-of-interest optics — the lead-investor lens

The lead investor of the current or most-recent round is the audience whose comfort with a founder secondary matters most. Their frame is straightforward: the lead's fund is illiquid; the fund cannot realise its position until the company exits; the fund's return on this investment depends on the founder continuing to build the company with the same intensity as before. A founder who has just realised $20M of cash may or may not still bring that intensity. The lead's job is to satisfy itself that the founder still will.

The four alignment questions the lead typically wants answered — explicitly or implicitly — before blessing a secondary:

1. **What is the founder's remaining exposure?** Retained equity value divided by pre-secondary equity value. A founder retaining 88% of their position after a secondary reads differently than one retaining 55%.

2. **What is the founder's remaining unvested-equity book?** A founder who is 90% vested and sells 15% of vested holdings has a materially different alignment posture than one who is 60% vested and sells 15% (because the latter continues to earn substantially more equity through remaining vesting).

3. **What is the use of proceeds?** The board is not going to audit the founder's spending, but the general framing matters. "Pay off house, fund kids' education, diversify a small portfolio position, secondary house" reads as household stabilisation. "Fund a new venture I am starting on the side" reads as an exit-intent problem. "General diversification" is neutral. The founder's own framing here signals which of these is the case.

4. **What is the founder's post-secondary commitment posture?** Some rounds explicitly include a *founder recommitment* — a re-signing of the assignment-of-inventions agreement, a re-affirmation of the founder's full-time exclusive commitment, or a specific term of continued employment (typically two to four years, aligned with the round's likely exit horizon) with a specific severance / clawback structure if the founder departs early. This is not universal but has become common at Series C and later for larger secondaries.

Communication discipline: the founder's own framing of the secondary to the lead is the most important governance artefact of the transaction. A founder who arrives at the discussion having already done the alignment math — "here is the proportion, here is the retained position, here is the vesting profile, here is my recommitment posture" — closes the discussion in one meeting. A founder who arrives asking for a secondary without having done that work turns a one-meeting discussion into a three-week negotiation and, often, a smaller number than would otherwise have been available.

## Board-approval choreography

The formal governance mechanic of a founder secondary depends on the transaction shape (A / B / C above) and on the specific provisions of the company's charter, stockholders' agreement, voting agreement, and ROFR / co-sale agreement. The typical steps for a Shape A or Shape B founder secondary:

### 1. Pre-brief the lead

The founder or the founder's counsel discusses the proposed secondary with the lead investor of the current round (Shape A) or the lead of the most-recent round (Shape B) *before* the transaction is papered. The pre-brief covers proportion, retained position, use of proceeds, and (for Shape A) the round context. The lead's initial reaction determines whether the transaction proceeds, gets resized, or gets structured differently.

### 2. Board-level informational discussion

Once the lead is comfortable, the transaction is brought to the board as an informational item (or, at some companies, a formal agenda item at the next board meeting). The board is not typically *approving* the founder's decision to sell — the founder's shares are their own — but is approving specific transaction actions the board must formally take.

### 3. Formal board approvals

The typical formal actions the board must take:

- **ROFR waiver.** The company's stockholders' agreement or ROFR / co-sale agreement typically gives the *company* a right of first refusal on any proposed sale of common stock by a stockholder. The board must formally decide whether to exercise or waive that right. In a founder secondary the board almost universally waives, but the formal action must be taken and minuted.
- **Investor ROFR waiver.** Preferred investors typically have a *secondary* ROFR that kicks in if the company waives its primary ROFR. In a Shape A transaction where the buyer is the round's lead, this is trivial (the lead is exercising, not standing on the ROFR). In a Shape B transaction with an outside secondary buyer, the preferred investors' ROFR must be formally waived — a document circulated among the preferred holders with a defined response window.
- **Co-sale (tag-along) rights.** The co-sale agreement (chapter 6) typically gives other common holders — often including other founders and early employees — the right to *participate* in a sale proportionally. In a Shape B transaction the co-sale mechanic must be exercised or formally waived; in a Shape A transaction where the round is buying up to a specific secondary allocation, the co-sale mechanic often surfaces additional participants and the round's secondary allocation is reprorated.
- **Board resolution approving the transaction.** For internal-record purposes and for future disclosure-schedule purposes, the board typically adopts a resolution acknowledging the secondary, the parties, the price, and the approvals granted.

### 4. Documentation

The transaction itself is papered as a stock-purchase agreement between the founder (seller) and the buyer (typically the round's lead in Shape A; the outside buyer in Shape B). Standard content: representations by the seller as to their title to the shares, no-liens, capacity to transfer, receipt of price, and — critically — a *company-side* representation of no-adverse-material-information. The company's counsel typically signs off that the company has no undisclosed material information that would make the transaction unfair to the buyer at the negotiated price.

The 10b5-1-adjacent point: the founder and the company are both effectively certifying, at the closing of the secondary, that they have no undisclosed material information about the company that would make the negotiated price unfair. If the company has an unannounced material development — a pending large customer loss, an unannounced M&A discussion, an unannounced pivot — the secondary should be paused until that information can be disclosed or resolved. This is not literal Rule 10b-5 exposure (the company is not publicly traded), but the private-company analogue of insider-trading discipline (state common-law fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, breach of the SPA's representations) applies.

### 5. Update the cap table and record the transaction

The company's fund administrator (in-house finance or Carta / Pulley / Shareworks depending on the platform) records the transaction in the cap table, transfers the shares from the founder's holdings to the buyer's holdings, and updates the fully-diluted schedule. The transaction is reflected in the next quarterly investor report and included in the disclosure schedules of any future definitive agreement.

## Tax treatment — LTCG, holding period, and the QSBS interaction

The tax treatment of a founder secondary depends on the founder's holding period, the founder's basis, and the founder's §1202 posture. The framework:

### Long-term capital gains at the individual level

A founder selling common stock they have held for more than one year realises **long-term capital gains** on the difference between the sale price and their basis in the shares. At the top federal rate, long-term capital gains are taxed at 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) for high-income taxpayers, plus state income tax at the founder's residence-state rate (California 13.3%, New York 10.9%, or 0% in Texas / Florida / Washington / Nevada / Wyoming). Total federal-plus-state can range from 23.8% (0-tax state) to 37.1% (California resident).

For most founder secondaries, the founder's basis is negligible (common stock issued at incorporation at $0.0001 per share) and the tax rate applies to essentially the full sale price.

### Short-term problem — holding-period preservation

If the founder has held the specific shares being sold for *less than* one year — this can happen when the founder has been early-exercising options and the just-exercised shares are the shares being sold — the gain is taxed as **short-term capital gains** at ordinary-income rates (up to 37% federal plus state, plus 3.8% NIIT). The tax difference between short-term and long-term treatment on a $10M gain is roughly $1.7M — enough to substantially reshape the transaction economics.

Practitioner discipline: identify *which specific shares* are being sold. If the founder has multiple lots of common — an initial founding grant from 2019, an ESOP-refresh grant from 2021, an early-exercised option lot from 2023 — the SPA must specify which lot is being transferred, and the specific-lot identification determines the tax treatment. The default first-in-first-out (FIFO) treatment is typically favourable; but where the founder has specific-lot elections available, the counsel and personal tax advisor should model the treatment before the transaction closes.

### QSBS interaction — the largest single tax consideration

The federal §1202 QSBS exclusion (mod-103 chapter 8) applies to founder common stock acquired at original issuance in a qualifying C-corp with less than $50M of aggregate gross assets at issuance, held for at least five years. For a founder whose common stock meets the qualifying criteria, a §1202-qualifying sale can exclude up to $10M (or 10× basis, whichever is greater) of gain *per issuer per taxpayer* from federal tax.

For a founder secondary of $8M of qualifying stock, that means potentially zero federal tax (subject to the state-conformity variations — California and Pennsylvania do not conform). For a founder secondary of $15M, that means the first $10M is excluded and the remaining $5M is taxed at LTCG rates.

The interaction with the secondary transaction requires attention to three points:

**Point 1 — Holding-period timing.** The five-year holding period runs from the *original issuance date* of the specific shares. A founder who received a founding grant on 2019-04-15 first becomes §1202-eligible on that grant on 2024-04-15. A secondary that closes on 2024-04-14 forfeits the §1202 exclusion on the sold shares; a secondary that closes on 2024-04-16 preserves it. Sequencing the secondary around the holding-period anniversary can be worth millions of dollars in tax.

**Point 2 — Specific-lot identification.** Where the founder holds multiple lots with different issuance dates, the SPA's specific-lot identification (or the founder's tax-lot-selection election, where the platform supports it) determines which shares are treated as sold. Sell the pre-5-year lot and the §1202 exclusion is forfeited; sell the post-5-year lot and it is preserved. Some Carta / Pulley platforms handle specific-lot identification automatically; others require explicit designation in the transaction paper.

**Point 3 — Per-issuer cap.** The §10M cap is *per issuer* — a founder holding QSBS in multiple qualifying companies has separate $10M caps for each. A founder who has already used $10M of §1202 exclusion on this issuer in a prior transaction does not have a second $10M cap available for a subsequent secondary; the remaining gain on the subsequent sale is fully taxable at LTCG. This matters for founders who took an earlier partial secondary and are now considering another.

The §1045 rollover — deferring gain by reinvesting sale proceeds into a new qualifying startup within 60 days (mod-103 chapter 8) — is a rare but non-trivial tool for a founder whose secondary shares are just short of the 5-year threshold and who intends to invest the proceeds into a new venture. In most cases, timing the secondary past the anniversary is the cleaner path.

### Non-US-resident founder considerations

For founders resident outside the United States at the time of the secondary — a founder who has relocated to the UK, Singapore, Australia, or elsewhere while continuing to hold US-domiciled common stock — the tax treatment involves both US withholding (typically none for a non-real-property equity sale by a non-resident under §865, but with treaty and PFIC considerations) and the founder's local-jurisdiction capital-gains regime. This is a personal-tax-counsel question with country-specific answers; the founder's home-jurisdiction tax advisor drives the analysis.

## Timing and signalling — keeping a founder secondary from reading as exit-intent

The single most costly failure mode of a founder secondary is signalling. A founder who takes a large secondary six months before running a sell-side M&A process signals to every acquirer at the process that the founder is trying to derisk before an anticipated event; acquirer offers arrive lower and negotiate harder. A founder who takes a secondary two months after the company misses its quarterly plan for the first time signals that even the founder does not believe in the recovery. A founder secondary that follows a spike of departures from the founding team signals exit intent even more strongly.

The signalling calculus has three levers.

### Lever 1 — Anchoring to a round

A secondary embedded in a priced primary round (Shape A) is the least-signalling shape available. The market reads the primary round as the primary event; the secondary sleeve is a companion mechanic. The lead investor's simultaneous check-writing on both sides — primary and secondary — communicates continued conviction rather than exit intent.

Whenever operationally feasible, structure the founder secondary as a sleeve on a round rather than as a standalone transaction. The premium in optionality preservation is often worth the wait if a primary round is anticipated within 12–18 months.

### Lever 2 — Sizing below the alignment ceiling

A founder secondary at 8–12% of the founder's position reads as household-diversification; at 25%+ it reads as material lightening. Almost every acquirer who reads the target's transaction ledger during diligence (mod-105) reads the founder-secondary history as a specific data point. Keeping each secondary transaction below the alignment ceiling (typically 15% of the founder's position, sometimes 20% for very later-stage transactions) preserves the ability to describe the transaction as household stabilisation rather than exit-derisking.

### Lever 3 — Timing distance from a sell-side event

A founder secondary that closes 24+ months before the eventual sale-of-the-company is analytically distinct from the sell-side event; a secondary that closes 3–6 months before the sale-of-the-company is not — the acquirer will read them as connected regardless of the founder's actual intent at each date. Practitioner discipline: any founder secondary anticipated in the twelve to eighteen months before a plausible sell-side process should be resized (or deferred) to keep the transaction ledger clean. This is not a rule of law; it is a rule of acquirer perception.

The obverse: a founder secondary that closes cleanly at Series C, with a sell-side event that emerges organically two or three years later, is entirely defensible and does not read as exit-intent by any acquirer.

## The disclosure-schedule footprint in a future definitive agreement

When the company eventually sells or goes public, the founder-secondary transaction history becomes part of the deal disclosure. In an M&A definitive agreement, the disclosure schedule to the SPA / merger agreement typically includes:

- All transactions in the target's securities in the last three-to-five years, including founder secondaries.
- All amendments to founder / executive assignment-of-inventions / employment / non-compete / confidentiality agreements executed in connection with prior secondaries.
- All ROFR waivers and co-sale waivers granted by the company.
- Any recommitment / employment-term agreements executed by the founder in connection with a secondary.

In an S-1 for an IPO, the disclosure includes:

- The founder's transactions in the target's securities during the reporting periods (typically last two fiscal years plus the interim period).
- The founder's employment agreement, including any recommitment provisions.
- Related-party disclosures if the buyer of any secondary was a related party (an existing investor with a board seat, for example).

Practitioner discipline: the paper trail of every founder secondary should assume future disclosure. Every SPA, every ROFR waiver, every board resolution, and every recommitment agreement should be maintained in the target's corporate-records file (workstream 01 of a future data room, mod-105 chapter 1) and preserved in a form that can be handed to future deal counsel and future underwriter counsel without a fire drill.

## Summary

A founder secondary is a structured, board-approved sale of common stock by the founder to an existing investor, a new round investor, or a purpose-built secondary buyer, ahead of the company's eventual exit. The three canonical shapes — secondary sleeve on a primary round, standalone off-cycle secondary, and executive tranche of an employee tender — carry different signalling implications and different governance mechanics. The market-practitioner rule of thumb sizes each transaction at 10–20% of the founder's fully-vested position, applied against the founder's *retained* position rather than absolute dollar amount, with the lead investor's alignment lens dominating any transaction near the ceiling. The board-approval choreography — pre-brief the lead, board informational discussion, formal ROFR / co-sale / investor waivers, board resolution, transaction documentation, cap-table update — is the standard governance package regardless of shape. The tax treatment turns on long-term-capital-gains treatment (holding period > 1 year), the specific §1202 QSBS interaction (5-year holding, $10M / 10× basis exclusion cap, specific-lot identification), and the interaction with residency and state conformity. The signalling calculus is dominated by three levers: anchoring the secondary to a primary round (least-signalling), sizing below the alignment ceiling, and preserving distance from any anticipated sell-side event.
