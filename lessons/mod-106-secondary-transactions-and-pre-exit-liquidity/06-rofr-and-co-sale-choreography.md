# ROFR and Co-Sale Choreography — The Standard NVCA Pattern, Notice, Waiver, and Tag-Along Rights

## Why this matters

Every private-company-share transfer described in the preceding chapters — the founder secondary of chapter 1, the employee tender of chapter 2, the structured secondary of chapter 4, the secondary-market-platform transaction of chapter 5 — sits on top of the same underlying contractual apparatus: the **Right of First Refusal (ROFR)** and the **Co-Sale (tag-along) rights** established in the target's charter, stockholders' agreement, and voting agreement. These provisions govern whether a shareholder can freely transfer shares to a third-party buyer, who has the right to step in and buy instead, and who has the right to *participate* alongside if the sale does proceed. They are not incidental — a poorly-run ROFR process can convert a routine founder secondary into a governance dispute, block a tender-offer close for weeks, or, in the worst cases, expose the target company to breach-of-contract litigation by a preferred holder who was cut out of a notice window.

The mechanics are stable and well-established in the venture-capital ecosystem. The National Venture Capital Association's (NVCA) Model Legal Documents — the industry-standard preferred-stock financing document set (charter, stockholders' agreement, voting agreement, ROFR / co-sale agreement) — establish a canonical pattern that most venture-backed private companies operate under with only modest modifications. Understanding the NVCA pattern is the price of entry to any private-company secondary transaction; every step of every transaction runs through this apparatus.

This chapter installs the ROFR and co-sale choreography as a practitioner would run it. It covers the standard NVCA pattern (company first, then preferred holders, then possibly other holders), the notice mechanics (what the transferring shareholder must deliver and to whom), the waiver mechanics (how the company's board and the preferred holders decide whether to exercise), the co-sale (tag-along) rights that let minority holders participate proportionally, and the failure modes when the process is not run cleanly.

> **Reminder: education, not legal advice.** The specific ROFR / co-sale provisions in any target's charter and stockholders' agreement may vary from the NVCA baseline. A live transaction requires the target's counsel to run the specific-agreement analysis and drive the notice, waiver, and participation mechanics. This chapter installs the vocabulary and the modal pattern; the specifics come from qualified counsel and the target's actual documents.

## The standard NVCA-pattern ROFR — company first, preferred second, common third

The typical NVCA ROFR / co-sale agreement is a contract executed by the company, the holders of preferred stock, and the founders (and often other significant common holders) that governs the transfer of shares by any of them. The core mechanic is a **cascading right of first refusal** — a proposed transfer must first be offered to the company; if the company declines, then to the preferred holders; if they decline, only then can the transfer proceed to the intended third-party buyer.

### The three-level ROFR cascade

The typical priority order:

1. **Company** — the company itself has the first right to purchase the shares proposed to be transferred at the negotiated price. The company's decision is typically made by the board; the board must formally decide within the notice period whether to exercise or waive.
2. **Preferred holders** — if the company declines, the preferred holders (typically pro rata based on their preferred-stock ownership) have the next right to purchase the shares at the negotiated price. Some agreements permit over-allotment — a preferred holder that wants more shares than their pro-rata allocation can get them if another preferred holder does not exercise their allocation.
3. **Other holders (occasional)** — some agreements provide a third-tier right for other significant common holders (typically named specifically or defined as holders above a specified ownership threshold) if the company and preferred holders both decline.

If all levels of the ROFR cascade decline (or fail to exercise within their notice windows), the transferring shareholder can proceed with the transfer to the intended third-party buyer at the negotiated price, subject to the co-sale mechanics discussed below.

### The exception categories

Not every transfer triggers the ROFR. Standard NVCA agreements typically exempt several categories:

- **Transfers to family members and to family-planning trusts** for estate-planning purposes.
- **Transfers by devolution** (inheritance following death).
- **Transfers among affiliated entities** of an institutional holder (VC fund transfers among its own affiliated vehicles).
- **Transfers pursuant to a divorce settlement** (with variations on whether the transferee is subject to a matching restriction).
- **Small transfers below a de minimis threshold** (e.g., aggregate transfers under 1,000 shares in a defined window).

The exceptions are typically narrow — a founder who gifts a small percentage of common to a spouse or trust does not need to run the ROFR process; a founder who is selling to any arms-length third-party buyer for value does.

### The company / preferred waiver dynamic

In practice, the company almost universally waives its ROFR on employee-and-founder secondary transactions — the company does not want to use its cash to buy back its own stock in a routine liquidity transaction, and the board's fiduciary discipline typically supports waiving. The preferred holders' decision is more variable: an aggressive preferred holder who wants to increase their position may exercise; a routine preferred holder who has no incremental capital allocation for the target's shares will waive. In tender-offer contexts (chapter 2), the preferred holders' ROFR is usually waived as part of the tender's overall approval package because the tender's buyer identity is already defined and the tender is structured against a specific committed buyer.

## The notice mechanics — what the transferring shareholder must deliver

The ROFR process begins with the transferring shareholder delivering a **transfer notice** to the company (and often to the preferred holders directly). The notice contents are typically specified in the ROFR / co-sale agreement and usually include:

- **The identity of the proposed transferee** — buyer name, and (in bilateral transactions) some basic buyer information sufficient to let the ROFR holders evaluate the counterparty.
- **The number of shares proposed to be transferred** — specific share counts and, where applicable, specific share lot identification.
- **The proposed transfer price** — per-share price and total transaction consideration.
- **The proposed closing date** — the target settlement date.
- **A bona fide offer document from the transferee** — some agreements require the transferring shareholder to attach a signed offer or a term sheet from the third-party buyer, evidencing that the negotiated price is genuine and enforceable.
- **The proposed transfer terms** — any conditions, contingencies, or non-price economics of the sale.

The notice starts specific **notice windows** for each level of the ROFR cascade. Typical windows are:

- **Company response window**: 15–30 days from receipt of the notice.
- **Preferred-holder response window**: an additional 15–30 days after the company's response (or after the expiration of the company's response window if the company does not respond).
- **Cleared-to-transfer window**: once the ROFR is fully waived or declined, the transferring shareholder typically has 60–120 days to complete the transfer at the specified terms; if the transfer does not close within that window, the process must be re-noticed.

The specific windows in any target's agreement can vary, and the target's counsel drives the exact timing.

### The specific-transferee-and-terms discipline

An important operational point: the notice is specific to the *identified transferee* at the *specified terms*. If the transferring shareholder cannot complete the transfer to that transferee or wants to change the terms, the notice must be re-run. This creates important discipline:

- A founder cannot notice a transfer to Buyer A, get the ROFR waived, and then transfer to Buyer B — the transfer to B requires its own notice and its own ROFR run.
- A tender offer with a defined buyer identity must go through the ROFR / waiver process with respect to that buyer specifically; if the tender's buyer changes mid-process, the ROFR run may need to restart.
- A price change during the process (e.g., the transferee negotiates the price down after diligence) may trigger a re-notice, depending on the specific agreement's terms.

Practitioner discipline: the transferring shareholder and their counsel confirm the buyer identity, the price, and the terms *before* delivering the notice. Iterating on any of these mid-process is expensive.

## The waiver mechanics — board decision and preferred-holder circulation

The company's ROFR waiver is a specific **board decision**. The board must formally decide whether to exercise or waive the company's ROFR, typically at a meeting or via written consent. The decision is documented in board minutes or the written consent record. In practice:

- The board's decision is almost always to waive on employee-and-founder secondary transactions (as noted above).
- The board's decision must be reasoned in some way — the board is exercising a corporate right and (in the standard NVCA agreement) the decision is subject to the board's fiduciary discipline. In practice, this means a brief board discussion and a clean resolution rather than an extended deliberation.
- The board's waiver is communicated to the transferring shareholder in writing (a formal waiver letter from the company's secretary or from counsel).

The preferred-holder waiver is typically administered by the **investor-relations function** or by outside counsel who circulates a specific waiver document to each preferred holder. The typical mechanics:

- Each preferred holder receives a specific written notice with the transfer details, the ROFR-participation percentage they are entitled to, and a response window.
- Each preferred holder responds (in writing) with their decision: exercise (specify the number of shares they wish to acquire, up to their pro-rata allocation), decline, or over-subscribe (if the agreement permits).
- Non-responses are typically treated as declines under a "silence is a waiver" provision. This is a critical operational point — a preferred holder who forgets to respond loses their right to participate.
- The company (or counsel) aggregates responses at the close of the window and confirms whether any preferred holders have exercised.

If the preferred-holder waiver process yields any exercises, the transferring shareholder must sell to the exercising preferred holders (up to their exercised amount) instead of to the intended third-party buyer for that share of the transaction. If the exercised amount is less than the total proposed transfer, the remainder can proceed to the intended third-party buyer.

## Co-sale (tag-along) rights — the participation right

The **co-sale right** (also called **tag-along right**) is a distinct provision that operates alongside the ROFR. Where the ROFR gives specified holders the right to *buy* the shares proposed to be transferred, the co-sale right gives specified holders the right to *participate* in the sale proportionally — selling their own shares alongside the transferring shareholder to the same buyer at the same terms.

### The co-sale mechanic

Typical NVCA-pattern co-sale rights:

- **Preferred holders** typically have co-sale rights on any transfer by a founder (or by other named significant common holders). If a founder proposes to sell 100,000 shares to a buyer, each preferred holder can elect to sell a proportional number of their preferred shares (typically on an as-converted-to-common basis) to the same buyer at the same per-share price, alongside the founder's transfer.
- **Other founders and significant common holders** sometimes have reciprocal co-sale rights on each other's transfers.
- The co-sale participation is typically prorated across the co-sale-eligible pool if the buyer's total purchase intent does not accommodate everyone's full proportional participation. In that case, the transferring shareholder's transfer is reduced proportionally as well — everyone participates on the same proportion of their proposed sale.

### The pro-rata proration mechanic in practice

Concrete example. Founder proposes to transfer 100,000 common shares to a buyer at $50/share ($5M total). The company has preferred holders with 500,000 preferred shares outstanding (on an as-converted basis), all of whom have co-sale rights. The founder + preferred co-sale pool = 100,000 + 500,000 = 600,000 shares. The founder's proportion of the pool = 100,000 / 600,000 = 16.67%. If the buyer is willing to purchase up to 100,000 shares at $5M and every preferred holder elects to fully participate:

- The buyer's 100,000 shares are allocated across the pool: each participant's proportion of the 100,000 shares is (their share count in the pool / total pool) × 100,000.
- Founder receives: (100,000 / 600,000) × 100,000 = 16,667 shares × $50 = $833,335 (16.67% of what they had originally proposed to sell).
- Preferred holders receive: (500,000 / 600,000) × 100,000 = 83,333 shares × $50 = $4,166,650 (spread proportionally across the preferred pool).

The founder wanted to sell $5M but ends up selling $833K because the co-sale rights were fully exercised. This is a real and specific risk in any founder-secondary transaction — the founder cannot count on selling the full proposed amount unless the co-sale-eligible pool waives.

In practice, co-sale rights on founder secondaries are frequently *waived* by the preferred holders (they support the founder's liquidity and have their own liquidity paths through eventual exit). But the waiver is a specific process the target's counsel must run — the co-sale-eligible pool must formally decline participation in writing, and the pattern is that some preferred holders will exercise, some will decline, and the transaction outcomes depend on the specific responses.

### Co-sale in tender offers

Tender offers (chapter 2) present a specific co-sale question: does an employee tender trigger co-sale rights that would give preferred holders the right to participate?

The answer depends on the specific agreement's language:

- If the co-sale right applies only to founder / significant-common-holder transfers, an employee tender that excludes founders does not trigger preferred co-sale rights, and the tender can proceed against the buyer's committed capital without preferred participation.
- If the co-sale right applies to any transfer of common shares by any holder, an employee tender could trigger co-sale rights for the preferred pool. This is unusual in modern NVCA-pattern agreements but not unknown; the target's counsel must run the specific-agreement analysis.

Where preferred co-sale rights *do* apply to employee tenders, the preferred pool must formally waive as part of the tender's approval package — a document circulated among the preferred holders with a defined response window, similar to the ROFR waiver process. In practice, at a tender's design phase the target confirms the co-sale posture with the preferred holders in advance to avoid surprises.

## The frequently-overlooked co-sale interaction

The single most-common failure mode in private-company secondary transactions is neglecting the co-sale mechanic. Practitioners familiar with ROFR often assume the co-sale is a parallel mechanic that runs automatically, but in fact:

- Co-sale rights are typically *distinct* from ROFR rights. A holder can waive one and exercise the other.
- Co-sale rights typically have their *own* notice window (usually parallel to but not identical to the ROFR window).
- Co-sale rights typically apply to a *broader* set of transfers than ROFR — some transfers exempt from ROFR (e.g., transfers to affiliates) still trigger co-sale, depending on the specific agreement.
- The co-sale exercise mechanic requires the *transferring shareholder* to accept the reduced allocation — the buyer's purchase order is fulfilled proportionally, not additively, so exercised co-sale reduces the transferring shareholder's realised transaction.

Practitioner discipline: at the design phase of any transaction, run the specific co-sale analysis with the target's counsel. Confirm the specific agreement language. Confirm which parties have co-sale rights. Confirm the notice mechanics and the pro-rata proration rules. Confirm whether waivers are pre-negotiated or in-process during the transaction. Neglecting this discipline is one of the most common ways a routine founder secondary or tender offer gets delayed by weeks and forced to re-run.

## Interaction with tender offers, structured secondaries, and platform transactions

The ROFR / co-sale mechanics apply to *every* private-company-share transfer, but their operational integration differs by transaction type.

### Tender offers

In a tender offer (chapters 2–3), the ROFR / co-sale process is typically **run once at the tender's design phase** as part of the overall approval package. The company's board waives ROFR for the entire tender window; the preferred holders' ROFR is waived (or, where applicable, exercised in the aggregate) as part of the approval; the co-sale rights are either determined not to apply or waived by the preferred pool. Once these approvals are in place, individual participating employees do not each run their own ROFR / co-sale process — the tender's approval covers them.

The tender documentation must specifically disclose the ROFR / co-sale posture — participating employees need to know that the company and preferred holders have waived, so the transferred shares actually go to the tender buyer rather than being redirected.

### Founder secondaries

For a founder secondary (chapter 1), the ROFR / co-sale process is run **specifically for the founder's transaction**. The company's board considers the founder's specific transaction, the preferred holders receive a specific notice with the founder-specific details, and the process runs on the ROFR / co-sale agreement's specific timing. In a Shape A transaction (secondary sleeve on a round), the ROFR / co-sale process is typically folded into the round's approval package for administrative efficiency; the round's investors have already been engaged and can waive quickly. In a Shape B transaction (standalone), the ROFR / co-sale process runs on its own timeline and can take 30–60 days.

### Structured secondaries

Structured secondaries (chapter 4) present specific ROFR / co-sale challenges. A collateralised loan with a security interest in shares may or may not trigger ROFR (depending on whether the pledge is treated as a transfer under the specific agreement). A forward contract with future share delivery is typically treated as a transfer subject to ROFR at execution, at settlement, or both. Practitioner discipline: obtain specific-agreement analysis before executing any structured secondary and coordinate with the target's counsel on the notice, waiver, and (if applicable) co-sale mechanics.

### Platform transactions

Secondary-market platform transactions (chapter 5) run on the same ROFR / co-sale mechanics as any bilateral secondary transaction. The platforms have typically institutionalised the notice-and-waiver flow — Forge, EquityZen, and NPM all run the ROFR notice on behalf of the transacting seller, coordinate with the target's counsel or investor-relations function on the waiver process, and only close transactions after the ROFR / co-sale process has cleared. The specific target's ROFR / co-sale agreement governs; the platform administers the process.

For a target that is passive with respect to platforms (angle 1 in chapter 5), the ROFR / co-sale process may function as a *veto*: the target simply declines to waive its own ROFR or works to have the preferred pool exercise theirs, blocking the transaction. This is not always a defensible posture — the target's ROFR must be exercised in the target's own interest, not to punish the transferring shareholder — but where the target's fiduciary duties support declining the waiver, the mechanic can block platform transactions.

## Documentation and record-keeping

Every ROFR / co-sale process generates a specific documentation trail:

- The **transfer notice** delivered by the transferring shareholder.
- The **company response** (waiver, exercise, or determination not to exercise).
- The **preferred-holder responses** (waivers, exercises, silences treated as waivers).
- The **co-sale-holder responses** (waivers, exercises).
- The **cleared-to-transfer confirmation** from the target's counsel or secretary.
- The **transfer documentation** (stock-transfer form, board resolution acknowledging the transfer, cap-table update).

This documentation is maintained in the corporate records file (workstream 01 of a future data room, mod-105 chapter 1) and is available for future M&A or IPO diligence review. Failures in the ROFR / co-sale process — an unnoticed transfer, a missing waiver, a transferred share that was subject to unwaived co-sale rights — surface in future diligence as material findings that can complicate transactions.

Practitioner discipline: the target's corporate secretary or paralegal function maintains a specific ROFR / co-sale ledger tracking every notice, every response, every waiver, and every completed transfer, alongside the cap-table system of record.

## The failure modes — what happens when the process is not run cleanly

Several specific failure modes worth naming:

**Failure mode 1 — Unnoticed transfer.** A shareholder transfers shares without delivering the ROFR notice. The transfer is technically a breach of the ROFR / co-sale agreement, and the transferee may not have valid title to the shares. Curing this typically requires ratification by the affected holders (company and preferred) at a later date, and can involve consideration renegotiation if the affected holders would have exercised. In extreme cases, the transfer may need to be unwound.

**Failure mode 2 — Missed notice window.** A preferred holder's notice window closes and the holder does not respond, later claiming they did not receive the notice or were unable to respond in time. Absent a "silence is a waiver" provision or explicit no-response-treated-as-waiver language, this can create disputes about whether the transfer is properly cleared. Practitioner discipline: use tracked delivery (certified mail, email with delivery receipt) and maintain proof of receipt for every notice.

**Failure mode 3 — Co-sale exercise that reduces the transferring shareholder's realised sale.** A founder who expected to sell $5M ends up selling $833K after co-sale exercise. This is not a failure of the process — it is the process working as designed — but a failure of the transferring shareholder's expectations if they did not model this outcome. Practitioner discipline: at the design phase, discuss expected co-sale participation with the preferred holders and, if the co-sale is likely to be exercised, either resize the founder's transfer or negotiate a specific waiver in advance.

**Failure mode 4 — Non-participating preferred exercising ROFR to block a competitive-buyer transaction.** In a Shape B founder secondary or a platform transaction, a preferred holder may exercise the ROFR specifically to prevent a competitive third-party buyer from acquiring shares in the target. This is a legitimate use of the ROFR but changes the transaction's economics (the founder now sells to the preferred holder rather than the intended buyer, at the negotiated price). Practitioner discipline: identify the risk in advance and, where competitive-buyer risk is material, either negotiate the ROFR waiver in advance or accept the risk that the exercised ROFR reroutes the transaction.

**Failure mode 5 — 409A / disclosure lag.** A transfer completes cleanly but the target's 409A refresh has not been updated to reflect the transaction price. Subsequent option grants at the stale 409A price create IRS-liability exposure for those grantees. Practitioner discipline: coordinate the 409A refresh cadence with the transaction cadence (chapter 8).

## Summary

The ROFR / co-sale choreography is the underlying contractual apparatus governing every private-company-share transfer, whether founder secondary, tender offer, structured secondary, or platform transaction. The NVCA-pattern cascade — company first, preferred second, occasionally other holders third — with specific notice-and-response mechanics, drives who has the right to buy the shares proposed to be transferred. The co-sale (tag-along) right, typically parallel and distinct from the ROFR, gives specified holders the right to *participate* in the sale proportionally, potentially reducing the transferring shareholder's realised transaction. Notice mechanics, waiver mechanics, and co-sale mechanics all run on specific-agreement timing and require specific documentation. Failure modes include unnoticed transfers, missed notice windows, co-sale exercise reducing realised sales, ROFR exercise blocking competitive-buyer transactions, and 409A refresh lag. Practitioner discipline requires the target's counsel to run every transaction's ROFR / co-sale process cleanly, maintain the documentation trail, and coordinate with the target's investor-relations, finance, and 409A functions. Chapter 7 turns to the increasingly-common tender-in-connection-with-financing pattern that overlays the ROFR / co-sale mechanic on a simultaneous primary round.
