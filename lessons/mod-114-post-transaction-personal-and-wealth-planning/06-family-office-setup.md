# The Family Office Decision — Direct vs. Multi-Family Office vs. Outsourced-CIO

## Why this matters

At a certain wealth threshold — typically starting around $25M–$50M in investable assets and clearly by $100M+ — the family's financial life becomes complex enough that ad-hoc management stops working. Investment allocation across public equities, private markets, real estate, alternatives, and (for founder families) residual concentrated positions requires professional research and monitoring. Tax filings across multiple entities (a personal return, spousal returns, trust returns, foundation Form 990-PF, holding-company returns, LLC filings for real estate) require CPA capacity beyond what a general-practice CPA can efficiently deliver. Estate planning across the founder generation, the next generation, and (increasingly) the grandchild generation requires coordinated legal-and-tax review. Reporting for consolidated wealth across entities requires accounting software beyond consumer QuickBooks. Lifestyle-and-security services — travel, property management, security team, art acquisition and insurance, aviation, philanthropic advising — start to require dedicated staff.

The **family office** is the organisational structure that houses these functions under a coordinated management team. For families at the appropriate wealth threshold, running a family office (or engaging one) delivers material value in efficiency, control, integration, and — most importantly — coordinated decision-making across the many silos that would otherwise operate independently.

The choice is not binary. There are three primary organisational forms:

- **Single-Family Office (SFO)** — a dedicated internal team serving one family exclusively. Highest control, highest cost, highest customisation.
- **Multi-Family Office (MFO)** — a service firm serving a small number of client families with dedicated senior staff and shared infrastructure. Moderate control, moderate cost, professional-firm quality.
- **Outsourced-CIO (OCIO) + point solutions** — a lightweight structure in which the family engages an outsourced Chief Investment Officer plus specific point-solution vendors (CPA, estate attorney, wealth manager, accounting-software vendor) rather than building a dedicated team. Lowest control, lowest cost, requires more principal-time engagement.

This chapter installs the decision framework, the staffing pattern for each form, the technology stack that any of the forms depends on, and the governance model that makes the family office function well across the years. This is the module's downstream chapter — it presumes the wealth event has produced enough capital to justify the discussion, and it presumes the founder has some intent to build a permanent multigenerational wealth structure rather than to simply spend the proceeds down. Chapter 7 develops the counsel-hand-off discipline that the family office coordinates with over time.

> **Reminder: this is education, not investment or business-management advice.** The family-office decision is deeply personal, deeply fact-specific, and involves ongoing commitments (staffing, technology, real-estate-lease obligations if the office is standalone) that are difficult to unwind quickly. A live decision requires engagement with (i) a family-office-formation consultant, (ii) the family's current wealth manager and CPA (whose role changes materially if the family builds an SFO or engages an MFO), and (iii) family members (spouse and adult children) whose long-term involvement is central to the design's success. This chapter is what you read *before* you engage them so their time goes to judgment, not education.

## The wealth-and-complexity thresholds

The academic and practitioner-consulting literature offers rough thresholds for the different organisational forms. These are guides, not rules; the specific threshold depends on the complexity of the family's wealth, the number of family members involved, and the family's expectations for service level.

- **$5M–$25M investable wealth** — typically served by a private-client wealth manager (a fiduciary registered investment advisor or a private-bank private-client team). No family office in any real sense. The wealth manager coordinates with the family's CPA, estate attorney, and other advisors on an as-needed basis. Cost is typically fee-based on assets under management (0.5–1.0% annually) plus advisor fees.
- **$25M–$100M investable wealth** — the OCIO + point-solutions range. The family may engage an OCIO for investment strategy and reporting, plus specific vendors (CPA, estate attorney, bookkeeper). Total cost typically 0.5–1.5% of assets annually. Multi-family offices become viable in this range and are often the right choice for families whose complexity requires more than point-solution vendors but whose scale does not justify a dedicated SFO team.
- **$100M–$500M investable wealth** — the SFO or MFO range. An SFO becomes economically justifiable, though most families in this range still use an MFO for scale reasons. A typical SFO in this range has 3–8 dedicated staff; a typical MFO relationship has 1–3 dedicated senior staff plus shared infrastructure.
- **$500M+ investable wealth** — SFO is standard. Full-scale SFOs at this level have 10–50+ staff across investment, tax, legal, accounting, philanthropy, lifestyle, and security functions.
- **$1B+ investable wealth** — full institutional-scale SFO with a defined organisational structure, board of advisors, formal investment committee, and often a family council governance layer.

For most first-generation founder families at exit — where wealth may be $30M–$300M — the practical choice is between the OCIO + point-solutions structure and the MFO structure. The dedicated SFO becomes more common at $100M+, and it becomes the norm at $500M+.

## Single-Family Office (SFO) — dedicated internal team

An SFO is a dedicated internal organisation serving exclusively one family (or a small group of related families sharing a common wealth pool). The SFO is typically structured as a separate legal entity — often an LLC or S-corp — that employs the office's staff and holds the operating infrastructure.

### Typical staffing pattern

- **Chief Investment Officer (CIO).** Senior investment professional responsible for portfolio construction, manager selection (for public-market allocations, private-fund allocations, direct co-investments), risk monitoring, and investment reporting. Sourced from institutional investment management or from other family offices. Compensation typically $500K–$2M+ base plus performance participation for larger offices.
- **Chief Financial Officer (CFO) or Controller.** Senior finance professional responsible for consolidated financial reporting across all family entities (personal, trusts, LLCs, foundation, holding companies), coordination with external CPAs for tax filings, treasury management, and cash-flow forecasting. Compensation typically $300K–$800K.
- **Tax Specialist / Senior Tax Manager.** Ongoing income-tax, gift-and-estate-tax, and (for the foundation) excise-tax planning and compliance. Often coordinates with external CPA firms that handle the actual filings. Compensation typically $250K–$500K for a senior in-house tax role.
- **General Counsel.** In-house legal function. For families with substantial operating businesses, trusts, foundation, and real-estate portfolios, an in-house GC coordinating with external specialists is standard at the $300M+ scale.
- **Lifestyle and Security Lead.** Coordinates travel, property management, security team, art acquisition and insurance, aviation, and other lifestyle services. Sourced from executive-protection, hospitality, or family-office-lifestyle backgrounds.
- **Philanthropic Advisor / Foundation Director.** For families with a substantial private-foundation program, a dedicated philanthropic advisor coordinates grant strategy, program design, and foundation operations. May be internal or an outsourced program-officer engagement.
- **Junior investment / analyst staff.** For larger offices, additional investment analysts, tax accountants, bookkeepers, and administrative staff.

### Setup and operating costs

A dedicated SFO is expensive to establish and to operate. Rough estimates:

- **Setup costs** — office space (either rented or owned), technology deployment (see below), initial hiring, entity structuring, and initial policy documentation. Typically $500K–$2M+ for a 5–10 person office.
- **Annual operating costs** — total-compensation for staff plus benefits, office rent, technology-platform fees, professional-service fees (external CPAs, attorneys, appraisers, custodians), and general overhead. Typically $2M–$10M+ per year for a 5–10 person office.

For an SFO to be economically justifiable versus an MFO or OCIO alternative, the wealth pool must be large enough that the SFO's costs are a small percentage of total wealth (typically 0.5–1.0% of assets). At $50M of wealth, a $2M-annual SFO is 4% of assets — far too expensive. At $300M of wealth, the same $2M SFO is 0.67% — reasonable. At $1B, a $5M SFO is 0.5% — efficient.

### When an SFO is the right choice

- **Wealth above $250M–$500M** where the cost-as-percentage-of-assets is reasonable.
- **Complex operating businesses** (real-estate portfolios, family-controlled operating businesses, multiple private-fund LP positions) where dedicated internal expertise adds value beyond what an outsourced structure can efficiently deliver.
- **Multigenerational commitment** — the family intends to sustain the wealth across generations and views the SFO as a permanent institution rather than a temporary structure.
- **Privacy** — the family wants dedicated privacy over its financial affairs (an SFO with its own staff has stronger privacy protection than an MFO shared with other clients).
- **Values-driven investment or philanthropic programs** that require dedicated internal capacity (impact investing, mission-aligned philanthropy, specific ESG or governance criteria).

## Multi-Family Office (MFO)

An MFO is a professional-services firm serving a small number of client families (typically 20–200 client relationships) with dedicated senior staff for each client plus shared infrastructure (research, operations, technology, compliance).

### Structure and staffing

MFOs typically assign each client family a **primary relationship manager** (usually a senior wealth advisor with 10–25 years' experience) plus a supporting team. Larger client relationships get dedicated dedicated senior staff; smaller client relationships share staff across multiple clients. The MFO's shared infrastructure includes:

- **Investment research and manager-selection** — the MFO maintains internal research on public-market managers, private-fund managers, and direct-investment opportunities that all client families can access.
- **Operations and reporting** — the MFO maintains the accounting and reporting platform (typically Addepar, Masttro, or a similar platform), with client-specific reporting drawing on the same infrastructure.
- **Compliance and administration** — the MFO handles ongoing compliance with SEC, state, and tax registrations; client-onboarding due diligence; and other administrative overhead.

- **Bessemer Trust** — one of the oldest and largest MFOs, serving multiple generations of family clients. <https://www.bessemer.com/>

- **Aspiriant** — West Coast MFO with a fiduciary-focused approach. <https://www.aspiriant.com/>

- **BBH Wealth Partners (Brown Brothers Harriman)**, **Wilmington Trust**, **US Trust (Bank of America Private Bank)**, **JP Morgan Private Bank**, **Goldman Sachs Private Wealth Management** — additional large MFOs and private-bank private-client teams that operate on similar principles.

Selection considerations for an MFO:

- **Fit with the family's asset composition and complexity.** An MFO strong in real-estate operations may not be strong in illiquid tech-founder-concentration management; an MFO strong in venture-fund manager access may not have the tax capabilities the family needs.
- **Cultural fit.** MFOs are long-term relationships. Cultural fit with the senior team and with the firm's philosophy matters more than for a transactional vendor.
- **Fee structure.** MFOs typically charge fees as a percentage of assets under advisement (0.5–1.5% annually), or a combination of fixed-fee-plus-percentage. Compare total cost to alternatives.
- **Reporting and technology.** The MFO's reporting quality — whether it produces consolidated wealth reports that the family finds useful, whether the technology platform is accessible to the family — varies materially across firms.
- **Conflicts and independence.** Some MFOs are owned by or affiliated with banks that offer proprietary investment products; independent MFOs (or those with disclosed conflict policies) may avoid conflicts of interest that reduce the client's returns. Fiduciary standing (registered investment advisor) versus broker-dealer standing is a specific consideration.

### When an MFO is the right choice

- **Wealth in the $50M–$500M range** where dedicated SFO is not yet economically efficient but a fully-outsourced structure lacks the coordination the family needs.
- **Complex wealth** requiring integrated investment, tax, and estate planning without dedicated internal staff.
- **Family with limited operational bandwidth** — the founder or family members do not want to manage a dedicated internal office and prefer to engage a professional service firm.
- **Scale efficiency** — the MFO's shared infrastructure delivers institutional-quality research, reporting, and administration at a lower cost than the family could build in-house.

## Outsourced-CIO (OCIO) + point-solutions

The OCIO structure engages a dedicated outsourced Chief Investment Officer for the investment-strategy and reporting function, combined with individual point-solution vendors for the other family-office functions (CPA for tax, estate attorney for planning, wealth-management custodian, foundation counsel if applicable, lifestyle vendors if applicable).

### Notable OCIO firms

- **Cambridge Associates** — institutional-quality OCIO serving endowments, foundations, and family offices. <https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/>
- **Wilshire Advisors** — OCIO with strong institutional heritage. <https://wilshire.com/>
- **Callan** — investment-consulting firm offering OCIO services. <https://www.callan.com/>
- **Meketa Investment Group**, **Verus Advisory**, **Portfolio Advisors**, and other institutional-consulting firms — additional OCIO options.
- **Iconiq Capital**, **Aspiriant**, **Bessemer Trust** — some MFOs offer an OCIO-only engagement for clients who do not want the full MFO service package.

### When OCIO + point solutions is the right choice

- **Wealth in the $25M–$100M range** where dedicated SFO is far too expensive and MFO is more than the family needs.
- **Family with strong bandwidth for coordination** — the founder or a designated family member is willing to serve as the general-manager of the family's financial life, coordinating with the OCIO, CPA, estate attorney, and other vendors directly.
- **Investment-strategy-heavy needs** — the primary complexity is in the investment portfolio (multiple asset classes, private-fund positions, direct co-investments), and the tax / estate / lifestyle overhead is manageable through vendors.
- **Cost-sensitive** — the family wants professional investment management without the fully-loaded cost of a dedicated SFO or MFO.

## The technology stack

Any of the three organisational forms depends on a technology stack that consolidates the family's financial data across accounts, entities, and asset classes. The primary platforms:

- **Addepar** — the market-leading family-office reporting and analytics platform. Provides consolidated reporting across public-market accounts, private-fund positions, direct-investment positions, real-estate holdings, and other asset classes. Data-integration with major custodians (Schwab, Fidelity, JP Morgan, Goldman Sachs). Used by many MFOs and SFOs. <https://addepar.com/>
- **Masttro** — enterprise-scale family-office platform emphasising multi-entity accounting, consolidated reporting, and workflow. Often used by larger SFOs. <https://www.masttro.com/>
- **Eton Solutions** — family-office platform combining accounting, reporting, and workflow. Used by many mid-sized SFOs and MFOs. <https://eton-solutions.com/>
- **Ledgex** — accounting-and-reporting platform for family offices. <https://ledgex.com/>
- **Archway Platform (SEI Family Office Services)** — comprehensive family-office platform. <https://www.seic.com/family-office-services>
- **Sage Intacct** — multi-entity accounting platform used by many family offices for the core accounting layer (with reporting and analytics layered on top via Addepar or similar).
- **Investment-fund-administration platforms** — for families with substantial private-fund LP positions, specialised platforms (Backstop Solutions, Dynamo, and others) can complement Addepar/Masttro for LP-specific administration.

Selection considerations:

- **Data integration.** The platform must integrate with the family's custodians, alternative-investment administrators, and other data sources. Manual data entry is a permanent operational-overhead cost.
- **Multi-entity accounting.** For families with multiple legal entities (personal, trusts, foundation, LLCs, holding companies), the platform must handle multi-entity accounting cleanly. Simpler personal-finance software (Quicken, Personal Capital) does not scale to this complexity.
- **Reporting flexibility.** The platform's ability to produce family-specific reports (consolidated net worth, by-asset-class allocation, by-entity ownership, tax-year gain-and-loss, philanthropic-vehicle balances) affects the platform's usefulness.
- **Cost.** Platform licensing plus implementation typically runs $50K–$500K per year for a family-office deployment, depending on scale and complexity.

## Governance model

The family-office structure is not just a set of vendors and technology — it is a governance system that coordinates the family's financial life. A well-designed governance model includes:

### Family council

For families with multiple adult members (spouse, adult children, potentially grandchildren approaching adulthood), a **family council** is the deliberative body that makes joint decisions about wealth allocation, philanthropic priorities, next-generation involvement, and legacy planning. Family councils typically meet quarterly or twice-yearly, with an agenda covering investment performance review, philanthropic-program review, next-generation education / preparation, family-office operations, and any pending decisions.

Family-council design considerations:

- **Membership.** Who is a member? Adult children only, or all adult family members? What about spouses of children? Grandchildren once they reach adulthood?
- **Decision authority.** Is the family council advisory, or does it have binding authority over specific decisions? For an SFO, the family council typically has binding authority over major decisions (large capital deployments, major philanthropic commitments, family-office staffing).
- **Facilitator.** Many families engage an external facilitator for family-council meetings — a family-business advisor, a family-office consultant, or a family therapist experienced in wealth-family dynamics.

### Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

The **Investment Policy Statement** is the written document that defines the family's investment objectives, risk tolerance, asset-allocation targets, permitted and prohibited investments, rebalancing rules, and monitoring cadence. The IPS is the anchor document for the CIO's or OCIO's investment work.

A well-drafted IPS covers:

- **Return objectives** — nominal or real return targets over defined horizons.
- **Risk tolerance** — maximum drawdown tolerance, liquidity requirements, currency exposure limits.
- **Asset-allocation ranges** — target percentages for public equity, private equity, fixed income, real estate, alternatives, cash, with permissible ranges around each target.
- **Investment restrictions** — categories of investment the family will not make (ESG restrictions, industry restrictions, values-based restrictions).
- **Manager-selection criteria** — for external managers, the minimum criteria (track record length, AUM thresholds, cost caps, transparency requirements).
- **Rebalancing rules** — triggers for rebalancing (allocation drift thresholds, time-based rebalancing schedules).
- **Review cadence** — quarterly performance review, annual IPS review, longer-horizon strategic review.

### Next-generation development

For families intending to sustain wealth across generations, a **next-generation development programme** is the ongoing preparation of adult children (and grandchildren) to understand, engage with, and eventually lead the family's wealth. Common elements:

- **Financial literacy education** — starting in the teen years and continuing through adulthood, structured learning on investment principles, wealth planning, and philanthropy.
- **Family-office exposure** — inviting next-generation members to family-office meetings, board meetings of the family's philanthropic vehicles, and investment-review sessions.
- **Board or committee positions** — assigning specific responsibilities within the family-office governance structure (foundation board, investment committee, family council) to next-generation members as they mature.
- **External education and experience** — supporting next-generation members' education in finance, business, or philanthropy through structured programs (Wharton Family Office Program, Kellogg Family Enterprise Program, Institute for Private Investors, Family Firm Institute, and others).
- **Mentoring relationships** — pairing next-generation members with experienced advisors (family-office staff, external advisors, other principals from other families) for ongoing mentorship.

The next-generation programme is often the *most important* long-term determinant of whether the family's wealth survives across generations. The "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations" pattern — where wealth is created by the first generation, maintained by the second, and dissipated by the third — is the outcome most family offices are designed to prevent, and next-generation preparation is the primary mechanism.

### Philanthropic-strategy integration

For families with charitable vehicles (DAF, CRT, private foundation — see chapter 5), the family-office governance should integrate philanthropic strategy with the broader wealth-planning arc. Typical integration mechanisms:

- **Annual philanthropic-review meeting.** Reviews the past year's grants, the philanthropic-vehicle balances, the family's aggregate philanthropic commitment for the coming year, and any strategic changes.
- **Philanthropic-advisor engagement.** Either an internal philanthropic-advisor role (for larger foundations) or an external philanthropic-advisor engagement (for DAFs and smaller foundations) that provides program-strategy guidance and grant due diligence.
- **Coordination with income-tax planning.** The wealth-management team coordinates with the CPA to time charitable contributions in high-income years, maximise deduction benefits, and coordinate with the family's overall tax posture.
- **Next-generation involvement in philanthropy.** Many families use the philanthropic-vehicle governance as the primary next-generation development mechanism — assigning grant-making responsibility to adult children as they mature and demonstrate philanthropic engagement.

## Common failure modes — what to watch for

- **Over-building the SFO too early.** Founders sometimes establish full-scale SFOs at wealth levels where a lighter structure would suffice. The result is a high fixed-cost overhead that consumes returns without adding proportional value. The rule of thumb: start with the lightest structure that meets the family's actual needs, and upgrade only when complexity clearly demands it.
- **Under-investing in coordination.** OCIO + point-solutions structures fail when the coordination gap between the vendors is not managed. The wealth manager does not know about the estate attorney's recent trust changes; the CPA does not know about the philanthropic-vehicle contributions the wealth manager processed. The family (or a family-office coordinator) must actively coordinate across the vendors, or the coordination gap becomes an expensive failure mode.
- **Hiring the wrong CIO.** For an SFO, the CIO hire is the single most consequential staffing decision. A CIO whose skills match a prior institutional context (e.g., an endowment CIO) may not match the family's investment needs (which typically emphasise private-market and concentrated-position management more than endowment CIOs are used to). Careful CIO selection, ideally with an executive-search firm specialising in family-office CIO placements, is essential.
- **Under-thinking privacy and cybersecurity.** Family offices are attractive targets for financial fraud, kidnapping (for principals with international exposure), cyber-attack (for the office's technology platform), and social engineering. Investment in cybersecurity, physical security, and staff-training on fraud recognition is a specific operational discipline that many family offices under-invest in.
- **Confusing the family office with the family therapist.** Family offices manage wealth. They do not resolve family conflicts, mediate spousal disputes, or manage next-generation career development at a personal level. Families that expect the office to serve these roles set up disappointment. Explicit boundary-setting between the office's role and the family's personal roles is worth doing.
- **Not engaging the family in governance early.** Family-office governance benefits from early involvement of spouse and adult children. Waiting until the next generation is adults to introduce family-office concepts often produces adults who do not understand the wealth they will eventually manage. Ongoing exposure and involvement, starting in the teen years, is a specific investment in the family's long-term success.
- **Under-attending to the exit-of-a-key-family-member scenario.** What happens if the founder dies, becomes incapacitated, or divorces? The family-office structure must be resilient to these events. Estate documents, key-person insurance, succession plans for family-office leadership, and (for divorce planning) prenuptial and postnuptial agreements coordinated with the family-office structure are ongoing needs, not one-time actions.

## Summary

The family-office decision — dedicated SFO, MFO, or OCIO + point-solutions — is a wealth-and-complexity threshold decision balanced against the family's operational bandwidth, privacy needs, and long-term commitment. Wealth below $25M typically stays with a private-client wealth manager. Wealth $25M–$100M is often best served by OCIO + point-solutions or an MFO. Wealth $100M–$500M is the MFO or (increasingly) SFO range. Wealth $500M+ typically justifies a dedicated SFO with 5–20+ staff. Any structure depends on a technology stack (Addepar, Masttro, Eton Solutions, Ledgex, Archway) that consolidates the family's financial data across entities and asset classes. Governance — via a family council, an investment policy statement, a next-generation development programme, and philanthropic-strategy integration — is the mechanism that carries the wealth structure forward across generations. The specific decision requires engagement with a family-office-formation consultant, the family's existing wealth-management and tax advisors, and the family members whose long-term involvement is central to success. Chapter 7 closes the module with the counsel-hand-off discipline that keeps the whole apparatus functioning.
