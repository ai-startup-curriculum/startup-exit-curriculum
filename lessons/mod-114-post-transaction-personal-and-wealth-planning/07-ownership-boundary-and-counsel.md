# The Ownership Boundary — Education, Not Advice, and How to Hire Well

## Why this matters

Every chapter in this module ends with a reminder that the material is education, not personal tax, investment, legal, or estate advice. The reminder is not boilerplate. The IRC §1202 analysis in chapter 1 depends on a shareholder's specific acquisition dates, holding periods, state of residence, and corporate-level documentation; the stacking design in chapter 2 depends on gift-tax lifetime-exemption consumption, GST allocation, and trust-situs choices that interact in ways the shareholder cannot author alone; the Rule 10b5-1 plan in chapter 3 is adopted *by the insider* only after the issuer's audit committee has approved it and the insider's and the issuer's securities counsel have reviewed the specific document; the diversification plan in chapter 4 depends on wealth-manager suitability analysis, broker options approval, counterparty-term review, and CPA tax modelling; the charitable-vehicle plan in chapter 5 depends on §170 AGI projections, appraisal procurement, and foundation counsel's self-dealing analysis; the family-office decision in chapter 6 depends on long-horizon staffing, technology-vendor contracting, and family-governance design that the family cannot build without specialists.

In practice, almost every founder who reads this module arrives at the specialists they need either *before* they understand the shape of the decisions on the table (in which case the specialists bill substantially to educate them from scratch) or *after* they have already made a decision the specialists would have steered away from (in which case the specialists bill substantially to undo it). The purpose of this chapter — the module's closing synthesis — is to make the hand-off itself a discipline. By the end of this chapter you should know which specialist owns which part of the plan, when in the planning arc to engage each one, how to evaluate their fit, how to coordinate them so they do not duplicate work or contradict each other, and how to recognise when a specialist is operating outside their competence and needs to be supplemented or replaced.

The chapter has a secondary purpose: making explicit the **fiduciary-vs-broker distinction** that governs much of the wealth-management industry. A fiduciary advisor is legally required to act in the client's best interest; a broker-dealer under the suitability or Regulation Best Interest standard is not. The distinction matters most where incentives and advice could diverge — in investment-product recommendations, in the choice of exchange-fund provider, in the engagement of a prepaid-variable-forward counterparty, in the selection of a DAF sponsor, in the selection of a 10b5-1 plan broker, and in the sale of permanent-life-insurance structures that are commonly marketed into founder wealth events. A founder who does not know the distinction can end up with advisors whose compensation structure materially conflicts with the founder's interest without the founder understanding it.

> **Reminder: this chapter is education on how to hire well, not advice on which specific advisor to hire.** Advisor selection depends on the founder's specific fact pattern, geography, existing relationships, and personal fit considerations that cannot be captured in a general-purpose module. This chapter installs the vocabulary and the discipline; the specific-advisor selection belongs with the founder's existing trusted-relationship referral network and, where no such network exists, with the founder-specific due-diligence process this chapter describes.

## The specialist map — who owns what

A full post-transaction personal and wealth plan is authored and maintained by (typically) seven to twelve specialists working in coordination. The specialist map:

### Private-client tax attorney

**Owns:** the §1202 QSBS analysis, the §1045 rollover planning, the gift-tax and estate-tax strategy, the state-residency analysis, the pre-close contribution-and-stacking design, the private-foundation structure, the §501(c)(3) application, and the ongoing coordination of personal-tax strategy across the founder's full financial life.

**Credentials to look for:** JD with LLM in Taxation (preferred for complex planning), partner-level experience at a private-client-tax-focused firm (Withers, Kozusko Harris Duncan, McDermott Will & Emery private-client, Morgan Lewis private-client, Perkins Coie private-client, WilmerHale private-client, Day Pitney private-client, Loeb & Loeb private-client, Katten private-client, Proskauer private-client, Holland & Knight private-client — plus specific regional or boutique firms), a defined §1202 practice (the attorney should be able to walk you through recent §1202-related work without hesitation), and client references from other founder-exit situations.

**Engagement timing:** earliest possible — ideally 12–24 months before any contemplated exit transaction. The attorney's early-stage work (trust formation, pre-contribution structuring, QSBS attestation support) is what makes the pre-close window viable; engaging the attorney 3 weeks before signing an LOI reduces their options to compressed, higher-risk structures.

**Fee structure:** hourly billing at partner rates ($900–$1,500+/hour for top firms as of 2026; verify current market rates), with specific-project flat-fee or capped engagements available for discrete workstreams (trust drafting, foundation setup). For a complex founder situation, cumulative fees in the first year of engagement commonly range $50K–$300K+.

**Fiduciary posture:** attorneys are governed by state-bar rules and are agents of their clients. The private-client-tax attorney represents the founder (or the founder's trust, foundation, or other entity) — not any counterparty, broker, or product vendor.

### Estate attorney

**Owns:** the trust drafting (both for QSBS stacking and for broader estate planning), the will and ancillary documents (durable power of attorney, healthcare directive, HIPAA authorisation), the beneficiary designations across retirement accounts and life insurance, the GST-tax exemption allocation and planning, the trust-situs selection, the trustee selection and ongoing trustee relationship, and the integration of the estate plan with the family-office governance structure.

**Credentials to look for:** JD with LLM in Taxation or Estate Planning (preferred), American College of Trust and Estate Counsel (ACTEC) Fellow (a specific credential that practitioners recognise as a quality signal), partner-level experience at an estate-focused firm or an estate practice within a broader firm, and specific experience drafting non-grantor trusts for high-value QSBS stacking. Many private-client tax attorneys at the firms listed above are also qualified estate attorneys; some founders use the same attorney for both roles, others separate them.

**Engagement timing:** parallel with the private-client tax attorney. Trust drafting typically runs 4–12 weeks; situs establishment and trustee engagement add more time. Begin 12–24 months before any contemplated exit transaction.

**Fee structure:** hourly billing or flat-fee for specific documents (will, trust, foundation) at $700–$1,500/hour partner rates. Trust-drafting flat fees commonly $15K–$75K per trust depending on complexity. Ongoing retainer or hourly for post-formation maintenance.

**Fiduciary posture:** same as private-client tax attorney — governed by state-bar rules, representing the client.

### CPA (private-client / high-net-worth practice)

**Owns:** the ongoing personal-income-tax preparation (Form 1040 and all state returns), the trust-income-tax preparation (Form 1041 for non-grantor trusts), the foundation-tax preparation (Form 990-PF), the CRT-tax preparation (Form 5227), the estimated-tax-payment coordination across the year, the §1202 reporting on Form 8949 and Schedule D, the §1045 election and reporting, the gift-tax return preparation (Form 709), the carryforward tracking for charitable deductions and net operating losses, and the long-term tax-projection modelling that drives the personal-financial-planning decisions.

**Credentials to look for:** CPA license in the relevant state, personal-financial-specialist (PFS) credential (a specific AICPA credential that signals personal-financial-planning capability), partner-level experience at a private-client-focused CPA firm (Deloitte Private, EY Private, KPMG Private, PwC Private, Grant Thornton Private, BDO Private, Rehmann, Plante Moran — plus specific regional and boutique firms), and specific experience with founder-exit situations and the §1202 / §1045 / §664 mechanics.

**Engagement timing:** typically engaged earlier in the founder's career for ongoing tax filings; for the exit event, the CPA's role expands to include scenario modelling, estimated-tax planning, and coordinated filings across entities. Engage the CPA 12+ months before any contemplated exit so the pre-close year's filings and the gift-tax-return filings are coordinated.

**Fee structure:** hourly or fixed-fee per return at $350–$750+/hour partner rates. Annual retainer or per-engagement structures common. For a complex multi-entity founder situation, annual CPA fees commonly range $25K–$150K+.

**Fiduciary posture:** CPAs are governed by state-board rules and the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct. CPAs owe duties of confidentiality, integrity, and objectivity to their clients. CPAs who sign financial statements or opinions are subject to additional independence requirements.

### Wealth manager (investment advisor)

**Owns:** the investment strategy (asset allocation, manager selection, direct-investment selection, rebalancing), the concentration-risk analysis, the diversification plan (including coordination with the 10b5-1 plan for the sale-based toolkit and the counterparty engagement for the hedge-based toolkit), the ongoing portfolio monitoring and reporting, the coordination with the CPA on tax-aware investing (tax-loss harvesting, capital-gains timing), and the ongoing financial-planning scenario modelling (retirement, education funding, philanthropic funding).

**Credentials to look for:** Chartered Financial Analyst (CFA) and Certified Financial Planner (CFP) credentials (CFA signals investment-analysis capability; CFP signals financial-planning capability), registered as an investment advisor under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 (either as a Registered Investment Advisor (RIA) or as an Investment Advisor Representative (IAR) of an RIA firm), **fiduciary standing** (see fiduciary-vs-broker discussion below), and specific experience with founder-exit situations and concentrated-position management.

**Engagement timing:** can be engaged earlier for pre-exit financial planning, but the wealth manager's primary role becomes active at or just before exit, when the actual investment management decisions begin. Engage 6–12 months before exit to allow for RFP, selection, and relationship-establishment time.

**Fee structure:** typically a percentage of assets under management or advisement (0.5–1.5% annually), with lower rates on larger portfolios. Some firms offer fixed-fee engagements for financial planning without AUM-based investment management. For a founder with $50M+ of investable wealth, annual fees commonly $250K–$750K+.

**Fiduciary posture:** this is the critical distinction. See the dedicated section below.

### Securities counsel (the insider's own)

**Owns:** the insider's personal Rule 10b5-1 plan review, the Section 16 compliance review, the Rule 144 compliance for post-lock-up resales, the coordination with the issuer's securities counsel on insider-trading-policy compliance, and (for insiders involved in corporate transactions) the review of transaction-related obligations that affect the insider personally.

**Credentials to look for:** JD with specific securities-law practice experience (ideally at a firm that regularly represents public-company insiders — many of the Big Law securities-litigation and securities-compliance practices), partner-level experience with Rule 10b5-1 plans and the 2023 amendments, and the willingness to serve as *personal counsel* to the insider rather than as counsel to the issuer. Securities counsel is a specialised field — not every corporate attorney does it well.

**Engagement timing:** pre-IPO or at the first opportunity post-IPO for insiders of newly-public companies. For insiders of long-public companies, the engagement typically arises when the insider is adopting their first 10b5-1 plan or facing a specific disclosure or compliance question.

**Fee structure:** hourly at $800–$1,500/hour partner rates. 10b5-1 plan review typically a defined project; ongoing compliance support on an hourly or retainer basis.

**Fiduciary posture:** attorneys are governed by state-bar rules and represent the insider (not the issuer, not the broker, not the counterparty).

### Trust company or independent trustee

**Owns:** the ongoing administration of non-grantor trusts (and other irrevocable trusts), the independent trustee discretion required for the trust to be a separate taxpayer, the fiduciary duty of care and loyalty to the trust's beneficiaries, the record-keeping and reporting, the ongoing coordination with the trust's investment advisor and CPA, and (for trusts with distribution discretion) the specific distribution decisions.

**Credentials to look for:** Trust-powers charter (a bank or trust company authorised to serve as trustee in the relevant jurisdiction), long-standing reputation (trust companies often have multi-generation track records), specific capability in the jurisdictions used for trust situs (Delaware, South Dakota, Nevada, Wyoming, Alaska for the common favourable situs choices), and demonstrated willingness to exercise genuine independent discretion. Major providers include Northern Trust, Bessemer Trust, US Bank Private Wealth Management, BNY Mellon Wealth Management, JP Morgan Private Bank, specific trust companies in the favoured situs states (South Dakota Trust Company, Delaware Trust Company, and others), and smaller specialised firms.

**Engagement timing:** at trust formation. The trustee is a party to the trust document and must be in place before the trust is funded.

**Fee structure:** trust-administration fees are typically a percentage of trust assets (0.4–1.0% annually, with minimum annual fees of $5K–$25K per trust). Some trust companies charge additional fees for investment-management services (which the trust may or may not use the trustee for) and for specific administrative actions.

**Fiduciary posture:** trustees are fiduciaries by law to the trust's beneficiaries. The trustee's duties of care and loyalty are the strongest fiduciary duties in US law.

### Insurance specialist

**Owns:** the life-insurance analysis and procurement (if applicable), the disability-insurance analysis, the long-term-care insurance analysis, the umbrella and other liability insurance, and (for family-office structures) the directors-and-officers insurance, kidnap-and-ransom insurance, cyber-insurance, and other specialised coverages.

**Credentials to look for:** Chartered Life Underwriter (CLU) credential, Chartered Financial Consultant (ChFC) credential, independent-broker standing (not captive to a single insurance carrier), and specific experience with high-net-worth and family-office insurance structures.

**Engagement timing:** life-insurance-based estate-planning structures (irrevocable life insurance trusts, premium-financed life insurance) often require the policy to be in place before certain estate-planning triggers; engage earlier rather than later.

**Fee structure:** life-insurance brokers are typically compensated via policy commissions paid by the carrier (not directly by the client). This creates a potential conflict of interest — the broker's compensation depends on which policy is sold, and some policy types pay substantially higher commissions than others. Fee-based insurance consultants are available as an alternative, charging the client a direct fee for analysis independent of the eventual policy purchase.

**Fiduciary posture:** this is a specific area of concern. See the fiduciary-vs-broker discussion below.

### Foundation / philanthropic specialist

**Owns:** for founders with substantial private-foundation programmes, the ongoing foundation administration, grant-program design, due-diligence on specific grantees, coordination with foundation counsel on compliance questions, and (for larger foundations) the development of a defined philanthropic strategy. For founders without a private foundation but with substantial DAF or CRT activity, a philanthropic advisor can play a similar advisory role at smaller scale.

**Credentials to look for:** demonstrated experience in grant-making and nonprofit-sector navigation; often sourced from senior roles at major philanthropic institutions (Rockefeller Philanthropy Advisors, Arabella Advisors, Bridgespan, National Center for Family Philanthropy) or from senior roles at specific foundations or nonprofit organisations.

**Engagement timing:** at the point the family's philanthropic programme has grown beyond ad-hoc giving. For a private foundation at setup, immediately.

**Fee structure:** retainer or project-based engagement, varying with the scale of philanthropic activity.

**Fiduciary posture:** advisors to philanthropic organisations are typically retained as consultants or employees; the fiduciary relationship (if any) is with the foundation or vehicle, not with the individual donor.

### Family-office consultant and executive-search

**Owns:** the family-office organisational-design decisions (SFO vs. MFO vs. OCIO), the staffing-plan development, the CIO and other senior-staff search (for SFOs), the technology-platform selection, and the governance-model development (family council, IPS, next-generation programme).

**Credentials to look for:** firms specialising in family-office formation and operations consulting (Family Office Exchange (FOX), Cambridge Associates family-office advisory, Rockefeller Family Office advisory, specific boutique consulting firms); for executive search, firms with family-office placement specialisations (Russell Reynolds, Spencer Stuart, Egon Zehnder family-office practices, and specialist firms like Hilb Group, True Search, and others).

**Engagement timing:** at the inflection point when a dedicated family office becomes operationally necessary (typically $100M+ of wealth with sufficient complexity to warrant dedicated staff).

**Fee structure:** retainer or project-based for consulting; percentage-of-first-year-compensation for executive search (typically 25–33% of first-year total compensation for senior roles).

**Fiduciary posture:** consultants are retained advisors; the fiduciary relationship is contractual, defined by the engagement letter.

## The fiduciary-vs-broker distinction

The single most consequential piece of due-diligence in advisor selection is confirming the advisor's **fiduciary posture**. The regulatory framework in the US is a patchwork, and the standards that apply to different advisor types produce materially different alignment with the client's interest.

### The two primary standards

- **Fiduciary standard.** A fiduciary advisor is legally required to act in the client's best interest — placing the client's interest above the advisor's own, avoiding conflicts of interest (or disclosing them fully and obtaining informed consent), and exercising care, skill, and diligence. Investment advisors registered under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 are fiduciaries to their clients under both federal law (through the Supreme Court's Capital Gains Research Bureau decision and subsequent SEC interpretations) and state fiduciary-duty law. Attorneys, CPAs, and trustees are fiduciaries by profession.
- **Suitability standard / Regulation Best Interest.** Broker-dealers and their registered representatives are not fiduciaries. They are governed by the SEC's Regulation Best Interest (adopted 2019, effective June 2020), which requires them to act in the retail customer's best interest when making a recommendation but allows them to consider their own interests and to recommend products that pay them higher commissions, so long as the recommendation satisfies specific best-interest, care, disclosure, and conflict-of-interest obligations. Regulation Best Interest is a step up from the pre-2020 pure "suitability" standard but is widely regarded as weaker than a true fiduciary standard. The regulatory distinction has specific operational consequences: a broker-dealer may legally recommend a higher-commission product so long as it is appropriate for the customer; a fiduciary advisor cannot.

### Dual-registered advisors

A specific and common complication: many advisors at wirehouses, private banks, and other large wealth-management organisations are **dual-registered** — they are registered as investment-advisor representatives (fiduciary) for some services and as broker-dealers (not fiduciary) for others. The same person, in the same meeting, may be acting as a fiduciary when discussing the firm's advisory services and as a broker when recommending a specific product. The regulatory separation is maintained via different account types, different fee structures, and different disclosure documents (Form ADV for advisory services, Form CRS for brokerage services).

The practical implication for a founder: ask the advisor directly, in writing, whether they are acting as a *fiduciary* with respect to the specific service being discussed, and request the specific disclosure documents (Form ADV Part 2A, Form CRS, and any specific-product disclosure). If the advisor cannot or will not provide an unambiguous answer, that itself is a signal.

### Where the distinction matters most

Specific areas where the fiduciary-vs-broker distinction has material consequences:

- **Investment-product recommendations.** Mutual funds, annuities, private placements, and structured products pay different commissions. A broker-dealer may recommend a specific product in part because of the compensation structure; a fiduciary advisor cannot.
- **Permanent life insurance.** Life-insurance products — specifically variable universal life, indexed universal life, and whole life — are commonly marketed into founder wealth events as estate-planning or tax-deferral vehicles. The commissions on these products are substantial (often 70–120% of first-year premium plus trailing commissions), and the products are complex. A fiduciary analysis of whether a permanent-life-insurance product is in the founder's best interest is often very different from a commissioned-broker recommendation.
- **Exchange-fund and prepaid-variable-forward-contract placements.** These are private-placement securities with significant fees embedded in the structure. A fiduciary advisor's recommendation to use an exchange fund will consider all-in cost, alternative strategies, and the specific fit to the founder's situation; a broker's recommendation may be influenced by placement fees or revenue-sharing arrangements.
- **DAF-sponsor and investment-fund-selection.** Some DAF sponsors are owned by or affiliated with investment firms whose funds are among the available investment options. A fiduciary advisor will consider whether the sponsor's affiliated-fund menu offers competitive performance and cost; a broker-sponsored recommendation may default to the affiliated-fund menu.
- **10b5-1 plan broker selection.** Multiple broker-dealers offer 10b5-1 plan administration (Fidelity, Morgan Stanley at Work, E*TRADE Corporate Services, Charles Schwab Stock Plan Services, and others). Each has different fee structures, execution-quality metrics, and interaction with the founder's broader financial life. A fiduciary advisor can help the founder compare options; a broker-dealer recommending its own platform has an obvious conflict.
- **Credit and lending products.** Many wealth-management firms offer securities-based lending, mortgages, and other credit products. A fiduciary advisor will consider whether specific credit products are in the client's best interest; a broker-dealer recommending its own firm's credit products has a conflict.

### The practical discipline

Three operational rules:

1. **Ask every prospective advisor, in writing, whether they are acting as a fiduciary.** The answer should be clear and specific. Vague responses ("we always put clients first", "we have the highest ethical standards") are not fiduciary-standard answers.
2. **Prefer fee-only RIAs for the primary wealth-management role.** Fee-only (as distinct from fee-based) RIAs do not accept commissions, kickbacks, or revenue-sharing from product vendors. Their only compensation comes from fees paid directly by the client. This structure eliminates most of the conflicts that affect broker-dealer recommendations.
3. **Separate the advisory and product-sales functions where possible.** Use fiduciary advisors for recommendations; use product-specific vendors (brokers, insurance brokers, specialised placement agents) for execution of specific products, with the understanding that the product vendors are not fiduciaries.

## The coordination problem — making the specialists work as a team

Even when every specialist is well-selected, the specialists do not automatically coordinate. The private-client tax attorney does not know what the wealth manager is doing with the hedge-structure recommendation; the CPA does not know the estate attorney has drafted new trusts since the last tax year; the family-office consultant does not know the foundation specialist's grant-program revisions; the securities counsel does not know the wealth manager has placed a prepaid-variable-forward position that may trigger Form 4 disclosure. The coordination gap is where the plan degrades.

The common failure modes:

- **Duplicative work.** Two specialists perform the same analysis, each billing the client, because neither knows the other has already done it.
- **Contradictory advice.** Specialists give the client conflicting recommendations because they are not in dialogue with each other.
- **Dropped responsibilities.** A specific task (e.g., the §1045 election filing, the Form 709 preparation, the trustee-notification of a trust distribution) falls between specialists because no one owned it.
- **Delayed decisions.** Decisions requiring multi-specialist input stall because no one coordinates the input-gathering.
- **Lost context.** When a specialist retires or departs, the client's context goes with them if the other specialists did not share the context.

The coordination mechanisms that address these failures:

### The coordinator role

Someone must be the designated **coordinator** of the specialist team. The coordinator's role is to maintain the shared context, convene the team when multi-specialist decisions are pending, ensure that each specialist knows what the others are doing, and track open items across the team. The coordinator is not the client's advisor on any specific question — the coordinator's job is to make the team function.

The coordinator can be:

- **The founder** (or the founder's spouse, or a designated family member). This works for simpler situations with 3–5 specialists but becomes unwieldy as the specialist count and the complexity grow.
- **A dedicated family-office staff member** (CFO or Chief of Staff). This is standard for SFOs and for larger MFO relationships.
- **A designated external coordinator**, often a senior wealth manager or the lead private-client-tax attorney. For OCIO + point-solutions structures, this is a common arrangement — one of the point-solution advisors is designated as the primary coordinator.

Whoever the coordinator is, the role must be explicit, documented in writing, and known to all the specialists. A coordinator-by-default (no one explicitly designated, each specialist assuming someone else is coordinating) produces the worst coordination outcomes.

### The annual all-hands review

At least annually, the full specialist team should convene (in person or by videoconference) for a comprehensive review. The agenda:

- Review the year's financial results and the year's major transactions.
- Review each specialist's work during the year and the specialist's current open items.
- Identify cross-specialist dependencies and coordination needs for the coming year.
- Review the client's current financial-planning scenarios and update the long-term plan.
- Confirm each specialist's engagement and compensation for the coming year.

The all-hands review is where the specialists see each other, confirm the shared context, and identify gaps. For complex founder situations, this is the single most valuable coordination mechanism.

### The shared-document repository

A shared repository — accessible to all specialists on the team, under appropriate security — maintains the current state of the client's financial life. Contents typically include:

- Current entity structure (personal, trusts, foundation, LLCs, holding companies, operating businesses).
- Current estate documents (will, trusts, durable power of attorney, healthcare directive, beneficiary designations).
- Current investment-policy statement (if applicable).
- Current tax returns and tax-planning memos.
- Current QSBS attestation letters and §1045-election records.
- Current 10b5-1 plan and other securities-compliance documents.
- Current insurance policies.
- Current family-office organisational chart and compensation structure (if applicable).

The repository is maintained by the coordinator or by a dedicated family-office staff member. Platforms used for the purpose include Addepar and Masttro (for the financial-reporting layer), DocSend / Dropbox Business / specific family-office platforms (for the document layer), and secure-email or encrypted-messaging platforms (for the ongoing communications layer).

### The engagement-letter discipline

Each specialist should have a clear engagement letter defining:

- The scope of the engagement (what the specialist is retained to do).
- The fee structure (hourly, flat-fee, retainer, percentage of assets).
- The communications protocol (how the specialist communicates with the client, with other specialists, and how often).
- The confidentiality rules (what the specialist can share with other specialists, with family members, etc.).
- The term and termination provisions.

Engagement letters prevent the ambiguity-driven scope creep, surprise billing, and communication gaps that otherwise degrade the specialist relationships over time.

## Red flags — when to replace or supplement a specialist

Specific signals that a specialist is operating outside their competence or is otherwise not well-suited to the client's situation:

- **Confident answers in uncertain areas.** §1202 state-conformity, §643(f) multiple-trust aggregation, §1259 constructive-sale structuring, the SEC's 2023-amendment application to specific plan features — these are areas where practitioner opinion divides. A specialist offering unqualified certainty on these questions, without reference to the specific authorities or to the open interpretive questions, is likely overconfident.
- **No references.** A specialist who cannot provide client references for similar engagements is a risk. Request references and actually call them.
- **Reluctance to coordinate.** A specialist who resists the all-hands review, declines to engage with other specialists on the team, or insists on keeping the client's information siloed is impairing the coordination that makes the plan work.
- **Fee opacity.** A specialist who cannot or will not provide a clear fee estimate or ongoing billing summary is a problem. Fee transparency is a baseline professional discipline.
- **Product-pushing.** A specialist (particularly a wealth manager or an insurance specialist) who pushes specific products without a clear process for comparing alternatives is a red flag. Fiduciary advisors demonstrate their process; non-fiduciary advisors often skip the comparison.
- **Non-response to specific questions.** A specialist who gives generic answers to specific questions, or who cannot explain the specific authorities (statutes, cases, SEC releases) supporting their recommendations, is operating from template rather than from specific analysis.
- **Firm-captive recommendations.** A specialist at a firm that offers proprietary products (an investment-management firm with proprietary funds, a bank with proprietary credit products) who always recommends the firm's products has an obvious bias.
- **Scope mismatch.** A specialist whose specific-engagement history does not match the client's situation (a general-practice tax attorney retained for a complex §1202 stacking case, a retail wealth manager retained for a $200M concentrated-position situation) is at risk of operating outside their competence. Match the specialist's practice history to the client's needs.

Replacing a specialist is not usually traumatic — it can be, but with proper documentation and coordination most transitions are manageable. The cost of staying with a mismatched specialist is almost always higher than the cost of transitioning.

## The ongoing-maintenance arc

Post-transaction personal and wealth planning is not a one-time event. The initial plan — the §1202 analysis, the stacking structure, the 10b5-1 plan, the diversification programme, the charitable-vehicle commitments, the family-office setup — is the starting point. The ongoing arc:

- **Annual tax-filing cycle.** CPA leads; private-client tax attorney, estate attorney, wealth manager, foundation specialist contribute as needed. The annual cycle is the most reliable touchpoint for all the specialists.
- **Life events.** Marriage, divorce, children, grandchildren, deaths, inheritances, and other major life events all trigger plan updates. Each event is a specific review cycle.
- **Legislative changes.** The federal tax law changes frequently; the state tax law changes more frequently. Every material change requires plan review.
- **Market events.** Major market moves can trigger rebalancing, change concentration metrics, and shift the specific tax-planning calculus.
- **Family-office evolution.** The family office structure evolves over time — adding staff, upgrading technology, changing governance, bringing next-generation members into the structure.
- **Multigenerational transition.** At some point, the founder generation hands off wealth management responsibility to the next generation. Preparing for and managing this transition is one of the most consequential long-term tasks.

The specialist team sustains the plan across this arc. Specific team members may change (CPAs retire, wealth managers change firms, estate attorneys retire); the coordination discipline persists.

## The humility clause

A founder who has read this module in full now knows substantially more about personal-wealth planning than most founders at the same stage. The humility clause: that knowledge is a *floor* for engaging specialists, not a *ceiling*. Specific statutes have exceptions this module does not cover; specific state laws have variations this module does not catalogue; specific case law has developments this module does not cite; specific IRS guidance has nuances this module elides. For every topic in this module, there is a specialist whose life's work is that topic, and who knows substantially more about it than any founder reasonably can.

The point of the module is to let the founder arrive at those specialists *already understanding the shape of the decisions on the table* — not to let the founder think they can skip the specialists. A specialist's time is expensive; a specialist's error is more expensive; a founder who tried to self-execute a complex personal-wealth plan is most expensive of all.

Hire well. Coordinate carefully. Review annually. Replace when the specialist's capability no longer matches the client's needs. The specialists do the specialist work; the client (with the coordinator's help) makes sure the specialists work as a team.

## Summary

The post-transaction personal and wealth plan is authored and maintained by a team of 7–12 specialists — private-client tax attorney, estate attorney, CPA, wealth manager, securities counsel, trust company or independent trustee, insurance specialist, foundation / philanthropic specialist, and (for larger structures) family-office consultant and executive-search firm. Each specialist owns a specific portion of the plan; the plan degrades when the specialists do not coordinate. The fiduciary-vs-broker distinction is the single most consequential piece of due-diligence in advisor selection — fee-only RIAs for the primary wealth-management role, product-vendor engagements with eyes open on the compensation structure. Coordination mechanisms (a designated coordinator, an annual all-hands review, a shared-document repository, engagement-letter discipline) make the specialist team function as a team rather than a collection of siloed advisors. Red flags for specialist replacement include overconfidence in uncertain areas, fee opacity, product-pushing, firm-captive recommendations, and scope mismatch. The ongoing-maintenance arc runs across annual tax cycles, life events, legislative changes, market events, family-office evolution, and multigenerational transitions. The humility clause: this module provides the floor for engaging specialists, not a ceiling. Hire well, coordinate carefully, and keep the plan alive across the decades after the transaction.
