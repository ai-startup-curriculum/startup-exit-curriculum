# Rule 10b5-1 Trading Plans — Authoring a Post-IPO Founder Sale Programme That Survives 2023 Amendments

## Why this matters

A newly-public founder who wants to sell stock after the IPO lock-up expires faces a paradox: they typically have more material non-public information (MNPI) about the company than any other insider, and yet they also have a legitimate need to diversify concentrated exposure and to fund life decisions (a home purchase, a philanthropic commitment, an angel-investment portfolio, an estate-planning transfer). Trading on MNPI is a federal securities-fraud crime under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5. Trading *without* MNPI is legal, but a founder who trades in the open window between earnings releases faces the constant challenge of establishing, after the fact, that they were not in possession of MNPI at the time of each trade.

**Rule 10b5-1** is the SEC's affirmative-defence framework that resolves this paradox. Adopted in 2000 (17 CFR §240.10b5-1), it lets an insider adopt, in advance and at a time when they are *not* in possession of MNPI, a written trading plan that specifies future trades according to a formula or set of triggers. Trades executed under the plan are afforded an affirmative defence against Rule 10b-5 insider-trading liability — the insider can point to the plan's pre-adoption date and terms as evidence that the trades were not made "on the basis of" MNPI. For a founder who wants to sell stock on an ongoing basis after the IPO, a well-authored 10b5-1 plan is the standard mechanism.

The 2023 SEC amendments to Rule 10b5-1 (adopted December 14, 2022, effective February 27, 2023 for most provisions, with disclosure provisions phased in during 2023) materially tightened the rule after years of criticism that pre-2023 plans were being used to trade around MNPI. The amendments introduced cooling-off periods, self-certification requirements, no-overlapping-plans restrictions, single-trade-plan-per-12-months limitations, and expanded disclosure obligations. A 10b5-1 plan authored today must satisfy the amended rule; a plan authored under the pre-2023 rule that survived into the amendment date must either be terminated or must be modified to comply.

This chapter installs the anatomy of an amended Rule 10b5-1 plan, the plan-design vocabulary, the disclosure-and-audit-committee-approval discipline, and the interaction with the IPO lock-up expiry and staggered-release provisions.

> **Reminder: this is education, not securities-law advice.** Rule 10b5-1 plan authoring for a specific insider requires securities counsel (the issuer's outside counsel and, ideally, the insider's personal counsel), the issuer's insider-trading-policy administrator, the broker-dealer that will execute the plan, and — for the disclosure interaction — the issuer's audit committee or a designated committee. A plan authored without that specific-fact-pattern engagement is not a plan; it is a template. This chapter is what you read *before* you engage counsel so their time goes to judgment, not education.

## The pre-amendment problem — what the 2023 amendments were solving

Before the 2023 amendments, Rule 10b5-1 provided an affirmative defence with relatively few operational constraints. In practice, insiders had exploited the lack of constraints in several patterns that the SEC found problematic:

- **Very short cooling-off periods.** Insiders could adopt a plan on Monday and execute trades under it on Tuesday, giving the plan essentially no independence from the insider's contemporaneous knowledge.
- **Overlapping plans.** Insiders could maintain multiple simultaneous plans and cherry-pick which trades to execute (or which plan to terminate) based on updated MNPI.
- **Single-trade plans.** An insider could adopt a plan specifying a single trade of a large block on a specific future date, providing minimal formulaic independence from contemporaneous MNPI.
- **Serial adoption-and-termination.** Insiders could adopt a plan, wait to see if the price moved favourably, and terminate the plan (or amend it) if the price moved against expectations.
- **Limited disclosure.** Investors and the market had limited visibility into plan adoptions, terminations, and modifications.

Academic research (notably work by Cohen, Malloy, and Pomorski published around 2012, and later work by others) documented systematically higher returns on 10b5-1 sales than on unplanned insider sales — suggesting insiders were selectively using the affirmative defence to trade with informational advantage. The 2023 amendments were the SEC's response.

## The amended Rule 10b5-1 — the plan requirements

To qualify for the affirmative defence under the amended rule, a plan must satisfy each of the following conditions (17 CFR §240.10b5-1(c) as amended):

### 1. Written plan, adopted at a time without MNPI

The plan must be a *written* plan that specifies, or provides a written formula for determining, the amount, price, and date of each trade. The plan must be adopted at a time when the insider is *not* in possession of material non-public information. The pre-2023 requirement was similar; the amended rule adds the self-certification requirement below to make the "no MNPI at adoption" test operational.

### 2. Cooling-off period before first trade

For **officers and directors** (as defined in the amended rule — generally Section 16 officers and directors), the plan must include a cooling-off period between the plan's adoption or modification date and the first trade under the plan. The cooling-off period is the *later* of:

- 90 days after the plan's adoption or modification; or
- Two business days after the disclosure of the issuer's financial results for the fiscal quarter in which the plan was adopted or modified (via Form 10-Q, Form 10-K, or a foreign-private-issuer equivalent).

The cooling-off is capped at 120 days regardless of the earnings-release timing — i.e., the 90/2-business-days rule can extend the cooling-off past 90 days if the earnings release is delayed, but not past 120 days.

For **issuers** (the corporation itself executing buybacks under 10b5-1), the cooling-off period is 30 days.

For **non-officer, non-director insiders** (e.g., employees below the officer level), the amended rule does not impose a mandatory cooling-off period, though issuers typically impose one via internal policy for consistency.

### 3. Self-certification of good faith and no MNPI

At the time of plan adoption or modification, officers and directors must *self-certify* in the plan document that:

- They are not aware of any material non-public information about the issuer or its securities; and
- They are adopting the plan in good faith and not as part of a plan or scheme to evade Rule 10b-5.

The self-certification is a specific written representation in the plan document. Its purpose is to make the pre-adoption MNPI condition operational — the insider is on the record with a specific certification that can be examined in any subsequent enforcement or litigation matter.

### 4. Good-faith operation

The insider must have acted in good faith with respect to the plan throughout its life — not merely at adoption. The amended rule expanded the good-faith requirement from a good-faith adoption test to a *continuing* good-faith operation test. An insider who influences plan trades, terminates the plan strategically after adverse information, or otherwise operates the plan opportunistically loses the affirmative defence.

### 5. No overlapping plans

An officer or director may not have more than one 10b5-1 plan in effect at the same time for the same class of securities of the issuer, with narrow exceptions:

- **Sell-to-cover plans.** A separate plan solely for tax-withholding sales in connection with equity-compensation vesting (a common mechanism used by issuers for withholding on RSU vesting) does not count against the single-plan limit.
- **Consecutive plans.** An insider may adopt a new plan whose first trade date is *after* the last trade of the current plan — i.e., non-overlapping successive plans are permitted.
- **Terminated plan replacement.** If an existing plan is terminated without any trades having occurred, a new plan can be adopted, subject to a new cooling-off period.

The no-overlapping-plans restriction significantly curtails the cherry-picking pattern that motivated the amendments.

### 6. Single-trade-plans limited to one per 12 months

An officer or director may adopt a plan designed to execute a *single trade* (one transaction) not more than once in any 12-month period. The exceptions are sell-to-cover plans and plans with multiple trades. The intent is to prevent insiders from using a series of single-trade plans to work around the plan-design formalism.

### 7. Plan adopted outside a blackout window

Although not a Rule 10b5-1 requirement per se, plans are effectively required to be adopted during an issuer-designated open trading window (i.e., outside quarterly earnings blackouts and event-driven blackouts). Issuer insider-trading policies almost universally impose this constraint, and it is a common condition of audit-committee approval.

## Disclosure requirements — Item 408 of Regulation S-K

The 2023 amendments added disclosure requirements to Regulation S-K:

- **Quarterly disclosure of plan adoption / termination / modification** — Item 408(a) of Regulation S-K requires issuers to disclose in Form 10-Q (and Form 10-K for the last fiscal quarter) whether any of their directors or Section 16 officers adopted, terminated, or modified a 10b5-1 plan during the quarter, and to describe the material terms (name and title of the insider, plan adoption / termination / modification date, plan duration, aggregate number of securities subject to the plan). Note: individual trade prices are not required to be disclosed.
- **Annual disclosure of insider-trading policies** — Item 408(b) requires issuers to file their insider-trading policies as an exhibit to Form 10-K (or state that they do not have such policies).
- **Item 402(x) — equity-award timing disclosure.** Related amendments to Item 402 require disclosure of timing of equity awards near the release of MNPI.

The disclosure regime is a structural check on plan behaviour — a plan modification that in the pre-2023 era might have gone unnoticed now appears in the next Form 10-Q, visible to plaintiffs' counsel, class-action monitors, and the market.

## Plan-design vocabulary — how insiders shape a real plan

A defensible 10b5-1 plan is not a bare grant of authority to a broker to sell shares. It specifies, in detail, the timing and quantity of each trade or the formula that determines them. The design choices:

### Regular-cadence sales

The simplest and most defensible pattern: a fixed schedule of sales at regular intervals. For example: "Broker shall sell 10,000 shares on the first trading day of each calendar month for 24 months, beginning [start date after cooling-off]." Regular-cadence plans are formulaic, mechanical, and hard to attack as opportunistic. They are the default recommendation for most founders whose primary objective is orderly diversification over a defined horizon.

### Staggered releases

For a founder whose position is very large and whose lock-up release is staggered (a common IPO lock-up design — see below), the plan may stagger the sale schedule to match. For example: "Broker shall sell 5% of the founder's shares in month 1, an additional 5% in month 3, an additional 10% in month 6, ..." Staggered-release plans align the sale schedule with the lock-up release structure and provide the market with predictable expectations of insider supply.

### Price-condition triggers

The plan can include price conditions that permit or require sales at specific price levels. For example: "Broker shall sell up to 10,000 shares on any trading day the closing price on the prior trading day was at or above $X." Or: "Broker shall sell up to 20,000 shares on any trading day the closing price was at or above $Y." Price-condition triggers let the founder participate in upside without violating the "no discretion after adoption" principle — the trigger is formulaic, the broker executes when the market crosses the trigger, and the founder has no ongoing decision role.

Price-condition triggers have to be carefully drafted to avoid appearing opportunistic. A plan with a single high price trigger and no other trades looks like a market-timing bet; a plan with a base regular-cadence schedule plus modest price triggers looks like a diversification plan with upside participation. Plaintiffs' counsel and the SEC pattern-match on plan design; a plan that looks like a market-timing bet is at higher risk of loss-of-defence attack.

### Volume-participation formulas

For very large positions, the plan may use a volume-participation formula that ties daily sales to a percentage of the day's trading volume. For example: "Broker shall sell up to 10% of the daily reported trading volume, up to a maximum of X shares per day." Volume-participation formulas minimise market-impact of the founder's sales and are commonly used for positions where a fixed daily quantity would materially move the stock.

### Event-triggered pauses

The plan should specify events that pause the plan without terminating it — most importantly, the issuer's regular blackout periods (quarterly earnings blackouts, event-driven blackouts). A well-drafted plan pauses automatically during blackout periods without requiring the insider or broker to take affirmative action. The affirmative-defence position depends on the plan operating mechanically, without discretion; a plan that requires the insider to instruct the broker to pause is doing something the amended rule frowns on.

### Termination provisions

The plan should specify when and how it terminates — typically at the end of a defined period (e.g., 12–24 months from the first trade date), on the sale of a specified aggregate number of shares, or on the insider's death or incapacity. Termination *by the insider* is heavily disfavoured — a plan that the insider can terminate at will loses much of its affirmative-defence value. In practice, plans typically permit termination only for defined events (a change of control of the issuer, a major life event of the insider) or after a minimum duration.

## The IPO lock-up interaction

An IPO lock-up typically prevents pre-IPO shareholders (including founders, officers, directors, and early investors) from selling shares for a defined period after the IPO date — historically 180 days, though many recent IPOs have used staggered releases (a portion released after 90 days if certain price conditions are met, the balance after 180 days, and so on). The lock-up is imposed by the underwriters via the underwriting agreement and by direct lock-up agreements signed by the pre-IPO shareholders.

A 10b5-1 plan for a founder cannot execute sales during the lock-up (the lock-up bars sales regardless of the plan). But the plan *can be adopted* during the lock-up, with the first-trade date set to the day after the lock-up expires (or the first business day after that satisfies the 10b5-1 cooling-off period, whichever is later).

The design considerations:

- **Timing plan adoption relative to earnings and MNPI.** The plan should be adopted during an open window, ideally shortly after an earnings release, when the founder can defensibly represent that they are not in possession of material MNPI. Adopting the plan immediately post-IPO (before the first quarterly release as a public company) has some challenges around what MNPI the founder holds; adopting shortly after the first post-IPO earnings release is often the cleaner choice.
- **Cooling-off calculation.** The 90/2-business-days-after-earnings cooling-off applies at adoption. If the plan is adopted the week of the first-quarter earnings release, the cooling-off is 90 days from adoption. If adopted 2 weeks before an earnings release, the cooling-off is 2 business days after the earnings release plus whatever remains of the 90-day count.
- **Coordination with the lock-up release schedule.** For a staggered-release lock-up, the plan can be structured so trades are scheduled to coincide with each release milestone. Some plans stagger sales across multiple release events; others concentrate sales in the early release window to minimise ongoing overhang risk.
- **The "early-release" trigger interaction.** Some staggered-release lock-ups include an early-release provision (e.g., if the stock trades above a defined price for a defined number of days). The 10b5-1 plan should account for the possibility of an early release; a common approach is to include conditional first-trade dates that trigger on either the scheduled release or the early-release event, whichever is earlier.

## Adoption process — the audit-committee-approval discipline

Most public-company insider-trading policies require insiders to obtain approval from a designated committee (typically the audit committee or a subcommittee thereof) before adopting or modifying a 10b5-1 plan. The approval process serves multiple purposes:

- **Confirmation that the adoption timing is within an open window.** The committee confirms that no undisclosed material events are pending that would put the insider in possession of MNPI.
- **Review of plan terms.** The committee reviews the plan design (cadence, price triggers, termination provisions) for consistency with the issuer's insider-trading policy and for absence of obvious opportunistic features.
- **Documentation.** The committee's approval creates a written record supporting the "no MNPI at adoption" position, complementing the insider's self-certification.
- **Compliance with disclosure obligations.** The committee coordinates with the issuer's SEC-disclosure function to ensure the plan adoption is timely reflected in the next Form 10-Q under Item 408(a).

For a founder-insider, engagement with the audit committee is not adversarial — it is the standard governance discipline the plan needs. Founders should expect a 2–4 week review timeline from plan drafting to approval, with legal review by the issuer's securities counsel and (ideally) by the founder's personal securities counsel.

## Personal counsel vs. issuer counsel — the fiduciary boundary

A founder-insider adopting a 10b5-1 plan has two relevant counsel:

- **Issuer counsel** (the corporation's outside securities counsel — typically the firm that acted as the company's securities counsel in the IPO). Issuer counsel represents the *corporation*, not the insider. Their engagement is with the audit committee and the disclosure function.
- **Personal counsel** for the insider — a private-client attorney with securities expertise, or a specialised individual-insider practice. Personal counsel represents the *insider* and negotiates on the insider's behalf around plan terms, disclosure implications, and the interaction with the insider's other planning (10b5-1 plan for the insider's spouse, gift transfers to trusts that will hold subject securities, etc.).

For founders with material positions, engaging personal counsel is standard. The issuer counsel serves the corporation's interest in a compliant plan and a clean disclosure record; personal counsel serves the insider's interest in a plan that maximises the affirmative-defence position and coordinates with the insider's broader wealth-planning arc. Chapter 07 develops the counsel-hand-off discipline.

## Loss-of-defence risks — what invalidates the plan

The affirmative defence is not unconditional. Specific patterns can invalidate the defence, exposing the insider to Rule 10b-5 liability for trades executed under the plan:

- **Insider-influenced trades.** The insider directing or influencing the broker's execution of specific trades (deviating from the plan's formula) breaks the defence. The plan must be operated by the broker mechanically.
- **Non-good-faith adoption.** Adopting a plan in bad faith — e.g., specifically to trade around known MNPI — invalidates the defence.
- **Non-good-faith operation.** Terminating a plan strategically after adverse information, or otherwise operating opportunistically, loses the defence for the trades already executed (per the 2023 amendments' expansion of good-faith to a continuing test).
- **MNPI at adoption.** If the insider possessed material non-public information at plan adoption (regardless of the self-certification), the defence is unavailable. The self-certification is not a shield against actual MNPI possession — it is evidence of the insider's representation at adoption, but the SEC or plaintiffs' counsel can prove actual possession through other evidence.
- **Plan-adoption during a blackout window.** Adopting a plan during an issuer-designated blackout window is inconsistent with the "no MNPI at adoption" position and typically invalidates the plan for the affirmative-defence purpose.
- **Overlapping plans.** Maintaining multiple simultaneous plans for the same class of securities (outside the narrow exceptions) invalidates the plans.
- **Serial single-trade plans.** More than one single-trade plan in a 12-month period invalidates the plans as single-trade plans (they must fit within the multi-trade-plan structure).

Plaintiffs' counsel monitors 10b5-1 disclosures and Section 16 filings and files securities-fraud class actions when they identify patterns suggesting opportunistic trading. Recent enforcement actions (e.g., SEC enforcement against specific insider-trading patterns cited in the 2023-amendment adopting release) have made clear that the SEC intends to actively police the amended rule.

## Rule 144 interaction

Sales of restricted securities and control securities under the 10b5-1 plan must also comply with **Rule 144** — the SEC's safe harbour for public sales of restricted securities by control persons and affiliates. Rule 144 requires:

- A **holding period** for restricted securities (6 months for reporting-company securities, 1 year for non-reporting-company securities).
- **Current public information** about the issuer (the reporting-company requirement is typically satisfied by the issuer's ongoing SEC filings).
- **Volume limitations** for affiliates — sales in any 3-month period cannot exceed the greater of 1% of outstanding shares or (for securities listed on an exchange) the average weekly reported trading volume over the 4 preceding weeks.
- **Manner-of-sale requirements** for affiliates — sales must be brokers' transactions, transactions with a market maker, or riskless-principal transactions, and cannot involve solicitation of orders.
- **Form 144 filing** for affiliates whose sales exceed defined thresholds (5,000 shares or $50,000 aggregate in any 3-month period).

For a founder who is a Section 16 officer or director, the founder is an affiliate for Rule 144 purposes, and all these requirements apply. The 10b5-1 plan should be designed to comply with the Rule 144 volume and manner-of-sale limitations; the broker administering the plan is responsible for the Rule 144 compliance mechanics on each trade.

## Section 16 short-swing profits interaction

Under Section 16(b) of the Exchange Act, an insider (Section 16 officer, director, or 10% beneficial owner) who purchases and sells (or sells and purchases) issuer stock within any 6-month period is subject to disgorgement of the "short-swing profit" — the profit that would have been realised by matching the highest sale price with the lowest purchase price in the period. The 10b5-1 plan's sale trades are matched against any purchase trades in the 6-month window before and after each sale.

Section 16(a) also imposes disclosure requirements — insiders must file Forms 3, 4, and 5 to disclose their holdings and transactions. Plan trades appear on Form 4 within 2 business days of execution.

Practical implications for plan design:

- **Coordinate the plan with any anticipated equity-award receipts.** RSU vesting, option exercises, and ESPP purchases are all "purchases" for §16(b) matching purposes. A plan that sells shares in the same 6-month window as an equity-award vesting can produce a §16(b) exposure to be managed.
- **Coordinate with any planned open-market purchases.** If the founder ever plans to buy issuer stock in the open market (a rare pattern for founders, but not unknown), those purchases will match against plan sales in the 6-month window.

## Interaction with QSBS and other wealth planning

For a founder whose position includes QSBS-qualifying stock (chapter 1), the 10b5-1 plan interacts with the QSBS planning in specific ways:

- **The IPO does not by itself trigger QSBS gain.** The founder's stock becomes freely tradeable after the lock-up, but no QSBS sale has occurred simply because the corporation went public. The QSBS analysis runs against actual sales, which the plan schedules.
- **The 5-year holding period runs from stock acquisition, not from IPO.** A founder whose stock was acquired 6+ years before the IPO has satisfied the 5-year holding period at IPO and can sell any time thereafter with §1202 treatment (subject to cap). A founder whose stock was acquired more recently may need to time sales to fall after the 5-year date; the 10b5-1 plan can be designed to schedule sales accordingly.
- **The stacking-plan interaction.** If the founder has gifted portions of the QSBS position to non-grantor trusts (chapter 2), the *trusts* — not the founder personally — hold that portion of the stock. The trusts are separate persons for both QSBS and 10b5-1 purposes. Each trust that is a §16 insider (typically not the case for a small-percentage trust, but potentially the case for a large-percentage trust of a Section 16 insider under attribution rules) may need its own 10b5-1 plan. Trust-level 10b5-1 planning is a specialised discipline; personal securities counsel should confirm the specific analysis.

## Common failure modes — what to watch for

- **Adopting the plan too early relative to earnings.** A plan adopted immediately before an unannounced earnings surprise puts the insider in an awkward position — the SEC and plaintiffs' counsel will examine whether the insider knew of the pending release at adoption. Adopting during a defined open window (typically after an earnings release and during the issuer's ordinary trading window) is standard.
- **Plan-modification patterns.** Modifying a plan can trigger a new cooling-off period and creates disclosure exposure. Modifications should be rare and driven by non-opportunistic reasons (e.g., a lock-up modification that changes the release schedule); every modification is examined for opportunism.
- **Termination patterns.** Termination is heavily disfavoured. A plan that terminates immediately before a material adverse event, or that is repeatedly adopted-and-terminated, will not survive the good-faith test.
- **Not documenting the adoption record.** The plan document, the self-certification, the audit-committee approval, and the "no MNPI at adoption" contemporaneous record should be preserved and easy to retrieve years later. Some issuers use an insider-plan-administration platform (Fidelity, Morgan Stanley at Work, E*TRADE Corporate Services, Charles Schwab Stock Plan Services) that maintains the record digitally.
- **Underestimating the disclosure obligation.** Item 408(a) disclosure appears in the next Form 10-Q — visible to the market, to plaintiffs' counsel, and to short-sellers. Founders should expect (and prepare communications for) the possibility of market reaction to disclosed insider plans.
- **Failing to coordinate with the corporation's insider-trading policy administrator.** The corporation's designated administrator (typically the general counsel or a securities-law specialist within the legal team) has to be in the loop. A plan adopted without the administrator's engagement may be non-compliant with the corporation's own policy, even if it satisfies Rule 10b5-1.
- **Not thinking about tax withholding.** Sales under the plan generate capital-gains tax (or ordinary income, depending on the underlying stock's holding and character). The plan should coordinate with the founder's estimated-tax payment schedule; a large sale under the plan can produce a substantial estimated-tax liability for the current quarter. Sell-to-cover plans for RSU vesting are a separate mechanism handled by the corporation's stock-plan-administration platform.

## Summary

Rule 10b5-1 provides an affirmative defence against Rule 10b-5 insider-trading liability for insiders who adopt, in advance and without MNPI, a written trading plan that specifies future trades according to a formula or set of triggers. The 2023 SEC amendments materially tightened the rule — introducing a 90-day cooling-off period for officers and directors (with a 2-business-days-after-earnings extension capped at 120 days total), a self-certification of no MNPI and good faith at adoption, a no-overlapping-plans restriction, a single-trade-plan-per-12-months limit, an expanded continuing good-faith requirement, and new quarterly disclosure requirements under Item 408 of Regulation S-K. A defensible plan uses regular-cadence sales, staggered releases matched to any staggered lock-up, price-condition triggers that participate in upside without appearing opportunistic, volume-participation formulas for large positions, event-triggered pauses for blackout periods, and constrained termination provisions. Adoption requires audit-committee approval, coordination with the issuer's insider-trading policy administrator, and disclosure in the next quarterly report. Personal counsel for the insider is standard for material positions; issuer counsel represents the corporation, not the insider. The plan interacts with Rule 144 (holding period, volume limits, manner-of-sale, Form 144 filing), Section 16 (short-swing profit matching, Form 4 disclosure), the IPO lock-up expiration schedule, and — for QSBS-qualifying positions — the §1202 planning framework of chapters 1–2. Chapter 4 develops the diversification framework that sits on top of (or, for M&A founders, replaces) the 10b5-1 sale programme.
