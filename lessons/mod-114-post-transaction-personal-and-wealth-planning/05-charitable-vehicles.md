# Charitable Vehicles — CRT, DAF, and Private-Foundation Structuring for a Founder Wealth Event

## Why this matters

A founder wealth event surfaces a specific decision that is easier to make at the moment than at any other point in the founder's life: what portion of the wealth is destined for personal use, what portion for family provision, and what portion for philanthropic purposes. The tax code offers substantial benefits for the philanthropic portion — but the benefits are largest when the giving vehicle is structured and (most importantly) *funded with appreciated assets* **before** the sale, not with cash after. A founder who contributes $5M of pre-sale appreciated stock to a donor-advised fund receives a §170 charitable deduction for the *fair market value* of the stock (not the founder's basis), avoids the capital-gains tax that would otherwise be owed on the sale of that stock, and unlocks up to $5M of philanthropic capital that will fund charitable distributions for decades. A founder who instead sells the stock first, pays the tax, and then contributes the after-tax cash to the same DAF has left roughly 20–25% of the philanthropic capital on the table (the capital-gains tax the pre-sale structure would have avoided) and typically receives a smaller net deduction.

The three primary vehicles — **Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT)**, **Donor-Advised Fund (DAF)**, and **Private Foundation** — sit on a spectrum. At one end, the DAF is fast, cheap, flexible, and low-administration, with the trade-off that the sponsor legally controls the assets and the founder's role is *advisory* (though sponsors overwhelmingly follow donor advice). At the other end, the private foundation gives the founder (and successor family members) permanent legal control and multigenerational governance, at the cost of substantial ongoing administrative burden, higher setup and operating costs, tighter regulatory constraints (§4940–§4945 excise taxes), and a lower charitable-deduction ceiling. The CRT sits somewhere in between and serves a specific need — providing the donor with an income stream during their lifetime while committing the remainder to a charitable beneficiary — with its own tax mechanics under §664.

This chapter installs the anatomy of each vehicle, the trade-off framework for choosing among them, and the pre-close structuring discipline that captures the largest tax benefit. The specific-fact-pattern design belongs with a private-client tax attorney, an estate attorney (for the trust and foundation drafting), and a foundation-specialist consultant (for large private-foundation structures with multigenerational governance considerations).

> **Reminder: this is education, not tax, legal, or estate advice.** Charitable-vehicle design involves gift-tax, estate-tax, income-tax, and (for private foundations) excise-tax mechanics that interact in ways that are not always obvious. Every vehicle has irrevocable elements — assets contributed cannot be reclaimed for personal use, and the founder must accept that constraint. A live plan requires a private-client tax attorney (charitable-planning specialist), an estate attorney, a CPA, and — for private foundations — a foundation-specialist consultant. This chapter is what you read *before* you engage them so their time goes to judgment, not education.

## The tax-deduction / donation / legacy trade-off

Every charitable-vehicle decision balances three considerations that a founder should be explicit about:

- **Tax deduction** — the current-year (or carryforward) charitable deduction the founder receives under §170. The deduction reduces the founder's taxable income for the year of the contribution, at whatever marginal rate applies. For a founder in the top federal bracket (37% marginal ordinary rate) with substantial income the year of a sale, a $5M charitable deduction is worth roughly $1.85M in federal tax reduction plus state tax where applicable.
- **Donation timing and control** — when the assets are actually deployed to charitable purposes, and how much control the founder (or the founder's designees) retains over the deployment. DAFs give near-immediate deployment capacity (the founder can grant to any qualified charity at any time from the sponsor's account), CRTs deploy the remainder only at the founder's death, and private foundations deploy at whatever schedule the foundation adopts (subject to the §4942 minimum-distribution rule).
- **Legacy** — the multigenerational structure the vehicle creates. DAFs can be designed with successor advisors (children and grandchildren advising the sponsor on grants) but are ultimately controlled by the sponsor. Private foundations can be structured for permanent family control across generations with defined roles for successive family members.

The three vehicles map differently onto this trade-off:

- **DAF** — highest immediate deduction (100% FMV of appreciated public stock, up to 30% of AGI, with 5-year carryforward), highest flexibility on deployment timing, lowest setup and operating cost, moderate legacy structure via successor advisors, minimum control (advisory only).
- **CRT** — deduction based on the present value of the remainder interest (typically 15–50% of contribution FMV, depending on payout rate and life expectancy), assets support annual payments to the donor for life or a term, remainder to charity at end of term, moderate setup cost, moderate ongoing administration, limited multigenerational structure.
- **Private Foundation** — lower immediate deduction ceiling (30% of AGI for cash to a private foundation, 20% for appreciated stock), full legal control by family (subject to §4940–§4945), highest ongoing administrative burden, highest setup cost, strongest legacy structure with permanent family governance.

The framework in practice: a founder with $50M of exit proceeds who wants to allocate $10M to philanthropy across the arc of their life typically ends up with a *combination* — say, $5M to a DAF for immediate flexibility and near-term granting, $3M to a CRT for lifetime income plus eventual charitable remainder, $2M to a private foundation for the family's long-term charitable legacy. The specific mix depends on the founder's philanthropic goals, family structure, and desired governance style.

## Donor-Advised Funds (DAFs)

### What a DAF is

A DAF is a *fund of a public charity*. The founder contributes assets to the sponsoring organisation (a public charity that operates a DAF program — Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, Vanguard Charitable, Impact Assets, National Philanthropic Trust, Silicon Valley Community Foundation, and others). The contribution is a completed gift for both income-tax and gift-tax purposes; the sponsoring charity is now the legal owner of the assets. The founder receives *advisory privileges* — the right to recommend grants from the fund to qualified §501(c)(3) charities. The sponsor is legally obligated to make an independent judgment on each grant recommendation, but in practice sponsors approve virtually all recommendations to properly-qualified charities (rejection is rare and typically only for grants that fail the charity-qualification review or that involve prohibited transactions).

### Tax mechanics

- **Deduction at contribution.** The founder receives a §170 charitable deduction for the *fair market value* of the contributed assets (assuming the assets are long-term-capital-gain property held more than 1 year and the DAF sponsor is a public charity — see AGI-percentage-limit table below).
- **AGI percentage limits.** For contributions of *cash* to a public charity, the deduction is limited to 60% of AGI. For contributions of *long-term appreciated property* (including appreciated stock) to a public charity, the deduction is limited to 30% of AGI. Amounts above the AGI limits carry forward for up to 5 years.
- **Avoidance of capital-gains tax.** The founder does not recognise the appreciation in the contributed assets. The sponsor (a tax-exempt public charity) can sell the contributed assets without capital-gains tax. This is the primary structural benefit of contributing appreciated stock (as opposed to selling and contributing cash).
- **No estate tax on contributed assets.** Contributed assets are removed from the founder's estate for estate-tax purposes.
- **No excise taxes.** Unlike private foundations, DAFs are not subject to the §4940 net-investment-income tax or the §4942 minimum-distribution rule. The sponsor may have its own internal minimum-granting guidelines, but there is no federal minimum-distribution requirement at the DAF-account level.

### Sponsor selection

The major DAF sponsors:

- **Fidelity Charitable** — the largest DAF sponsor. Broad investment-fund menu, low minimum ($0 minimum for many programs), granting to qualified US charities and (with additional review) to certain international charities. <https://www.fidelitycharitable.org/>
- **Schwab Charitable** — second-largest sponsor. Similar profile. <https://www.schwabcharitable.org/>
- **Vanguard Charitable** — third-largest by assets. $25,000 minimum (as of 2024; verify current). Vanguard's low-cost investment options. <https://www.vanguardcharitable.org/>
- **National Philanthropic Trust** — independent sponsor. Broader investment flexibility including alternative investments in some programs. <https://www.nptrust.org/>
- **Impact Assets** — focuses on impact investing within the DAF. Investment menu emphasises impact-oriented and mission-aligned investments. <https://impactassets.org/>
- **Silicon Valley Community Foundation** and **community foundations more broadly** — regional sponsors that combine DAF services with community-focused programmes. Founders with a specific geographic-community giving focus may prefer a community-foundation sponsor.
- **DAFgiving360 (formerly Rockefeller Philanthropy Advisors)**, **Bank of America Charitable**, **Morgan Stanley GIFT**, and others — additional sponsor options for founders with existing wealth-manager relationships.

Selection considerations:

- **Investment options.** Most sponsors offer 5–20 investment pools ranging from money-market and short-term-bond funds to global-equity index funds to alternative-investment allocations. Founders who want to hold specific investments (individual stocks, ETFs, private-fund positions) should confirm what the sponsor permits.
- **Grant flexibility.** All major sponsors permit grants to qualified US public charities. International giving, private-operating-foundation giving, and grants with restrictions (endowments, program-specific restrictions) vary in ease across sponsors.
- **Fees.** Sponsor fees typically 0.5–1.0% annually on the DAF-account balance, plus investment-fund expenses. Model these into the total cost.
- **Minimum and account rules.** Some sponsors have minimum contribution amounts, minimum grant amounts, or minimum activity requirements. Confirm the specific rules.
- **Sponsor's own governance and reputation.** DAF sponsors' policies on politically-oriented grants, sponsor-recommended investment options, and grant-recommendation approval have varied over time. Founders whose granting programme includes politically-adjacent charities should confirm the sponsor's posture.

### Successor-advisor structure

A DAF account typically permits the founder to name *successor advisors* — individuals (typically family members) who will have advisory privileges after the founder's death or incapacity. The advisor privileges extend for the sponsor-permitted duration (typically 2–3 generations of successors, varying by sponsor). This is the DAF's mechanism for multigenerational family involvement in philanthropy, though it is not permanent — after the successor-advisor chain ends, the sponsor retains discretion over the fund's remaining assets (typically with a defined terminal-distribution plan that reflects the family's stated preferences).

### When a DAF is the right choice

- **Founder wants immediate flexibility on granting cadence.** DAFs are the most flexible vehicle for donors who want to grant across many recipients over many years without ongoing administrative burden.
- **Founder wants to make a large deduction in a high-income year and spread the granting over decades.** DAF contributions can be made in the year of the sale (maximising the deduction against high income) while granting can occur over many subsequent years.
- **Founder does not need lifetime income from the assets.** If the founder needs the assets to produce ongoing income for personal use, a CRT (see below) is a better fit.
- **Founder does not need permanent family control.** DAFs work well for successor-advisor structures across 2–3 generations; for indefinite family control, a private foundation is a better fit.

## Charitable Remainder Trusts (CRTs)

### What a CRT is

A CRT is an *irrevocable split-interest trust* under §664 that provides an *income interest* to one or more non-charitable beneficiaries (typically the donor and, optionally, the donor's spouse or other named individuals) for a defined term, with the *remainder* passing to one or more qualified §501(c)(3) charities at the end of the term. The donor contributes appreciated assets to the CRT, receives a partial charitable deduction based on the present value of the remainder interest, and receives distributions from the CRT (either fixed or variable) during the income term.

### The two structural variants

Two primary variants (with sub-variants):

- **Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust (CRAT) — §664(d)(1).** The CRAT pays a *fixed* annual amount (a specified dollar amount or a fixed percentage of the initial contribution value) to the income beneficiary each year for the term. The payout amount is fixed at trust inception and does not vary with the trust's investment performance.
- **Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT) — §664(d)(2).** The CRUT pays a *variable* annual amount equal to a fixed percentage of the trust's assets valued *annually*. When the trust's assets appreciate, the annual payment increases; when they depreciate, it decreases.

Sub-variants of the CRUT:

- **Standard CRUT (SCRUT).** Pays the fixed percentage of annual asset value regardless of the trust's income.
- **Net Income CRUT (NICRUT).** Pays the *lesser* of the fixed percentage or the trust's actual net income for the year.
- **Net Income with Makeup CRUT (NIMCRUT).** Pays the lesser of the fixed percentage or actual net income, with a *makeup* provision — in later years when income exceeds the fixed percentage, the trust can make up any prior-year shortfalls.
- **Flip-CRUT.** Starts as a NICRUT (or NIMCRUT) and *flips* to a SCRUT on the occurrence of a defined triggering event (typically the sale of a specific asset). Flip-CRUTs are common for donors contributing illiquid assets — the trust pays only its actual (typically minimal) income until the asset is sold, then flips to a standard payout after the sale generates cash.

### Tax mechanics

- **Charitable deduction at contribution.** The donor receives a §170 deduction equal to the *present value of the charitable remainder interest*. This value is computed using IRS actuarial tables (§7520 rates, updated monthly) based on the payout percentage, the term of the trust (life, joint lives, or a defined number of years up to 20), and the projected trust performance. Higher payout rates and longer terms produce lower charitable deductions (more of the value goes to the income beneficiary). The deduction is subject to AGI percentage limits (30% for appreciated property to a public charity, 20% for appreciated property to a private foundation) with 5-year carryforward.
- **Minimum charitable interest.** §664 requires the projected charitable remainder to be at least *10% of the initial contribution value*. This constraint limits the maximum payout rate given the term and the §7520 rate.
- **No capital-gains tax on sale of contributed assets inside the trust.** The CRT is generally tax-exempt (subject to specific exceptions). The trustee can sell the contributed appreciated stock without triggering current-year tax; the entire proceeds are available for reinvestment inside the trust.
- **Tax on distributions to income beneficiary.** Distributions from the CRT to the income beneficiary are taxed based on the trust's income character *and* the four-tier ordering rule of §664(b): ordinary income first, then capital gains, then tax-exempt income, then trust corpus. In practice, a CRT funded with appreciated stock will produce distributions to the income beneficiary that are treated as capital-gains distributions for many years (as the beneficiary "receives" the deferred appreciation over time).
- **Unrelated business taxable income (UBTI).** Any UBTI earned by the CRT results in a 100% excise tax on the UBTI under §664(c)(2). This is a specific concern if the trust invests in leveraged real estate, active-business partnerships, or other UBTI-generating investments. Trustees typically design the investment mix to avoid UBTI.

### When a CRT is the right choice

- **Founder wants lifetime income and a charitable remainder.** The CRT provides annual distributions during the income term, converting an appreciated position into a stream of payments (typically 5–8% of trust value annually) with the remainder to charity at the end of term.
- **Founder is highly appreciated in a specific asset and wants to spread the tax over years.** By contributing pre-sale and having the CRT sell inside the trust, the founder defers the tax cost by paying it gradually as distributions are received (rather than paying the full tax on the sale in the year of sale).
- **Founder has a specific income-support plan.** For a founder without other income and needing the CRT distributions for living expenses, the CRT can provide reliable annual income backed by a diversified investment portfolio.
- **Founder wants a defined charitable remainder to a specific charity** rather than the flexibility of ongoing granting. The CRT commits the remainder to defined charities named in the trust document.

CRTs are more complex to establish than DAFs (require trust drafting, trustee selection, ongoing tax filings via Form 5227, actuarial computations for the deduction) and less flexible on the charitable-recipient designation (the beneficiary charities are fixed at trust creation, though some flexibility exists depending on trust drafting). The choice between CRT and DAF often comes down to whether the founder wants an income stream from the contributed assets: CRT if yes, DAF if no.

## Private Foundations

### What a private foundation is

A private foundation is a §501(c)(3) tax-exempt organisation that is *not* a public charity — typically because its funding comes from a single family or a small number of related donors rather than from broad public support. Private foundations are subject to a specific set of excise taxes and operational restrictions under §4940–§4945 that public charities are not.

### The excise-tax and operational framework

The private-foundation restrictions:

- **§4940 — net investment income tax.** The foundation pays a 1.39% (as of the 2020+ tax years) excise tax on its net investment income each year. This is a small tax but a persistent one.
- **§4941 — self-dealing.** The foundation and "disqualified persons" (the founder, family members, substantial contributors, and certain related entities) are prohibited from engaging in specified self-dealing transactions. Sales, leases, loans, and other transactions between the foundation and disqualified persons are subject to substantial excise taxes on both the foundation and the disqualified persons.
- **§4942 — minimum distribution requirement.** The foundation must distribute at least 5% of its assets each year for charitable purposes (grants to qualified public charities, or direct charitable activities). Failure to meet the minimum triggers substantial excise taxes.
- **§4943 — excess business holdings.** The foundation and disqualified persons together cannot own more than 20% (generally) of the voting stock of a business enterprise. Contributions of stock in a founder's operating company can trigger excess-business-holdings problems if the founder personally owns a large stake and the foundation's stake pushes the combined ownership over the threshold.
- **§4944 — jeopardising investments.** The foundation cannot make investments that jeopardise its charitable purpose. This is a subjective standard applied to reckless or speculative investment behaviour.
- **§4945 — taxable expenditures.** The foundation cannot make grants to individuals (except under strictly limited scholarship-and-award programs approved by the IRS), to non-charitable organisations, for lobbying or political campaigns, or for other "taxable expenditure" categories. Grants to non-public charities (foreign charities, other private foundations) require "expenditure responsibility" — a specific set of pre-grant inquiries and post-grant reports designed to ensure the funds are used for charitable purposes.

### Tax mechanics for the donor

- **Deduction at contribution.** For contributions of *cash* to a private foundation, the AGI percentage limit is 30% (compared to 60% for cash to public charities). For contributions of *long-term appreciated property* to a private foundation, the limit is *20%* (compared to 30% for appreciated property to public charities). This is a material difference; the private foundation is less generous on the deduction side than the DAF.
- **Deduction is generally limited to basis for non-publicly-traded stock.** A specific complication: for contributions of appreciated property to a private foundation, the deduction is generally limited to the donor's *basis* rather than fair market value, *except* for contributions of "qualified appreciated stock" (publicly-traded stock for which market quotations are readily available). This means contributions of pre-IPO stock, private-company stock, or restricted stock to a private foundation typically produce only a basis-level deduction — a substantially smaller deduction than the same contribution to a DAF or a public charity.
- **Avoidance of capital-gains tax.** As with the DAF, the foundation can sell the contributed assets without triggering capital-gains tax at the donor level.
- **No estate tax on contributed assets.** Contributed assets are outside the donor's estate.

### Administration and cost

Private foundations require:

- **Formation** — typically as a Delaware nonprofit corporation or as a trust. Legal setup costs are typically $10K–$50K depending on complexity.
- **Federal tax-exempt status application** — IRS Form 1023 (or the streamlined 1023-EZ for smaller foundations). Application preparation and review can take 6–18 months.
- **State registration** — most states require annual registration as a charitable organisation with the state attorney general.
- **Annual Form 990-PF filing** — a detailed public tax return disclosing the foundation's assets, income, expenses, grants, and (importantly) grants and salaries paid to disqualified persons. Preparation typically requires a CPA specialising in foundation returns.
- **Ongoing operational compliance** — periodic self-dealing review, minimum-distribution tracking, expenditure-responsibility documentation for non-public-charity grants, and (for foundations with staff or programs) HR, contracts, and program-administration infrastructure.
- **Foundation-specific counsel** — for material foundations, ongoing engagement with foundation counsel to review grants and structural questions.

Annual operating costs for a small family foundation typically run $25K–$100K in professional fees plus any staff or program costs. For a large foundation with programmatic activities, annual operating costs can run into the millions.

### When a private foundation is the right choice

- **Founder wants permanent, multigenerational family control** of the philanthropic vehicle. Private foundations can be structured for indefinite family involvement, with successive generations serving as directors and officers.
- **Founder plans to build a substantial philanthropic institution** with defined programs, staff, and long-term program commitments. The foundation form supports institutional-scale philanthropy in ways that DAFs and CRTs do not.
- **Founder is contributing publicly-traded stock** (post-IPO) and can accept the AGI limits. Contributions of pre-IPO stock to a private foundation often produce only a basis-level deduction, materially reducing the tax benefit vs. a DAF.
- **Founder accepts the ongoing administrative burden** in exchange for control and legacy.

Private foundations are the right choice for a small but material minority of founder-donors — typically those with $20M+ dedicated to permanent philanthropic use, a genuine commitment to institutional-scale giving, and a family that will continue the work across generations. Most founders whose philanthropic capital is <$5M and who want flexible granting choose a DAF instead.

## The pre-close-contribution discipline

The single largest tax lever in charitable-vehicle planning is contributing *appreciated stock before the sale*, not cash after. The specific rules:

- **Contribution must be complete before the sale becomes effectively certain.** The tax doctrine of "anticipatory assignment of income" holds that a taxpayer cannot avoid income by transferring income-producing property after the income has effectively been earned. Applied to a stock sale, a contribution made after the definitive agreement is signed (or after the sale is otherwise essentially certain) can be recharacterised as a sale by the donor followed by a contribution of the after-tax proceeds — losing the appreciation-avoidance benefit.
- **The safe harbour timing.** Practitioner consensus is that contributions made *before* the definitive agreement is signed, and preferably before the LOI, are safely within the pre-close window. Contributions made *after* the definitive agreement is signed but *before* closing carry higher recharacterisation risk. Contributions made *at closing* or *after closing* are typically effective for the after-tax cash portion only. Tax counsel makes the specific call for the specific fact pattern.
- **Contribution of stock with a pending merger.** For a stock-for-stock merger where the founder will receive acquirer stock rather than cash, the founder can contribute the target stock to the charitable vehicle before closing. The vehicle then receives acquirer stock in the merger and can either hold or sell without triggering donor-level tax.
- **Appraisal requirement.** For contributions of non-publicly-traded stock exceeding $10,000 (and generally recommended for any material contribution of privately-held stock), a *qualified appraisal* is required under §170(f)(11). The appraisal must be prepared by a qualified appraiser meeting specific IRS criteria and must be attached to the donor's tax return via Form 8283.
- **Publicly-traded stock is easier.** Post-IPO publicly-traded stock does not require an appraisal (the market quotation is the FMV). Pre-IPO stock, private-company stock, or restricted stock requires a qualified appraisal — a specific engagement that typically takes 4–8 weeks.

The practical implication: charitable-vehicle planning is *transaction-integrated planning*, not post-transaction planning. A founder who intends to contribute pre-sale should have the vehicle established, the contribution documented, and (for private stock) the appraisal complete, before the LOI is signed. This is a 4–12 week planning window that must run in parallel with the transaction preparation.

## Combining the vehicles

Most founder philanthropic plans use a *combination* of vehicles rather than a single one. Common patterns:

- **DAF + CRT combination.** DAF for immediate flexible granting; CRT for lifetime income support and eventual charitable remainder. The founder contributes $X to a DAF pre-close (immediate deduction, ongoing granting flexibility) and $Y to a CRT (income during life, remainder to charity at end of term).
- **DAF + Private Foundation combination.** DAF for a portion of the giving that benefits from immediate deployment; private foundation for the multigenerational legacy component with permanent family control. The DAF handles the flexible-granting layer; the private foundation handles the institutional-legacy layer.
- **CRT + Private Foundation combination.** CRT provides the founder's lifetime income; the CRT's charitable remainder can be designated to the founder's private foundation (rather than to a public charity), extending the family's charitable control across the CRT term.
- **All three combination.** For very large philanthropic budgets ($20M+), all three vehicles can serve distinct roles: DAF for the flexible-granting layer, CRT for the lifetime-income layer, private foundation for the multigenerational-institutional layer.

Combining vehicles requires careful design across the vehicles to avoid inefficiency (e.g., over-consuming AGI deduction limits in a single year) or double-planning (e.g., the CRT's remainder to the foundation may not be the most tax-efficient way to fund the foundation compared to a direct contribution).

## Common failure modes — what to watch for

- **Contributing after the sale.** The most common failure. A founder who did not plan pre-close and contributes after-tax cash loses the capital-gains-avoidance benefit — typically 20–25% of the value of the intended charitable capital. The rebuttal is that the contribution still receives a §170 deduction, but the deduction against ordinary income at 37% is less valuable than the combined capital-gains avoidance (23.8% + state) plus §170 deduction that pre-close contribution would have delivered.
- **Choosing a private foundation for pre-IPO stock.** A founder who contributes pre-IPO stock to a private foundation gets only a basis-level deduction (typically minimal) because pre-IPO stock is not "qualified appreciated stock." The same stock contributed to a DAF or CRT (both of which are public-charity structures) receives a full-FMV deduction. Founders should confirm the deduction treatment before choosing the vehicle for pre-IPO contributions.
- **Contributing QSBS-eligible stock without considering the §1202 offset.** For a founder's QSBS position that is within the §1202 cap, direct sale under §1202 exclusion produces $0 federal tax on the excluded gain — no charitable deduction is needed to achieve zero federal tax. Contributing that stock to a charitable vehicle produces a large deduction that can offset *other* income sources (salary, non-QSBS gains), but the analysis must consider whether the deduction is more valuable than the alternative uses of the QSBS-excluded proceeds. Above-cap gain is a much clearer candidate for charitable-vehicle contribution.
- **Over-funding the vehicle relative to actual philanthropic intent.** Founders sometimes contribute more to charitable vehicles than they actually intend to give away philanthropically, on the theory that "it's for a good cause anyway." The problem: charitable-vehicle contributions are irrevocable. Once contributed, the assets cannot be reclaimed for personal use. Founders should think carefully about their actual philanthropic intent (measured in dollars, not vague aspirations) and contribute at that level, not above it.
- **Not planning for successor advisors or trustees.** DAFs, CRTs, and private foundations all have successor structures. Failure to name and prepare successors can result in the vehicle's continuation being uncertain after the founder's death or incapacity. The successor design is part of the estate plan and should be reviewed periodically.
- **Under-attention to state tax and state-charity-registration rules.** State tax treatment of charitable contributions varies. State charity-registration requirements for private foundations and CRTs vary. Some states have specific rules on trust situs and taxation. Estate counsel should review the state-level analysis.
- **Confusing the vehicles' relative deduction ceilings.** The 60% / 30% / 20% AGI percentage limits are commonly confused. Practitioners typically build the deduction plan around the specific AGI number for the year of contribution and select the vehicle mix to maximise the current-year deduction with 5-year carryforward for excess.
- **Not documenting the qualified appraisal.** For pre-IPO or other non-publicly-traded contributions, missing or defective qualified-appraisal documentation can invalidate the deduction. The appraisal is a specific IRS-defined process; not any valuation qualifies. Foundation counsel and the appraiser should coordinate to ensure the documentation is compliant.

## Summary

The three primary charitable vehicles — DAF, CRT, and Private Foundation — sit on a spectrum defined by three trade-offs: tax-deduction size (DAF and CRT more generous under §170 AGI limits than PF), donation timing and control (DAF flexible on granting timing, CRT commits remainder at end of term, PF requires 5% annual distribution), and legacy (DAF supports successor-advisor structures for 2–3 generations, CRT ends at income-term end, PF supports permanent family governance). The single largest tax lever in charitable-vehicle planning is contributing appreciated stock *before the sale*, avoiding capital-gains tax on the sale and receiving a §170 deduction for the FMV; contributions after the sale become effectively certain risk recharacterisation as anticipatory assignment of income. Most founder philanthropic plans combine vehicles — often DAF for flexible granting, CRT for lifetime income plus charitable remainder, private foundation for multigenerational-legacy structure. Live vehicle design requires a private-client tax attorney (charitable-planning specialist), estate attorney (for trust and foundation drafting), CPA, and — for private foundations — foundation-specialist consultant. Chapter 6 develops the family-office decision that carries the wealth-management arc forward for the long term.
