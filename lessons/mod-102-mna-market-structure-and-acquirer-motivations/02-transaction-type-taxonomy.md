# Transaction-Type Taxonomy — Aqui-Hire, Tuck-In, Platform, Transformative-Strategic

## Why this matters

The acquirer-typology from chapter 1 tells you *who* the buyer is. It does not, by itself, tell you *what kind of deal* they intend to do. A single strategic acquirer can offer any of four fundamentally different transaction shapes depending on how they see the target, and each shape carries a different consideration mix, different employee-retention terms, different integration approach, and different post-close governance.

Founders — and, embarrassingly often, bankers — sometimes negotiate the wrong shape. They spend six weeks arguing headline price on a deal that is structurally an aqui-hire (where retention is the point), or they push for autonomy carve-outs on a platform deal (where absorption is the acquirer's whole thesis). Both mistakes cost money, credibility, and sometimes the deal.

This chapter installs the four-shape classification and teaches you to recognise which shape an inbound is *before* the LOI is drafted, so you negotiate against the right terms and the right expectations.

## The four transaction shapes

Every M&A transaction — regardless of acquirer sub-type — fits (roughly) into one of four shapes. The shapes are defined by what the acquirer is fundamentally buying and what they will do with it.

### 1. Aqui-hire

The acquirer is buying **the team**, primarily. The product may be preserved, folded, or shelved; the deal exists because acquiring the intact team is faster, cheaper, or more certain than hiring the same people separately in the open market.

**Typical shape:**
- **Consideration mix:** cash headline that is often modest relative to the last-round valuation — sometimes below it, sometimes as low as return-of-capital plus a small premium — combined with a large **retention pool** (RSUs or restricted stock, cash bonuses, unvested-equity refresh grants) that pays out over 2–4 years contingent on the team staying.
- **Structure on a per-employee basis:** widely-cited industry ranges put the *implied per-employee spend* on tech-team aqui-hires in the $1M–$5M range, higher for specialised talent (ML researchers, deep-domain PhDs, specific-technology veterans). The upfront-vs-retention split is often 20–40% upfront and 60–80% retention. These ranges vary by cycle and category; treat them as folklore, not benchmarks.
- **Retention terms:** typically 2–4 year vesting with a 1-year cliff, sometimes with acceleration triggers on involuntary termination without cause. Non-compete and non-solicit terms are aggressive. Key-person clauses may name specific founders / VPs.
- **Integration:** the product is often folded or shelved within 6–12 months. The team is re-slotted into the acquirer's projects, sometimes as an intact pod under a preserved leader, sometimes distributed across teams.
- **Post-close governance:** founders typically become individual contributors, engineering directors, or VP-level executives *inside* the acquirer, without board seats or reserved decision rights. There is no "run separately" thesis.

**Investor recovery:** frequently the biggest issue in an aqui-hire. If the headline number is below the preference stack, common-stock holders (founders, employees on options) often get zero from the deal proper, and their economics come entirely from the retention pool that pays out over time — which they receive as employees, not as shareholders. Preferred holders may take their preference, or (increasingly common) may waive a portion of preference into a "carve-out" that funds a management incentive plan (MIP) for the common. Waterfall design in an aqui-hire is a specific art; mod-101 chapter 5's optionality-preserving audit flags the preference-stack terms that most constrain it, and mod-103 develops the mechanics.

**When you should recognise this shape:** the acquirer is asking mostly about team composition, individual bios, prior work, retention-plan history, and technical culture. They are not doing deep product diligence or extensive customer-reference calls, because those are not what they are buying. Time from first serious conversation to LOI is often short (weeks, not months).

### 2. Tuck-in

The acquirer is buying **a specific product or product line** to fold into their existing portfolio. The team comes with the product but is not the primary asset; the product's revenue, technology, and customer base are.

**Typical shape:**
- **Consideration mix:** cash-heavy at the small-and-mid end (below, say, $200M–$300M for public strategics); mix of cash and stock at larger scale. Earn-outs are possible but not universal.
- **Pricing anchor:** revenue or ARR multiple in the acquirer's public-comp set, adjusted for growth and margin, plus a modest synergy premium (10–30% above stand-alone comps if the fit is strong). For pre-revenue or thin-revenue targets, the pricing looks more like a strategic hire's aqui-hire economics blended with technology valuation.
- **Retention terms:** standard retention grants for the target's leadership (2–3 year vesting, sometimes with earn-out linkage for the founder-CEO), moderate for the broader team. Non-compete for the founder / key leaders.
- **Integration:** the product is preserved as a distinct offering for a period (often 12–24 months), then folded into the acquirer's naming convention. Engineering integrates; product management typically integrates within a year; go-to-market may run parallel initially before folding into the acquirer's sales motion.
- **Post-close governance:** the founder-CEO usually reports to the SVP or GM of the acquiring business unit. No board seats; typically a defined operating role for 12–24 months with a natural transition out at the end.

**Investor recovery:** the standard sell-side waterfall runs cleanly — preferred takes its preference and participation (or converts to common), common holders participate above the preference stack. In a well-shaped tuck-in the common holders (founders, employee options) get material economics, especially if the sale is at a healthy multiple relative to the last preferred round.

**When you should recognise this shape:** the acquirer's questions in early diligence focus on product functionality, revenue quality, customer mix, technology architecture, and how the product would slot into their portfolio. They may want to meet the head of engineering and the head of product; they may not spend much time on the broader team. Time to LOI is typical M&A pace (2–4 months from first serious conversation).

### 3. Platform

The acquirer is buying **a piece of infrastructure or a platform-defining capability**. The target becomes a chassis on which the acquirer builds a broader business, or a component that meaningfully upgrades an existing platform.

**Typical shape:**
- **Consideration mix:** stock-heavier than the smaller shapes, especially at scale. Cash consideration may still dominate at mid-market prices, but stock consideration becomes the norm above $500M–$1B. Rollover equity for the founder / senior leadership is common.
- **Pricing anchor:** platform-lift math — what does owning this target do to the acquirer's platform revenue, retention, and total addressable market? The synergy premium can be very large (50–100%+ above stand-alone comps) if the platform thesis is defensible, because the math is not "add product X's revenue" but "raise ARPU on the entire installed base."
- **Retention terms:** aggressive multi-year retention for the target's leadership, especially the founder-CEO (if they are technical) and the VP-of-Engineering / CTO. Reserved decision rights are unusual; the acquirer wants full ownership of the platform roadmap.
- **Integration:** deep and (relatively) fast. Engineering integrates into the platform stack within 6–12 months. Product management is absorbed into the platform-product organisation. The target's brand may be preserved for 12–18 months before being retired in favour of the acquirer's platform naming.
- **Post-close governance:** the founder-CEO often becomes a GM or business-unit leader inside the acquirer, or a VP-Platform / VP-Product role that reports to the acquirer's CTO or Chief Product Officer. There may be a "founder-office" arrangement with an executive-team seat for 1–2 years, though these arrangements are notoriously fragile in practice (mod-111 chapter 3's honest-signal pattern).

**Investor recovery:** typically strong. Platform deals command the highest synergy premiums in the strategic set, which usually translates to healthy multiples on invested capital for the preferred stack and material upside for the common.

**When you should recognise this shape:** the acquirer's questions focus on architecture, extensibility, roadmap ambition, technical culture, and *fit with their platform strategy*. Executive-level meetings involve the acquirer's CTO / CPO / CEO earlier than they would for a tuck-in. The acquirer's investor-relations narrative in past periods has named the category as a strategic priority.

### 4. Transformative-strategic

The acquirer is making a **company-defining bet**. The transaction is large enough — often 20%+ of the acquirer's own market capitalisation, sometimes much more — that its success or failure will materially shape the acquirer's future. This is the sub-type covered by phrases like "bet the company" and "generation-defining acquisition."

**Typical shape:**
- **Consideration mix:** stock-heavy by necessity (the acquirer often cannot fund a deal at this scale with cash alone). CVRs, seller notes, and structured consideration are sometimes used to bridge valuation gaps.
- **Pricing anchor:** the negotiation is often above traditional comp anchors because the transformative thesis (new-category dominance, existential defense against a peer) supports a valuation that a pure comparable-transaction chart would not.
- **Retention terms:** aggressive multi-year retention plus, sometimes, board seats and reserved decision rights for the target's senior leadership. Deal-team-alignment is a headline item because the transaction's success depends on the acquired team not turning over.
- **Integration:** varies widely. Some transformative deals preserve the target as a semi-independent business unit indefinitely (Instagram-inside-Facebook is the reference example of this pattern). Others rapidly integrate with the acquirer being materially reshaped in the process. The choice is a strategic call by the acquirer's board.
- **Post-close governance:** transformative deals often bring the founder-CEO onto the acquirer's board or executive committee. In some cases, the target's leadership takes over the acquirer's platform (rare but not unheard-of — the reverse-takeover cultural pattern).

**Regulatory / stakeholder complexity:** transformative deals almost always trigger HSR / CFIUS / foreign-antitrust scrutiny (mod-103 depth), often trigger stockholder litigation (mod-109 depth), and always trigger extensive employee / customer / press choreography (mod-110 depth). The pre-marketing choreography for a plausibly-transformative deal is materially different from the others; chapter 6 develops that.

**When you should recognise this shape:** the conversations are CEO-to-CEO early. The strategic narrative is generational, not incremental. Analyst reaction models are being run internally at the acquirer. Board-to-board conversations happen before LOI, not just after.

## The taxonomy is not a stack rank

A common misread: founders assume "aqui-hire = worst, transformative = best." That is wrong on at least two axes:

- **Founder-personal outcome depends on the specific deal, not the shape.** A well-structured aqui-hire that clears the preference stack and pays substantial retention over four years can be a very good outcome for founders and key employees. A poorly-negotiated platform deal that squeezes the founder into a two-year non-compete and folds the product within 18 months can be a bad one. The shape frames the *terrain*; the outcome depends on the specific negotiation.
- **Investor outcome depends on the price relative to the preference stack, not the shape.** A tuck-in at a healthy multiple can produce a better DPI than a transformative deal at a stretched one. The transformative label attracts attention; the waterfall math is what determines what the LPs actually receive.

The taxonomy is a *classification* to align expectations, not a preference ordering.

## How the shape and the acquirer sub-type interact

Any one of the four acquirer sub-types (from chapter 1) can produce any one of the four transaction shapes, though some combinations are more common than others:

| Acquirer sub-type | Most-common shape | Also-common | Rare-but-possible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjacency strategic | Tuck-in | Platform, aqui-hire | Transformative |
| Platform-extending strategic | Platform | Tuck-in | Transformative, aqui-hire |
| Defensive strategic | Any | (highly situation-dependent) | (situation-dependent) |
| Talent-consolidating strategic | Aqui-hire | (aqui-hire is close to defining) | Tuck-in |
| Buyout PE | (n/a — PE doesn't do the four-shape taxonomy the same way; see below) | | |
| Growth equity | (n/a — see below) | | |
| Take-private | (n/a) | | |
| Family office | (n/a) | | |

**A note on financial acquirers:** the four-shape taxonomy is a fundamentally strategic-acquirer frame — the shapes are defined by *what the acquirer will do with the acquired entity*, and financial acquirers typically do "operate it and exit it" regardless. For buyout PE, the analogous classifications are *platform vs. add-on*: the target is either the *platform* for a subsequent add-on M&A programme (bought to be the foundation) or an *add-on* to an existing PE-owned platform (bought to be folded into a portfolio company). Growth-equity deals are almost always minority/majority-stake investments where the operating entity continues largely as-is. Take-private and family-office deals have their own patterns. The four-shape taxonomy in this chapter is most useful when you are talking to a strategic acquirer; when you are talking to a financial acquirer, use the platform-vs-add-on frame instead and the acquirer-sub-type deep-dive from chapter 1.

## What each shape implies for what you should ask for

Given a plausible shape identification, some concrete negotiating implications:

### If it is an aqui-hire

- Prioritise the **retention structure** — vesting, acceleration, cash-vs-equity mix, key-person definitions, non-compete scope. Every dollar of retention is a real dollar; every extra month of vesting is a real month.
- Push for **MIP / carve-out design** for common holders whose economics are otherwise zeroed by the preference stack. Preferred holders sometimes agree to fund a carve-out in exchange for closing certainty and management alignment. This is a specific negotiation with the preferred holders (not the acquirer), typically done in parallel.
- Do not spend energy on **product-preservation terms** — the acquirer's plan for the product is largely fixed, and asking for guarantees is usually unproductive.
- Set expectations with the team early. An aqui-hire that closes cleanly is a good outcome; an aqui-hire that surprises the team on day one is a disaster.

### If it is a tuck-in

- Negotiate the **integration timeline and product-preservation window** — often 12–24 months of the acquired product staying named and separately-branded is realistic and worth getting in writing. Beyond that, integration is largely at the acquirer's discretion.
- Push for **founder / key-leadership post-close roles** with defined scope and duration. If you want the founder-CEO to have a business-unit-GM role for 24 months, get it explicit; if you want the option to depart at 12 months, get *that* explicit.
- Design the **earn-out** carefully, if there is one. Post-close, the acquirer controls the levers that drive earn-out achievement (sales incentives, product roadmap, integration decisions); earn-out disputes are a leading cause of post-close litigation (mod-104 chapter 6 depth).

### If it is a platform

- Focus on the **stock consideration terms** — collar / floor / ceiling / fixed-vs-floating exchange ratio, lock-up structure, registration rights. At platform scale the stock terms often matter as much as the headline price.
- Negotiate **executive-team-integration structure** rather than product-preservation — the acquirer is going to absorb the product; what you can negotiate is where your leadership lands post-close and how empowered they are.
- Prepare for **regulatory review**. Platform deals at scale often draw HSR second-request activity; the deal timing needs to accommodate that.

### If it is a transformative-strategic

- Every element is negotiable and every element matters. Board seats, reserved decision rights, retention-plan design, integration governance, stock terms, break-up fees, matching rights.
- The **process discipline** (special committee, fairness opinion, banker-conflict analysis) becomes a first-order concern; mod-109 owns the depth here, but pre-marketing choices affect it.
- The **communications choreography** — employees, customers, partners, press, regulators, activist investors, ISS / Glass Lewis — is a project unto itself. Start planning it early; mod-110 owns the depth.

## Classifying an inbound before the LOI

The single most useful skill this chapter installs is *classifying an inbound quickly* — often before the LOI is drafted, sometimes before the first serious conversation is over. Some diagnostics:

- **What is the acquirer asking about first?** Team → aqui-hire signal. Product functionality and revenue → tuck-in signal. Architecture and platform-fit → platform signal. Category-defining strategic ambition and generational thesis → transformative signal.
- **Who is in the room on their side?** Corp-dev alone with a BU sponsor → tuck-in. Corp-dev plus the platform CTO → platform. CEO-to-CEO from meeting one → potentially transformative. HR leadership involved early → aqui-hire.
- **How does the acquirer talk about integration?** "We'd absorb it into our X business" → tuck-in or platform. "We'd want the team to keep building" → aqui-hire (team) or platform (product). "We'd run it separately for a while and then decide" → could be transformative or a deferred-integration tuck-in.
- **How large is the price they are hinting at, relative to the acquirer's own market cap?** <1% → probably tuck-in or aqui-hire. 1–10% → tuck-in / platform. 10–25%+ → potentially transformative.
- **Are they asking about specific individuals by name early?** Aqui-hire signal.
- **Are they asking to meet with your top customers early?** Tuck-in / platform signal — they are diligencing revenue quality.

These are heuristics, not proofs. When they conflict, escalate to a direct conversation: "It would help us prepare if we understood how you are thinking about this — as a fold-in of the product into your X business, as a platform investment, or as something else?" A serious acquirer will answer the question; if they will not, that itself is signal.

## Failure modes

Three specific mis-classifications recur:

- **Negotiating a tuck-in as a platform.** The acquirer wants to fold your product into their existing offering; you insist on multi-year product-preservation covenants and a business-unit-GM role for the founder. The acquirer walks or reprices down because the terms you are asking for do not fit what they are actually buying.
- **Negotiating an aqui-hire as a tuck-in.** The acquirer is buying the team; you push for a revenue-multiple-anchored headline price. The acquirer walks. The team then finds out about the process, retention risk spikes, and any later offer arrives from a weaker position.
- **Negotiating a transformative deal as a platform.** The acquirer is making a category-defining bet and expects governance influence and integration authority proportional to the size of the check. You treat it as a routine platform acquisition. The deal collapses on governance terms in the definitive-agreements phase.

The remedy in all three is the same: *classify early, verify the classification with the acquirer directly if it is ambiguous, and structure the negotiation around the shape you have identified*.

## What this classification does not decide

Classification tells you the *shape* of the deal. It does not decide:

- **Whether to do the deal at all.** That is the endgame-frame question (mod-101).
- **The specific price.** Classification narrows the range but does not produce a number; that comes from the valuation frame (`startup-finance-fundraising-curriculum` mod-106) applied to the specific comparable transactions and public multiples.
- **The specific structural mechanics.** Asset vs. stock vs. reverse-triangular, escrow / holdback design, earn-out mechanics, tax structure under IRC §368 vs. §338(h)(10), R&W insurance design — mod-103 and mod-104 own those.
- **The fiduciary-duty process discipline.** Revlon triggering, special-committee formation, fairness-opinion procurement — mod-109 owns that.

Classification is a *first move*. It puts you on the right terrain. The rest of the module and the following modules build the depth for what you do next.

## Summary

Every M&A transaction fits (approximately) into one of four shapes — aqui-hire, tuck-in, platform, transformative-strategic — and each shape has a different consideration mix, retention structure, integration approach, and post-close governance profile. The shape is orthogonal to the acquirer sub-type from chapter 1: a single acquirer can offer any of the four shapes depending on how they see the target. Classifying an inbound quickly — from the acquirer's early questions, who is in the room, how they talk about integration, and the price-to-market-cap ratio — is the operator-skill that lets you negotiate against the right terms rather than the wrong ones. Common failure modes (negotiating a tuck-in as a platform, an aqui-hire as a tuck-in, a transformative as a platform) all stem from mis-classification early. Get the shape right first; the rest of the negotiation has a chance.
