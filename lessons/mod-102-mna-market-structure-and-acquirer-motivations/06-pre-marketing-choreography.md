# Pre-Marketing Choreography — Seeding a Competitive Process Without Appearing Shopped

## Why this matters

A well-run sell-side process converts a company from a private asset into a competitive auction, and the winner overpays. That is the mechanic. The single hardest problem in the process is *starting* it — how do you seed acquirer interest, gauge willingness to pay, and align internal readiness *without* the market perceiving you as "shopped." A company that is perceived as shopped is a company whose price is discounted by every subsequent bidder, whose deal-team credibility is eroded, and whose fallback (stay-independent, raise more capital, take a smaller deal) is materially weaker.

Pre-marketing is the choreographed set of conversations, signals, and timing choices that solves the seeding problem. It is not the formal process — the formal process launches later, with the CIM in the market and bid deadlines defined. Pre-marketing is the 3–9 months before the formal launch, where the founder-CEO and CFO run a series of legitimate business conversations that (from the outside) look nothing like a sale process but that (from the inside) surface acquirer interest, warm the top-tier of the buyer map, calibrate price expectations, and time the formal launch against market and internal-readiness conditions.

Done well, pre-marketing produces a formal process where the top-tier acquirers have already privately signalled interest, the price band is roughly calibrated, and the formal launch generates real competitive dynamics. Done poorly, pre-marketing produces either (a) a formal launch to a cold market where the top acquirers were surprised and unprepared, or (b) a widely-known "everyone knows they're shopping" situation where the price is capped before the process opens.

This chapter is about the specific patterns — the discovery meeting, the market-intelligence conversation, the strategic-partnership-first pattern — and about the timing rules that keep pre-marketing from cannibalising an ongoing-company fundraise or triggering a fiduciary-out obligation on an existing engagement.

## The core tension

Pre-marketing exists because two constraints pull in opposite directions.

**Constraint 1 — the price is set at the pre-marketing stage, not the process stage.** Sophisticated acquirers form price conviction long before a CIM lands on their desk. Their internal alignment on "would we pay $X for this asset" is built over conversations, competitive-observation, and category-thesis-formation that unfolds over months. If you launch a formal process cold — no prior acquirer conviction, no pre-existing category thesis in place — the acquirers show up with a low anchor and price toward it. The pre-marketing conversations are what move that anchor.

**Constraint 2 — the *appearance* of shopping caps price.** If acquirers believe the company is being actively shopped, three things happen: (1) they discount their bid because they infer the seller must sell, (2) they slow their process because there is no urgency ("if they are shopping, we will see them come back"), and (3) they share information with each other through the informal channels (bankers, ex-corp-dev, industry advisors) that always exist. The result is bids that cluster low with no upside pressure.

The resolution: the pre-marketing conversations happen, but they happen as *legitimate business conversations* (strategic partnerships, market-intelligence exchanges, product-integration discussions) that do not — from either side's stated framing — pertain to a sale. Both sides know, at the executive level, what is possible; neither side says it. This is the choreography.

## The three foundational patterns

Three patterns of pre-marketing conversation recur in real sell-side execution. All three are legitimate business conversations that produce M&A signal as a side-effect.

### 1. The market-intelligence conversation

**Framing:** "we're not trying to sell you anything, and we're not interested in anything you're selling — but we respect your view on how the market is shifting, and we'd like to compare notes."

**Cadence:** twice a year for top-tier strategic acquirers, less frequently for others. The founder-CEO or CFO takes the meeting with the acquirer's business-unit executive, corp-dev lead, or CTO.

**Content:** you share a view on where the category is heading, what customer patterns you are seeing, what technology trends you are betting on. They share their view. The conversation is genuinely useful to both sides — you learn what they are prioritising, they learn what you are seeing. No one asks the M&A question.

**What it does for M&A:** it (1) keeps the relationship warm and current, (2) surfaces their category priorities so you can update your strategic-fit scoring on the buyer map, (3) makes their team *know* the quality of your thinking, which is the highest-fidelity M&A signal an acquirer's decision-maker can receive, and (4) positions you as a peer rather than a supplicant.

**Where it goes wrong:** if you inject M&A language into a market-intelligence conversation, you have collapsed the frame and revealed the pre-marketing intent. Discipline: no M&A language. Ever. The M&A signal is entirely in the pattern of you showing up as a peer with valuable perspective.

### 2. The strategic-partnership-first pattern

**Framing:** "we should explore a partnership between our companies — product integration, joint go-to-market, or a specific commercial arrangement."

**Cadence:** episodic, driven by real partnership opportunities. Do not manufacture partnership conversations that have no business logic; they read as pretextual and undermine the trust that legitimate ones build.

**Content:** you explore a genuine commercial relationship — a product integration that benefits both sides' customers, a channel partnership, a technology arrangement. The conversation is between the operating teams (product, engineering, business development), not between M&A teams.

**What it does for M&A:** partnerships that go well produce two side-effects: (1) the acquirer's business-unit executive develops direct experience of your team's execution quality, and (2) the joint-work exposes the acquirer to your product, customer relationships, and operating rhythm at a depth no CIM can convey. When (later) an M&A conversation opens, the acquirer is already inside the tent — they know what they would be buying because they have been doing business with it.

**The escalation dynamic:** in a meaningful fraction of cases, a successful strategic partnership escalates into an M&A conversation initiated by the acquirer. "We've been thinking about this partnership; we wonder if we should just acquire you" is one of the most common origin stories for well-priced strategic acquisitions. The pattern is not manufactured; it emerges from the acquirer's own realisation that owning the target creates more value than partnering with it.

**Where it goes wrong:** manufacturing partnerships as pretext for M&A conversations. Acquirers detect this quickly (their corp-dev teams have seen the pattern hundreds of times), and the credibility damage is severe.

### 3. The discovery meeting

**Framing:** the acquirer's corp-dev or business-unit executive requests an "exploratory" or "get to know each other" meeting. Sometimes you request it, sometimes they do; the specific framing is usually mutual and slightly ambiguous — "let's grab coffee and compare notes," "our CEO would like to meet your CEO," "we're doing a market survey in this category and would value your perspective."

**Content:** high-level conversation between senior executives (founder-CEO to their VP-of-M&A or business-unit executive, sometimes CEO to CEO). You discuss the category, your business, their business, where the two might fit together in the future. No specific transaction is proposed by either side. The word "acquisition" may or may not be spoken; if it is, it is in the context of "we're not talking about a specific transaction, but as we think about our long-term picture, we want to understand each other."

**What it does for M&A:** the discovery meeting is where the *acquirer's internal thesis about you as an M&A target* gets formed or updated. The meeting is often the trigger for the acquirer's corp-dev to build a target-file on you internally, run some initial financial modelling, and start the multi-quarter process of alignment across their internal stakeholders that has to precede any real approach.

**The specific choreography:**
- Requested (from either side) with the frame of "let's stay in touch" or "we should compare notes."
- Executed at senior-executive level (CEO / CFO / VP-BD to their counterpart).
- Content is genuinely substantive — not a sales pitch, not a plea for interest.
- Follow-up (a short note, a shared reference, an offer to reconnect in 6 months) is standard.

**Where it goes wrong:** treating the discovery meeting as a sales pitch. If you show up with a pitch deck, name a price range, or ask "would you be interested," you have terminated the discovery frame and shifted into the (much less favourable) approach-with-a-hand-out frame. Discipline: no pitch, no price, no ask.

## The staging model

A well-choreographed pre-marketing programme moves through defined stages, typically over 6–18 months before the formal process opens.

### Stage 0: standing pre-marketing (indefinite)

The mod-101 buyer-map-maintenance discipline. The market-intelligence conversations happen at their normal cadence. The partnership conversations happen when real opportunities exist. The buyer map is updated quarterly. No specific M&A intent is present.

This is the default. It runs for years. It is what makes the later stages possible.

### Stage 1: internal-readiness building (6–12 months before formal launch)

The decision to run a formal process in the coming year has been made (by the founder-CEO, CFO, and board). External signalling has not begun. Internal work:
- Finance-team readiness — audited financials current, three-year projections built, Q-of-E preparation begun.
- Data-room preparation (mod-105) — architecture built, workstream owners named, initial documents populated.
- Legal readiness — corporate housekeeping current, contract-database organised, IP-and-employment review conducted.
- Banker-selection begun (chapter 4) — bake-off with 2–3 candidate banks over 2–3 months, mandate signed 4–6 months before launch.

**Pre-marketing during stage 1:** the standing programme continues. No signalling. If an inbound arrives, handled as a discovery meeting (see below).

### Stage 2: quiet warming (3–6 months before formal launch)

Banker engaged. Pre-marketing intensifies:
- Market-intelligence conversations with the top-tier buyer-map entries are re-cadenced to happen in the pre-marketing window.
- Partnership conversations that were already in flight are advanced where possible.
- Discovery meetings are accepted when offered; sometimes requested.
- The banker begins their own quiet-warming with the buyer set — informal calls with corp-dev leads to signal (implicitly) that a process may be coming.

**The banker's role:** the banker is the primary channel for the least-attributable warming — a corp-dev-to-corp-dev call between the banker and the acquirer can signal interest levels without you being in the frame at all. Bulge-bracket and top-tier boutique bankers have this warming machinery as a core competency.

### Stage 3: formal launch

The teaser goes out to the buyer list on a defined day. NDAs are executed; CIMs are distributed. The bid deadlines and process timeline are formalised. The pre-marketing period is over.

## Timing rules

Several timing rules govern the interaction of pre-marketing with other ongoing activities.

### Rule 1: pre-marketing and fundraising cannibalise each other

If you are running an ongoing-company fundraising process and pre-marketing an eventual sale simultaneously, you have a conflict.

- **From the acquirer's side:** an acquirer who learns you are raising a round infers that you are not seriously exploring a sale, discounts their interest, and slows their process. Simultaneously, an acquirer whose exploratory conversation you have taken may leak the interest to your prospective investors, who then re-price the round down (an M&A option is a signal of company distress in some investor frames).
- **From the fundraising side:** investors who learn you are exploring a sale will either (a) walk from the round entirely (they do not want to fund a bridge that leads to a near-term liquidity event that dilutes their money), or (b) re-price against the expected acquisition value.

**The resolution:** typically do one, not both, in a defined window.
- If the endgame frame favours *sale over fundraising*, run pre-marketing openly (in choreographed form) and do not simultaneously pursue a competitive fundraise. If capital is needed to bridge to the sale, a bridge round from existing investors or venture debt is often the answer.
- If the endgame frame favours *fundraising over sale in this window*, pause meaningful pre-marketing until the fundraise closes. Continue the standing buyer-map maintenance; do not initiate new discovery meetings.
- If a dual-track (fundraise-or-sale) is genuinely correct — which is a specific and relatively rare situation — the choreography requires explicit board approval, a defined narrative to both channels, and careful management of the disclosure boundaries. mod-107 develops the dual-track pattern for IPO / strategic-sale processes; the fundraise / M&A dual-track uses similar disciplines.

### Rule 2: pre-marketing and existing commercial relationships intersect

If you have a strategic partnership, distribution agreement, or major commercial contract with a plausible acquirer, the M&A conversation and the commercial conversation are entangled. Some patterns:

- **Advance the commercial relationship first if possible.** A strong commercial relationship gives the acquirer more information about you and typically a stronger conviction on the M&A thesis. Do not sacrifice a well-performing commercial relationship for premature M&A signalling.
- **Watch for change-of-control provisions in the commercial contract.** If your commercial contract with a strategic acquirer has a change-of-control termination right, the acquirer's own leverage in an M&A conversation is materially different. mod-101 chapter 5's optionality-preserving-operating-decisions audit flags this; here the point is that pre-marketing choreography with a partner-slash-acquirer is a distinct pattern.
- **Do not disclose your process to a commercial-relationship partner without explicit intent.** Even trusted partners are, in an M&A frame, competing bidders. Confidentiality discipline applies.

### Rule 3: pre-marketing conversations do not trigger fiduciary-out or no-shop obligations — unless they do

A key legal-and-fiduciary line: pre-marketing conversations that are *legitimate business conversations* (market-intelligence, partnership exploration, discovery meetings) do not, on their own, trigger fiduciary-out obligations under an existing engagement (a signed LOI with another party, for example, that has a no-shop provision) or Revlon-style scrutiny in a public-company context. Conversations that *are* soliciting an acquisition offer, or that constitute a substantive discussion of transaction terms, may trigger those obligations.

**Practical implications:**
- If you have signed an LOI with a preferred acquirer that includes a no-shop provision, the no-shop typically restricts your ability to *solicit* other offers. The line between "solicit" and "respond to inbound" is usually the fiduciary-out escape valve. Discovery meetings with third parties during an active no-shop are risky and require external-counsel review before happening.
- If you are approached during an active no-shop by another acquirer, the fiduciary-out (if the LOI includes one — most do, for public companies and increasingly for private ones) governs how the board must consider a superior proposal. mod-104 develops LOI mechanics and mod-109 develops the Revlon / superior-proposal framework.
- In a private-company pre-marketing context (no signed LOI, no active engagement letter with a specific acquirer), the line between market-intelligence conversation and pre-marketing is legally largely irrelevant — but strategically it matters (see the two rules above).

### Rule 4: the pre-marketing period has a defined end

Pre-marketing that runs indefinitely becomes shopping. A rough rule: the intensive pre-marketing stages (stage 2 above) should not exceed 6 months. Beyond that, the accumulated conversations, folklore, and process signals produce a market perception that a process is underway — and the "not-shopped" premise collapses. If the internal readiness to launch a formal process is not there at the 6-month mark, either accelerate the readiness or pause the intensive pre-marketing and return to the standing programme (stage 0).

## Scenario patterns

Pre-marketing rarely follows the clean staging model. Several specific scenarios warrant tailored choreography.

### Scenario A: an unsolicited inbound arrives cold

An acquirer you had scored as depth-1 or depth-2 sends an unsolicited inbound with specific transaction language ("we'd like to explore acquiring your company").

**Choreography:**
- Do not respond alone. Involve the CFO and board chair (or independent director if the founder is chair) before responding.
- Respond with a controlled meeting — "we're happy to have a discovery conversation to understand your interest." Do not commit to sharing information yet.
- Assess the seriousness of the inbound (chapter 2's shape-classification is the starting point). Is this a real approach, or a fishing expedition? Is the sender a decision-maker or a corp-dev associate testing the waters?
- If serious, decide whether to run a broader process. A single-bidder situation is systematically worse for the seller than a competitive one; unless the price and terms of the inbound are compelling on their own, the right response is often "we appreciate the interest and would like to think about it" while accelerating the pre-marketing plan to bring in competitive tension before the inbound solidifies.
- If less serious, treat as a discovery meeting and warm the relationship.

### Scenario B: a competitor is acquired

A close competitor is acquired at a public multiple. Your buyer map's category-adjacency section just got materially rearranged. Every plausible acquirer in your space is now (a) newly aware of the category as an M&A target, (b) newly aware of a comparable-transaction multiple, and (c) newly under pressure to move quickly on any remaining independent targets.

**Choreography:**
- Accelerate pre-marketing selectively. The window during which acquirers are actively thinking about the category is finite (typically 3–9 months post-announcement); after that, attention shifts.
- Update the buyer map. A newly-priced comparable transaction changes the ability-to-pay analysis and the pricing-conversation anchor.
- Do not signal desperation. The natural temptation is to move fast — moving *too* fast signals that you are reacting to the competitor's exit rather than executing a planned process.
- Consider whether the acquirer of the competitor is now off the buyer map (they have already made their category bet), or newly on it more strongly (they need to consolidate and might buy more).

### Scenario C: fundraise is mid-flight when a serious M&A inbound arrives

An M&A inbound arrives during an active fundraising process. Both are live; both are attractive.

**Choreography:**
- Pause and consult. This is a board-level decision; involve the board chair and lead investor immediately.
- Assess which path is stronger. A serious M&A inbound at a valuation above the fundraising round's expected pre-money is a strong signal to shift to a formal sale process; an M&A inbound at a valuation below is often a distraction.
- If shifting to sale: notify fundraising investors that the process is paused. Some will respect the shift; some will feel misled. Manage the relationship carefully — you may need those investors in a bridge or in a future round if the sale does not close.
- If continuing the fundraise: park the M&A inbound with a soft "we appreciate the interest and would like to reconnect after we complete our current financing" — most serious acquirers will respect this, though some will interpret it as declining.
- If dual-tracking: explicit board approval, defined narrative to both channels, and a hard deadline for a decision point.

### Scenario D: pre-marketing has been running for 8+ months and no formal process has launched

You are in the "shopping" perception zone. The market has heard enough about your discovery meetings, your banker's warming calls, and your absent-from-conferences appearance that the informal folklore has consolidated: "they're for sale."

**Choreography:**
- Diagnose the root cause of the delay. Internal readiness gap? Board misalignment? Market-window uncertainty? Founder personal-readiness gap?
- Decide: launch the formal process within a defined near-term window (30–60 days), or fully pause and return to stage 0.
- If launching: accept that the pre-marketing has partly primed and partly damaged your position. The primed acquirers know they are competing; the market-priced-in-shopping effect will show in the initial bids. Manage expectations accordingly.
- If pausing: an explicit "we've decided to focus on building the business" narrative to investors and (through the banker) to the acquirer set can re-close the "shopped" perception over 6–12 months, especially if paired with a strong operating quarter. This is a recovery move, not a first choice.

### Scenario E: the founder-CEO is genuinely uncertain whether to sell

Not a scenario about market conditions; a scenario about the founder. The rolling buyer map is warm, an inbound is credible, but the founder-CEO is genuinely torn between selling and continuing.

**Choreography:**
- Do not run pre-marketing while uncertain. The founder-CEO's ambivalence will bleed into every conversation and undermine both paths.
- Use the endgame-optionality memo discipline (mod-101 chapter 6) to force the decision to a defensible position before signalling to the market.
- If the honest answer is "I don't know," the honest signal is stage-0 standing pre-marketing until the answer becomes clearer. Do not use pre-marketing conversations to make the decision for you — the market noise you create is real, and unwinding it is expensive.

## Confidentiality discipline

Pre-marketing operates in a confidentiality-sensitive environment. Some standing disciplines:

- **The "deal team clean list."** Inside the company, only a defined small group (founder-CEO, CFO, GC, board members, and 2–3 named executives on a need-to-know basis) knows the pre-marketing is happening. mod-110 chapter 1 develops the employee-communications discipline; here the pattern is: keep the circle small.
- **NDAs before CIMs.** Every acquirer receives the CIM only after signing an NDA. The NDA is standard-form, typically 2–3 years, includes a non-solicitation of employees clause, and (in some cases) a standstill clause. mod-104 develops the NDA / standstill mechanics.
- **Standstill provisions for public-target-adjacent contexts.** If the target is public, or if the acquirer is a public competitor and the diligence would expose material non-public information about them, standstill provisions restrict the acquirer's ability to accumulate stock or make unsolicited offers during the diligence period.
- **Selective attribution.** In market-intelligence conversations and discovery meetings, the founder-CEO carefully attributes any specific market signal to a source that does not reveal your process. "We're hearing X from customers" is different from "our banker tells us Y."
- **Never confirm shopping.** If a journalist, competitor, or industry contact asks whether you are for sale, the answer is a consistent "we don't comment on M&A speculation." Anything more specific is either an admission (bad) or a denial that will be shown to be false (worse).

## Coordination with the banker (if engaged)

If a banker is engaged, they are the primary owner of the choreography's execution.

- **The banker manages the buyer-warming calls.** Bulge-bracket and top-tier boutique bankers have long-standing corp-dev relationships that can carry a warming call better than the founder-CEO can. Delegate this deliberately.
- **The founder-CEO owns the CEO-to-CEO calls.** For top-tier strategic acquirers where a peer-CEO conversation is the appropriate signal, the founder-CEO takes the meeting personally, with the banker briefing beforehand.
- **The banker maintains the buyer-map process log.** Every touch, every response, every escalation is logged and reviewed at least weekly.
- **The banker owns the "not-shopped" positioning discipline.** Well-experienced bankers know exactly how to frame a warming call so that the acquirer receives real M&A signal without the "shopped" perception setting in.

## Failure modes

Six failure modes recur in pre-marketing:

- **Shopping-perception collapse.** Discussed above. Cure: staged pre-marketing with a defined end; if the launch does not happen within 6 months, fully pause.
- **Cannibalising the fundraise.** Cure: pick one; do not pursue both without explicit dual-track choreography.
- **Discovery-meeting-turned-pitch.** Cure: discipline the founder-CEO to *not* pitch in discovery meetings; if you pitch, the frame is broken.
- **Manufactured-partnership pretext.** Cure: only use the partnership pattern when there is a real partnership opportunity.
- **Confidentiality break.** Cure: the deal-team clean list, standing communications discipline, "we don't comment on M&A speculation" as a reflex.
- **Ambivalent-founder pre-marketing.** Cure: the endgame-optionality memo before any pre-marketing signalling.

## Summary

Pre-marketing is the choreographed set of conversations, signals, and timing choices that seeds acquirer interest and warms the buyer map without triggering the "shopped" perception that would cap price. Three foundational patterns — the market-intelligence conversation, the strategic-partnership-first pattern, the discovery meeting — carry the M&A signal as a side-effect of legitimate business conversations. Pre-marketing runs in staged fashion (standing → readiness-building → quiet-warming → formal launch), typically 6–18 months before formal launch, with defined end conditions that prevent it from running indefinitely. Timing rules govern the interaction with fundraises, commercial relationships, and existing engagements. Scenario-specific choreography addresses inbounds, competitor exits, dual-track dilemmas, prolonged pre-marketing, and founder ambivalence. Confidentiality discipline (deal-team clean list, NDAs, standstill provisions, no-comment reflex) is standing. The banker (if engaged) owns the execution; the founder-CEO owns the CEO-to-CEO calls and the ambivalence-resolution work. Done well, pre-marketing produces a formal process where the top-tier acquirers arrive with conviction and the price band is roughly calibrated; done poorly, it produces a cold formal launch or a "shopped" market that caps price before the process opens. mod-104's LOI mechanics and mod-109's fiduciary-duty depth cover what happens once a formal process delivers a bid; this module ends with the pre-marketing choreography that gets you to that point.
