# The Investment-Bank Engagement Decision

## Why this matters

The bank you hire (or the choice not to hire one) is the single largest structural decision you make about a sell-side process before it begins. A bank's brand-name, sector expertise, buyer-network, and process-management capability determine which acquirers take your outreach seriously, how price gets negotiated, how quickly diligence resolves, and how the deal team behaves at 2am the night before a signing that is about to slip. A wrong-fit bank costs money on both the fee stack and the deal outcome. A right-fit bank often pays for itself several times over. And a well-run no-banker process (yes, it exists) is a different discipline that requires the founder-CEO / CFO to install the process muscle a bank would otherwise provide.

This chapter is about that decision — sole-mandate vs. dual-mandate, tier-1 bulge-bracket vs. sector-boutique vs. Big-Four vs. no-banker, and the retainer + success-fee + expense economics that shape the banker's incentives once the mandate is signed. It is not about how to run the sell-side once the banker is engaged (that runs through the rest of this module); it is about which banker (or none) to engage in the first place, and what the engagement letter should say.

## The main structural choices

Two structural choices frame the entire discussion.

### Sole mandate vs. dual mandate vs. no banker

- **Sole mandate:** one bank owns the sell-side. They are the primary process-runner, primary buyer-outreach channel, primary negotiator on your behalf. The engagement letter typically names them as exclusive sell-side advisor for a defined period (12–24 months), with success fees earned on any deal closed with any acquirer during that period (and often a defined tail thereafter).
- **Dual mandate:** two banks share the sell-side. Usually the two divide by strategic-vs-financial buyer coverage, by geographic coverage, or by lead-vs-co-manager role. Fee-splitting is negotiated; success fees are typically higher in aggregate (dividing the pie is more expensive than baking one, and dual-mandate mechanics add coordination overhead).
- **No banker:** the founder-CEO / CFO runs the sell-side directly, with external legal counsel and possibly a smaller advisory shop (a former banker acting in an advisory rather than mandate capacity). More common in specific situations — smaller deals (below, say, $50M–$100M), tuck-in / aqui-hire transactions with a known preferred buyer, situations where the founder has genuine banker-quality process experience.

Sole mandate is the modal choice for a serious sell-side above ~$100M. Dual mandate is a niche choice usually driven by specific coverage gaps a single bank cannot fill. No-banker is common at smaller sizes and specific-shape deals but is uncommon in a competitive multi-buyer process at scale.

### Bulge-bracket vs. sector-boutique vs. Big-Four vs. specialist

Within the sole-mandate / dual-mandate space, four categories of firm compete.

**Bulge-bracket investment banks (tier-1):**
- Firms: Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Bank of America Merrill Lynch, Citigroup, Barclays, Credit Suisse (historically, now UBS), Deutsche Bank, Wells Fargo. For technology M&A specifically, Goldman's TMT group, Morgan Stanley's TMT group, and JPMorgan's TMT group are the reference points.
- **Strengths:** deep global buyer coverage across strategics and financials, deep balance sheet if debt financing or hedging is relevant, extensive relationships with public-company CEOs and boards, brand-name signalling that gets meetings scheduled and takes acquirer boards seriously.
- **Weaknesses:** at smaller deal sizes, you are a low-priority client and the deal team is typically junior. Tier-1 banks generally focus on transactions above ~$500M–$1B. At $200M–$500M you can get their attention but not their top team; below $200M the fit is usually poor.
- **Fee structure:** success-fee percentages that decline with deal size — smaller deals attract higher percentages (1.5–3%), larger deals lower (0.5–1.5% for multi-billion-dollar deals). Retainers are typically $150k–$500k. Break-up-fee and reimbursement provisions vary.
- **Best fit:** deals above $500M–$1B where global buyer coverage and public-company / regulatory sophistication matter.

**Sector boutiques (specialist bulge-alternative):**
- Firms in technology M&A: **Qatalyst Partners** (Frank Quattrone's firm, long-standing tech-M&A specialist with a defined position on tech-strategic transactions), **Centerview Partners** (broader-industry, deep tech coverage, high-touch process), **Evercore** (broader-industry with strong tech), **LionTree** (media / technology / consumer-technology specialist), **Union Square Advisors** (technology-focused mid-market), **Financial Technology Partners** (FT Partners — fintech specialist), **GCA Advisors** (mid-market technology, now part of Houlihan Lokey), **Raine Group** (media / entertainment / consumer-technology). In life sciences / biotech: **Torreya**, **Wells Fargo Healthcare**, and specialist boutiques. In industrials, healthcare-services, and consumer: analogous specialist firms.
- **Strengths:** deep sector knowledge (partners have often spent decades in the vertical), often stronger relationships in specific-sub-vertical corp-dev shops than the bulge-brackets, senior-partner attention on the mandate (you are a top client, not a bottom-quartile one), often better creative-structuring judgement on non-standard transactions.
- **Weaknesses:** narrower balance sheet (no debt financing or hedging capability), narrower geographic reach (some boutiques are US-centric), sometimes less credibility on very-large public-company transactions where the target's board wants a full-service bank in the room.
- **Fee structure:** success-fee percentages comparable to bulge-brackets on similarly-sized deals, sometimes slightly higher (0.75–1.5% on mid-to-large deals). Retainers are similar or slightly lower.
- **Best fit:** deals in the $200M–$5B range where sector expertise and senior attention matter more than global-full-service-bank capability.

**Big-Four M&A groups:**
- Firms: Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG. Their transaction-advisory or corporate-finance arms have expanded into sell-side M&A advisory, especially in the lower-middle-market and international mid-market.
- **Strengths:** integrated audit-and-transaction-advisory (relevant if the same firm is the target's audit firm and can leverage that knowledge), international network, competitive pricing at the lower end of the mid-market.
- **Weaknesses:** less name-brand recognition with US technology strategics and financials (though this is category-dependent — Big-Four is more competitive in industrials, healthcare-services, and cross-border deals), often less senior-partner attention.
- **Fee structure:** typically lower than boutique / bulge-bracket on similar-size deals, but the buyer-coverage delta may cost you more than the fee saves.
- **Best fit:** lower-middle-market deals (typically $50M–$300M), cross-border deals where their international network is a differentiator, deals where audit-firm knowledge transfer is a real advantage.

**Specialist advisors and no-banker options:**
- Ex-banker independent advisors, restructuring firms (for distressed-adjacent deals), M&A brokerages (for the lower end of the mid-market and below).
- **Strengths:** flexible fee structures, lower cost, senior-only attention.
- **Weaknesses:** limited process infrastructure (no full deal team of associates and analysts), narrower buyer-network, less institutional credibility.
- **Best fit:** smaller deals, aqui-hire and tuck-in transactions with a known buyer or narrow buyer set, situations where the founder has deep banker-relationships already and wants a light-touch advisor.

## The decision framework

Six variables shape the choice.

### 1. Deal-size band

Where does the plausible transaction land on the size axis? A crude first-cut fit map (deals in USD):

| Deal size | Modal fit |
|---|---|
| <$50M | No banker, or specialist advisor. Bulge-bracket is not economic; boutique is often not economic either. |
| $50M–$200M | Big-Four, mid-market boutique, or larger-boutique's lower-priority team. Sometimes no-banker if the buyer set is very narrow. |
| $200M–$500M | Sector boutique is typically the best fit. Bulge-bracket possible but you are a lower-priority client. |
| $500M–$2B | Sector boutique or bulge-bracket both viable; the choice hinges on sector expertise, buyer coverage, and the specific deal shape. |
| $2B–$10B | Bulge-bracket typically leads, with a boutique as co-advisor or independent advisor in some cases. |
| >$10B | Bulge-bracket lead is the modal shape; specific transactions have used dual bulge-bracket mandates with boutique-as-independent-advisor structures. |

These are not laws. Specific deals cross these bands based on sector, situation, and relationship. But the modal fits above are a good starting point.

### 2. Buyer-set shape

Who is on your buyer map (chapter 3)?

- **If your top-tier buyer set is dominated by strategics you already have deep relationships with**, the value of a banker's buyer coverage is lower — the banker's relationship-adder is smaller when you already have the doors open. In this case, the process-management, negotiation-tactics, and structuring-judgment functions of the banker matter more than the buyer-coverage function.
- **If your top-tier buyer set includes financial acquirers you do not have relationships with**, banker buyer-coverage matters more — bankers know PE funds' current focus, active deployment status, and specific senior-partner interests better than any founder can from the outside. This tilts toward bulge-bracket or well-known sector boutiques with strong financial-buyer coverage.
- **If your top-tier buyer set includes acquirers in geographies where you have no presence**, cross-border reach matters. Bulge-bracket or Big-Four fits better than a US-centric boutique.

### 3. Sector complexity

Does your business live in a sector where domain knowledge is a differentiator in the pitch, or one where the frame is generic enough that any competent M&A banker can deliver it?

- **High sector-specificity:** enterprise-software with vertical-focus (fintech, healthtech, legaltech, edtech, etc.), biotech, semiconductor design, hardware / hardtech, defense. Sector boutique often wins here because their sector knowledge is not learnable in a bake-off.
- **Medium sector-specificity:** general-purpose enterprise SaaS, consumer-internet, digital-media. Both sector boutique and bulge-bracket can deliver competently.
- **Low sector-specificity:** general-industry deals where the sector expertise is not the crux of the pitch. Big-Four, bulge-bracket, or sector-generic boutique are all viable.

### 4. Process-complexity requirements

- **Simple process** (single acquirer already engaged, tuck-in shape, minimal regulatory concerns): no-banker or light-touch advisor is often fine.
- **Standard process** (5–15 acquirers, standard shape, some HSR/CFIUS): sector boutique or bulge-bracket.
- **Complex process** (broad process with 20+ acquirers, regulatory concerns, potentially competitive tender, board-committee governance): bulge-bracket or top-tier sector boutique with process-management infrastructure.

### 5. Founder / CFO bandwidth

The banker is, in part, a bandwidth-buy — they replace hundreds of hours of founder-CEO / CFO time on process management, data-room coordination, and buyer-response tracking. If you are already at bandwidth capacity with the ongoing business, hiring a strong banker is worth it even if you could theoretically run the process yourself. If you have real bandwidth and deep experience (a serial-founder-CEO with prior sell-side experience, for example), the no-banker or light-touch option becomes viable.

### 6. Board and investor preferences

Some boards require a formal banker mandate as a matter of process discipline (for Delaware fiduciary-defensibility reasons, mod-109 depth). Some investors have preferences for specific banks based on their own relationships and prior experience. These preferences are legitimate inputs to the decision, but do not let them dominate — the *best-fit* banker for your specific deal beats the *preferred* banker of a board member 8 times out of 10.

## The fee stack

Once you have chosen the banker, the engagement letter negotiation is a distinct exercise. Four components matter.

### 1. Retainer

The banker charges an upfront and periodic retainer, typically credited against the success fee at closing.

- **Typical range:** $50k–$500k depending on firm and deal complexity, with retainers concentrated at the higher end for larger and more complex mandates.
- **Structure:** sometimes a single upfront payment, more often a monthly retainer ($25k–$100k per month) for the duration of the process.
- **Credited against success fee:** almost always; the retainer is effectively an interest-free advance on the success fee.
- **Non-refundable component:** part of the retainer may be non-refundable even if the deal does not close. Negotiate this down where possible.

The retainer aligns incentives in one direction (the banker has a real economic stake in launching the process) but the credit-against-success-fee mechanic means the banker's real economic exposure is the success fee, not the retainer.

### 2. Success fee

The main event. Structured as a percentage of aggregate consideration paid.

- **Base structure:** a percentage of the total transaction value (cash + stock + earn-out + rollover + assumed debt — the specific definition matters and is negotiable).
- **Percentage curve:** typically tiered. For a large-cap deal the percentages might look like 1% on the first $500M, 0.75% on the next $500M, 0.5% on the excess. For a mid-market deal the percentages are higher and less tiered. Specific structures vary meaningfully.
- **Modified Lehman formula:** a classic mid-market structure where the fee is 5% on the first $1M, 4% on the second, 3% on the third, 2% on the fourth, 1% on everything above $4M. Common at smaller deal sizes but rarely used in tech-M&A at scale.
- **Minimum fee:** many engagement letters include a minimum fee — often $2M–$10M depending on firm and deal — that applies regardless of the transaction size. This matters if a smaller deal closes than everyone anticipated.
- **Incentive fee (kicker):** some engagement letters include a threshold-and-kicker structure where the banker earns an incremental percentage on consideration above a defined threshold. Structured well, this aligns the banker toward pushing hard on price; structured poorly, it creates perverse incentives to hold out for a higher price when a lower certain deal is available.

**The definition of "transaction value" matters more than the percentage.** Common negotiation points:

- Does the base include rollover equity, or only new-money consideration to shareholders?
- Does the base include seller notes, CVRs, and contingent payments at face value, present value, or only when earned?
- Does the base include assumed debt?
- Does the base include cash retention pool, or is that a separate carve-out from banker economics?
- What happens if the deal is renegotiated post-signing at a lower value?

Get these definitions explicit before signing the engagement letter. Ambiguity at signing produces disputes at closing that are always resolved in the banker's favour because they wrote the language.

### 3. Expenses

Bankers charge for out-of-pocket expenses — travel, virtual-data-room fees, third-party research, printing, filing fees, etc.

- **Structure:** typically at-cost, with some banks charging a small administrative fee on top.
- **Cap:** negotiable and worth caveating — many engagement letters include no expense cap by default, and expenses can run into the hundreds of thousands on a complex deal. A cap of $250k–$500k with approval required beyond that is a reasonable target.
- **Approval process:** negotiate advance-approval requirements for meaningful expenses.

### 4. Tail / trailing rights

If the mandate ends without a deal, the banker often retains rights to a fee on any deal that closes within a defined tail period, if the eventual buyer was contacted during the mandate.

- **Typical tail:** 12–24 months post-termination.
- **Coverage:** the banker earns a fee on any deal with any buyer they "introduced" or "contacted" during the mandate. Definitions of "introduced" and "contacted" matter — a buyer the banker sent a teaser to is usually covered; a buyer whose CEO you were already talking to before engaging the banker is often carved out.
- **Named list:** best practice is to attach a named list of buyers to the engagement letter distinguishing "pre-existing contacts" (not covered) from "banker-introduced" (covered).

The tail matters if the process does not close a deal on the first attempt and you re-open it later or accept an unsolicited approach — the tail can dramatically constrain your ability to run the second process without the original banker.

## The incentive structure

Understanding banker incentives is the difference between using the banker well and being used by them.

**Where banker incentives align with yours:**
- Get the deal *closed* — a deal that does not close pays no success fee.
- Get the price *high* — a higher price pays a higher fee (typically, with tiering curves and thresholds).
- Get the deal closed *soon* — bankers work on multiple mandates and want to move on; a lingering mandate is a resource drain.

**Where banker incentives diverge from yours:**
- The banker is often willing to accept a *slightly worse* price on a *slightly earlier* close, because the marginal fee-percentage on the extra price at the margin is small compared to the opportunity cost of a delayed close. Watch for this in the final-round negotiation; it is where "you should take the offer" pressure from the banker sometimes exceeds what the deal actually justifies.
- The banker's relationships with acquirers are *ongoing*. Your relationship with a specific acquirer is *transactional*. The banker has an incentive not to burn the acquirer relationship over a specific deal in ways you may have no reason to preserve.
- The banker's fee is based on *aggregate transaction value*, which may or may not align with what the seller shareholders actually receive after the preference stack, escrow, and earn-out realities. A high headline number with a low common recovery pays the banker in full and pays your team badly.
- The banker's *next* mandate may be with an acquirer who bid on your deal. Explicit conflict-of-interest disclosures are standard; watch what is not disclosed (soft relationships, ongoing business dependencies, personal networks).

The remedy is not distrust; it is *specific process discipline*. Read the engagement letter word-for-word. Negotiate the transaction-value definition. Get the buyer-list carve-outs explicit. Watch the pace of pressure toward acceptance and calibrate against what the process actually shows. The bank is a professional service provider whose interests overlap significantly with yours but do not equal yours; treat the relationship accordingly.

## Rural Metro and banker-conflict discipline

For public-company sell-side transactions, banker conflicts of interest have been the subject of specific Delaware case law that shapes what conflicts must be disclosed, cleared, and mitigated. The Court of Chancery's decision in **In re Rural Metro Corp. Stockholders Litigation** (Del. Ch. 2014) held a sell-side banker liable for aiding and abetting the target board's breach of fiduciary duty in part because the banker had undisclosed relationships with a bidder and manipulated the process to benefit that bidder.

For private-company deals, the fiduciary framework is less bright-line, but the underlying discipline transfers: undisclosed conflicts corrode process defensibility. Best practice is to require the banker to disclose in writing, at engagement and updated throughout the process: any advisory relationship with any bidder, any prior transaction with any bidder in the last 24 months, any personal / family / financial relationship between banker personnel and bidder personnel, and any staple-financing arrangements with financial bidders. Staple-financing conflicts (where the sell-side banker is also offering debt financing to the buyer) are a specific category worth explicit attention (see the Court of Chancery's *In re Del Monte Foods Co. Shareholders Litigation* (Del. Ch. 2011) for the reference case).

mod-109 develops the full fiduciary-duty and banker-conflict frame at deal-scale. For this chapter, the takeaway is: get the disclosure in writing at engagement, update it throughout the process, and treat undisclosed conflicts as engagement-terminating events.

## The no-banker sell-side

For a specific class of deals, running without a banker is a legitimate — sometimes preferable — choice.

**When no-banker fits:**
- Small deals (below ~$50M–$100M) where the banker fee stack is disproportionate to the deal size.
- Tuck-in / aqui-hire transactions with a known preferred buyer and a narrow buyer set.
- Situations where the founder-CEO / CFO has genuine banker-quality experience (serial founders, ex-bankers, ex-corp-dev leaders).
- Situations where the deal is essentially pre-agreed and the sell-side is confirmatory rather than competitive.

**What the founder-CEO / CFO needs to install to make it work:**
- **External legal counsel with sell-side M&A experience.** This is non-negotiable — the definitive-agreement negotiation is not a place to economise. Firms with tech-M&A depth include Cooley, Wilson Sonsini, Gunderson Dettmer, Fenwick, Latham, DLA Piper, and Orrick, among others.
- **A finance leader (CFO or Head-of-Finance) with sell-side experience or a specialist advisor.** The financial-model preparation, working-capital-adjustment negotiation, and diligence-response coordination require the discipline a banker would otherwise provide.
- **A defined process cadence.** Even without a banker, run a real process — CIM (or its equivalent), formal indications of interest, staged rounds, defined bid deadlines. The absence of a banker does not mean the absence of process discipline; it means you own installing it.
- **A specialist advisor for specific gaps.** An ex-banker acting as an advisor (paid on a fixed / hourly basis rather than a success fee) can fill specific coverage gaps at a much lower cost than a full mandate.

**What you sacrifice:** the banker's brand-signalling to acquirer boards (some strategics take a banker's outreach more seriously than a founder's), the banker's process-management scale (many hours of associate-and-analyst work you now have to do yourself or via advisor), and — in a formal fiduciary-review context — the presumption of process discipline that a banker mandate contributes to.

## What the engagement letter should say

If you are hiring a banker, the engagement letter is your primary protection. Some specific line items to negotiate:

- **Scope of engagement:** be specific about what the banker is retained to do (advise on and effect a sale of the company) and what they are not (arrange debt financing, provide fairness opinion, etc.). A separate fairness-opinion engagement — often at a fixed fee, sometimes with the same firm and sometimes with an independent second firm — is negotiated separately and mod-109 develops the fairness-opinion mechanics.
- **Term and termination:** typical 12–24 months, with termination rights (with defined tail). Include a termination-for-cause provision that lets you exit if the banker's performance is materially deficient.
- **Success-fee definition of "transaction value":** as discussed above, negotiate every component.
- **Retainer and credit-against-success-fee mechanics:** get the credit explicit.
- **Expense structure and cap:** as discussed.
- **Tail structure and buyer-list carve-outs:** attach a named-list schedule.
- **Indemnification:** standard indemnification of the banker by the client for the engagement, subject to a fraud / gross-negligence carve-out. Negotiate the carve-out.
- **Conflict-disclosure obligations:** as discussed.
- **Confidentiality:** mutual.
- **Governing law and dispute resolution:** typically New York or Delaware, arbitration for disputes.
- **The written buyer list:** attached as a schedule identifying pre-existing contacts.

Cooley GO's engagement-letter templates and commentary are a useful reference (public materials — see resources). Most first-cut engagement letters from bulge-bracket banks lean toward the bank on every negotiable point; get outside counsel involved in the redline.

## Summary

The banker-engagement decision has two axes: sole vs. dual vs. no banker, and (within the banker path) bulge-bracket vs. sector boutique vs. Big-Four vs. specialist. The choice is shaped by deal-size band, buyer-set shape, sector complexity, process complexity, founder / CFO bandwidth, and board / investor preferences. Once the choice is made, the engagement letter — retainer, success fee, expense structure, tail, buyer-list carve-outs, conflict disclosure — is the primary artefact protecting your interests through the mandate. Bulge-bracket firms bring global buyer coverage and balance-sheet capability at their attention threshold ($500M+); sector boutiques bring senior-partner attention and sector depth at mid-market scale; Big-Four brings integrated audit / advisory and international reach at competitive pricing at the lower end; no-banker fits smaller and narrower-shape deals when the founder / CFO can install the process discipline directly. The banker's incentives overlap yours but do not equal yours; the difference is where the engagement letter and the process discipline earn their keep. Rural Metro / Del Monte conflict discipline shapes the disclosure obligations for any bank you engage; mod-109 develops the full fiduciary-duty context.
