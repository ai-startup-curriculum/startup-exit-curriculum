# exercise-01: Strategic vs Financial Acquirer Typology Drill

**Estimated effort:** 2.5 hours

## Objective

Take a real, current list of acquirers active in your company's sector — a mix of strategics and financials — and classify each one into the eight-sub-type taxonomy from chapter 1. For each classified acquirer, derive their expected pricing model, integration approach, and negotiating behaviour from the sub-type profile plus public evidence. By the end you should be able to look at any inbound in your space and, within a paragraph of justification, name what kind of buyer is across the table and what kind of deal to expect.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 1 — Strategic vs Financial Acquirer Typology](../01-strategic-vs-financial-acquirer-typology.md)

Chapter 2 (transaction-type taxonomy) is supporting material — the shape of a specific deal (aqui-hire / tuck-in / platform / transformative) sits on top of the acquirer sub-type; do not conflate them in this exercise. You classify the *acquirer*, not the transaction.

## Prerequisites

- The company profile you carried through mod-101 (or a comparable realistic hypothetical).
- Access to SEC EDGAR (for public-acquirer 10-K / 10-Q / 8-K / DEF 14A filings), PitchBook or CB Insights or Crunchbase (for private-acquirer intelligence, public sources fine if you lack subscriptions), the target acquirers' investor-relations sites, and their recent earnings-call transcripts (published on IR sites or via Seeking Alpha / SEC filings).
- A spreadsheet or Notion / Coda / Airtable table.

## Tasks

### 1. Select the sample

Assemble a sample of **15 acquirers** relevant to your company:

- **10 strategic acquirers** covering all four sub-types from chapter 1 — at least 2 adjacency-buying, 2 platform-extending, 2 defensive, 2 talent-consolidating. (The 10th and any extras can be your judgement call.)
- **5 financial acquirers** covering the four sub-types — at least 2 buyout PE (one lower-middle-market, one mid- or mega-fund), 1 growth-equity fund, 1 take-private-consortium example (or note that none is realistic for your company's stage and skip), 1 family office (or note none is realistic and skip).

If you cannot find 2 credible examples of a sub-type in your space, document the reason and substitute another example rather than forcing a fit. The point is not to fill quotas; it is to work through the diversity of the taxonomy.

### 2. Build the classification table

For each acquirer, populate a row:

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Company | Acquirer name |
| Ticker / private | For strategics, ticker (public) or "private"; for financials, fund name and fund vintage |
| Category | Strategic / financial |
| Sub-type | One of the eight sub-types from chapter 1 |
| Sub-type evidence | The specific public evidence that supports the sub-type classification (with citation) |
| Expected price ceiling | Based on the sub-type profile plus this specific acquirer's capacity |
| Expected consideration mix | Cash / stock / mix / rollover / retention — with justification |
| Expected integration depth | On the profile-consistent scale for the sub-type |
| Expected retention structure | Based on the sub-type profile |
| Expected process behaviour | Speed / diligence / certainty-of-close — anchored to prior-deal evidence where possible |
| Prior deals (last 5 years) | Up to 5 named acquisitions with dates and (where available) headline price |

Each row should be defensible against interrogation — the "sub-type evidence" column is the one that most often collapses under scrutiny.

### 3. Deep-dive on three of the fifteen

Pick three acquirers from the list — one strategic and one financial that you would most seriously expect to engage with, and one that surprised you during the classification (either fit an unexpected sub-type or turned out to be a different kind of buyer than you had assumed).

For each of the three, write a one-page acquirer profile:

- **Sub-type classification.** With three specific pieces of evidence.
- **Why this acquirer would consider your company.** The strategic thesis in their language, from their point of view — 2–3 sentences.
- **Expected deal shape.** Pricing anchor, consideration mix, retention structure, integration approach.
- **Expected process behaviour.** With citations to prior deals.
- **Where they would push back on price.** The specific reasons this acquirer might discount their bid — customer concentration, technology risk, competitive dynamics, integration risk, etc.
- **Where they would over-pay.** The specific reasons this acquirer might pay above comparable multiples — synergy math, defensive urgency, category-defining thesis.

### 4. Compare against your existing buyer map

Cross-reference the 15 acquirers against your mod-101 exercise-02 buyer map:

- How many of the 15 were already on the map?
- How many new entrants did this exercise surface?
- How many entries were mis-classified in the previous map (called adjacency but actually defensive, called buyout PE but actually growth equity, etc.)?
- How many entries on your existing map are missing from the exercise sample and, on reflection, should be re-evaluated for the correct sub-type?

Update your mod-101 buyer map to reflect the corrected sub-type classifications and add the new entrants. This is a genuine update to the artefact, not a paper exercise.

### 5. Match your company's shape to the sub-type demand

Write a short (½ page) section on which sub-types your company's *shape* (ARR, growth, profitability, category, geography, team size, team quality) makes you attractive to:

- **Which strategic sub-types would find you attractive today?** Adjacency-buyers who need what you build; platform-buyers who could use you as infrastructure; defensive-buyers for whom you are a threat; talent-consolidators for whom the team is the asset.
- **Which financial sub-types would find you attractive today?** Are you Rule-of-40-consistent enough for buyout PE? Growing fast enough for growth equity? Are you at scale for a take-private consortium? Are you a fit for family-office capital?
- **What would need to change** in your company's shape over the next 12–24 months to open sub-type interest that is currently closed? (Higher profitability opens buyout-PE interest; slower growth closes growth-equity interest; larger scale opens some strategics and closes others.)

This is where the acquirer-side view of the buyer map connects back to the operating-decision view of the company — what you build affects who wants to buy you.

## Starter guidance

Three anti-patterns to watch out for:

- **Sub-type-shopping.** You want a specific acquirer to fit a specific sub-type because that sub-type is more favourable (platform-extending or transformative-strategic sound better than tuck-in or aqui-hire). Fit the acquirer to the evidence, not to the preference. The wrong sub-type classification leads to the wrong negotiation strategy.
- **Fund-vintage neglect.** For financial acquirers, skipping the fund-vintage / dry-powder analysis is the number-one mistake. A fund at year 8–9 of a 10-year vehicle is a mirage no matter how well their prior deals match your profile.
- **Public-evidence outrun.** Confidence in the classification should not outrun the public evidence supporting it. If your only source is folklore ("everyone knows Company X is a defensive buyer"), note that as folklore and mark the classification confidence lower; the sub-type-evidence column is where this discipline lives.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- The classification table has 15 entries, covering at least 6 of the 8 sub-types (all 8 if realistic for your sector — some sub-types may not apply, and that is fine to note).
- Every entry has sub-type-evidence anchored in cited public sources.
- The three deep-dive profiles are each a full page with the six sub-sections filled in.
- The buyer-map cross-reference is a real update to the mod-101 artefact.
- The your-shape-vs-demand section connects the acquirer-side taxonomy back to your company's operating profile.
- A reviewer could challenge any single sub-type classification and see the evidence supporting it.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. Which sub-type is best represented in your buyer map, and which is under-represented? What does the imbalance suggest about the market for your company?
2. For your top-choice acquirer, name the specific piece of public evidence you would use to open the *first* substantive conversation about a potential fit. (This is a lead into chapter 6's pre-marketing choreography.)
3. Which sub-type would you most benefit from cultivating a warm relationship with over the next 12 months, and what is the specific relationship-depth investment (from mod-101 chapter 3) that would move that acquirer from depth 1 to depth 2?

## Stretch goals

- **Prior-deal reverse-engineering.** Pick one of the 15 acquirers' recent deals (with disclosed pricing) and reverse-engineer the pricing math from public data — comparable multiples, growth adjustments, likely synergy premium. Use it to sharpen your expected-price-ceiling estimates.
- **Corp-dev bio dive.** For 3 of the 15 strategic acquirers, look up the head of corporate development on LinkedIn. Note their tenure, prior deals, background. A newly-hired VP-Corp-Dev is a different signal than a decade-tenured one.
- **Ex-acquirer employee outreach.** For your top-tier acquirer, try to schedule a 20-minute call with a former employee (via investor network / warm intro) to update the cultural-fit and process-behaviour rows. Note anything the public evidence would not have surfaced.
