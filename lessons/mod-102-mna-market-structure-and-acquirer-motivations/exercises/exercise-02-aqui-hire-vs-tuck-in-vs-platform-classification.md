# exercise-02: Aqui-Hire vs Tuck-In vs Platform Classification

**Estimated effort:** 2.5 hours

## Objective

Take five real, recent M&A transactions in or adjacent to your sector, classify each into the four-shape taxonomy from chapter 2 (aqui-hire / tuck-in / platform / transformative-strategic), and defend the classification with public evidence. Then apply the same classification framework to a hypothetical inbound for your company, deriving what the shape would imply for consideration mix, retention structure, integration approach, and post-close governance. By the end, you should be able to classify an inbound quickly — often before the LOI arrives — and negotiate against the right terms rather than the wrong ones.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 2 — Transaction-Type Taxonomy](../02-transaction-type-taxonomy.md)

Chapter 1 (acquirer sub-type) is supporting material — recall that the transaction shape is orthogonal to the acquirer sub-type. A single acquirer can offer any of the four shapes; the shape depends on how they see this specific target.

## Prerequisites

- Access to SEC EDGAR (8-K Item 2.01 acquisition filings, 8-K Item 1.01 material-definitive-agreement filings, DEF 14A merger-vote proxies where relevant), acquirer press releases, and trade-press coverage (TechCrunch, The Information, Bloomberg, Reuters, WSJ, and sector-specific outlets).
- Optional but useful: PitchBook, CB Insights, Mergermarket if you have subscription access.
- The company profile you carried through mod-101.

## Tasks

### 1. Select five real transactions

Pick five acquisitions from the last 18 months that were disclosed publicly enough for you to work with. Sourcing:

- **Two transactions in your direct category.** These give you category-specific pattern recognition.
- **Two transactions in an adjacent category.** These give you a sense for how similar shapes translate across sub-categories.
- **One transaction that surprised the market at announcement.** Something that was reported as an unexpected fit, an unusual price, or an unexpected shape. The surprising ones stretch your classification muscle.

You do not need transactions where every detail is public. You need enough disclosure — 8-K / proxy filings, press release with transaction rationale, subsequent commentary from analysts, and (ideally) post-close reporting on integration outcomes — to defend a classification.

### 2. Classify each of the five

For each transaction, produce a one-page classification note:

- **Announcement summary.** Buyer, target, headline price, consideration mix, announcement date, close date, cited transaction rationale.
- **Shape classification.** Aqui-hire / tuck-in / platform / transformative-strategic. If the shape blends two of the four (a common real-world situation), name the blend and which shape dominates.
- **Evidence for the classification.** At least three specific pieces of public evidence — quotes from the buyer's investor call, integration announcements, post-close org-chart signals, product roadmap statements, subsequent brand-preservation or brand-retirement decisions.
- **What the buyer was fundamentally buying.** In their language, one sentence: team, product line, platform capability, category-defining bet.
- **Consideration-mix logic.** Why the cash / stock / rollover / earn-out mix looked the way it did, tied to the shape.
- **Retention structure (if disclosed).** Vesting terms, retention pool, earn-out design, key-person protections.
- **Integration approach (if disclosed or observable).** Brand preservation, org-chart moves, product folded / preserved / shelved.
- **What a wrong-shape negotiation would have cost.** If the buyer had (mis-)classified this as a different shape, what would the negotiation have gotten wrong?

### 3. Cross-shape comparison

Produce a comparison table across the five, one row per transaction:

| Transaction | Shape | Buyer sub-type (chapter 1) | Headline price | Consideration mix | Retention structure | Integration timing | Founder's post-close role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Analyse:

- Does the same acquirer sub-type do multiple shapes, or do you see a pattern (e.g., "this specific platform-extending strategic always does platform deals, never tuck-ins")?
- Do the consideration mixes cluster by shape as chapter 2's reference table would predict, or do you see outliers?
- What patterns of retention structure repeat by shape?

The point is not to find a rule; it is to build pattern-recognition for the range of shapes you might see.

### 4. Apply to a hypothetical inbound for your company

Imagine an inbound arrives from your top-tier strategic acquirer (from your buyer map). Assume for the exercise:

- The inbound is a genuine, senior-level expression of interest, not a fishing conversation.
- No specific price has been named yet, but the interest is serious enough that a formal offer would follow within 60–90 days if the seller engaged.

Write a **classification memo** (2–3 pages) that answers:

**(a) Which shape is this inbound most likely to be?** Draw on chapter 2's diagnostic signals — what the acquirer is asking about, who is in the room on their side, how they talk about integration, and the plausible price-to-market-cap ratio for a deal at your company's likely valuation. Name the most-likely shape and the second-most-likely; note what evidence you would collect early to distinguish.

**(b) What does each of the two most-likely shapes imply?** For each shape, name:
- Expected consideration mix at a plausible price band (three-point range: conservative, midpoint, optimistic).
- Expected retention structure — upfront vs. retention split, vesting, key-person coverage.
- Expected integration timeline for the product and the team.
- Expected post-close role for the founder-CEO and the executive team.
- Expected common-holder waterfall implications given your preference stack (draw on mod-101 exercise-04's waterfall model).

**(c) What is your negotiation-priority list under each shape?** For each shape, name the top three negotiating priorities you would push hardest on:
- Under an aqui-hire shape: retention terms, MIP / carve-out design, key-person definitions.
- Under a tuck-in shape: integration timeline, product-preservation window, earn-out design.
- Under a platform shape: stock consideration terms, executive-team-integration structure, regulatory-timing planning.
- Under a transformative shape: board seats and reserved decision rights, retention-plan design, communications choreography.

**(d) What early diagnostic conversation would let you refine the classification?** Draft a 3–5 sentence script for the "we want to understand how you're thinking about this" conversation with the acquirer's senior counterpart. The script should surface classification signal without conceding negotiating ground.

### 5. Recognise the mis-classification failure modes in prior deals

Look at the five transactions from tasks 1–3. Identify (from public reporting, from analyst commentary, or from your inference) at least one deal where a party appears to have mis-classified the shape — a seller who negotiated a tuck-in as a platform, an acquirer who bought as an aqui-hire but treated it as a tuck-in, or similar. Write a short (½ page) note on:

- What appears to have been mis-classified.
- What the observable cost was (headline price, subsequent departures, product-shelving unhappiness, litigation).
- What the correct classification would have suggested doing differently.

You do not need certainty — inference from public reporting is acceptable. The exercise is in the analytical muscle of naming what a mis-classification looks like from the outside.

## Starter guidance

Two anti-patterns to watch out for:

- **Pattern-matching on the acquirer sub-type instead of the deal shape.** A platform-extending strategic can do any of the four shapes. Do not classify a deal as a platform deal because the acquirer is a platform-extending strategic; classify based on the specific shape's evidence.
- **Ignoring blended shapes.** Many real deals are blends — a tuck-in with strong aqui-hire retention structure, a platform with transformative-strategic communications choreography. Naming the blend and the dominant shape is more accurate than forcing a pure-shape label.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- Five real transactions classified, each with a one-page note that includes shape, evidence, and consideration / retention / integration analysis.
- A comparison table across the five that surfaces the cross-shape patterns.
- A hypothetical-inbound memo (2–3 pages) covering the four sub-questions (a)–(d).
- At least one identified mis-classification example from the five with the observable cost noted.
- A reviewer could challenge any single classification and see the specific evidence used.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. Which of the five real transactions would you most want your company to be *modelled* on, and why?
2. Which of the four shapes is most likely for your company's next credible inbound — and if the modal shape is one you would rather not be in (e.g., aqui-hire when you want tuck-in), what does that suggest about the operating work to do over the next 12–18 months to shift the modal shape?
3. If your company received the hypothetical inbound today and the negotiation went as your memo predicts, what would you most regret about your current operating position?

## Stretch goals

- **Post-close outcome tracking.** For each of the five transactions, note (from public reporting) any observable post-close outcomes — founder departures, product shelving or retirement, brand changes, financial-performance disclosures. Compare against your shape classification: did the shape predict the post-close pattern?
- **Analyst-report review.** For the two of your five with the most public analyst coverage, read the deal-rationale analyst reports (typically sell-side research from the buyer's covering analysts, published within a week of announcement). Note where analyst framing agrees and disagrees with your classification.
- **Blended-shape catalogue.** Look for real transactions that were unusually clear blends (e.g., a tuck-in that included a very-large retention pool tilting it toward aqui-hire economics). Build a small catalogue of blend patterns — these are the deals your own classification will most often need to name.
