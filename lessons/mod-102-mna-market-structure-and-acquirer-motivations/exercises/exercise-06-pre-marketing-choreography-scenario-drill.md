# exercise-06: Pre-Marketing Choreography Scenario Drill

**Estimated effort:** 3.5 hours

## Objective

Work through five branching scenarios that the pre-marketing period actually generates — an unsolicited cold inbound, a competitor being acquired, a fundraise mid-flight when a serious M&A signal arrives, a prolonged pre-marketing that is drifting toward the "shopped" perception, and a genuine founder-CEO ambivalence — and design the specific choreography for each. By the end you should be able to respond to any of the five within 24–48 hours of the triggering event with a defensible playbook, and you should have the standing confidentiality and communications discipline in place before any of them fires.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 6 — Pre-Marketing Choreography](../06-pre-marketing-choreography.md)

Chapter 3 (buyer map) provides the acquirer set that every scenario references. Chapter 4 (banker engagement) determines whether the banker owns execution or whether you own it directly. Chapter 5 (pre-marketing document set) provides the artefacts that flow to acquirers once the choreography escalates to a formal-launch decision. mod-101 chapter 3 (rolling buyer map) provides the stage-0 standing discipline that all scenarios build on.

## Prerequisites

- The company profile and buyer map you have carried through the module.
- Completed exercise-03 (top-tier buyer map with tiers and pre-marketing plans).
- Completed exercise-04 (banker engagement decision — whether you have a banker, and if so which firm).
- Completed exercise-05 (teaser / CIM / management-presentation in draft form) — you may need to reference these in scenarios that require an accelerated formal launch.
- A shared writing environment for the playbooks (Google Docs, Notion, etc.).
- Optional: 1–2 board members or investors available for a scenario-planning conversation. Real board input on any of the scenarios (especially C and E) sharpens the choreography materially.

## Tasks

### 1. Install the standing confidentiality and communications discipline

Before working the scenarios, name the standing disciplines that every scenario depends on. If they are not in place, some of the scenarios' choreographies will not work.

- **Deal-team clean list.** Name the specific individuals inside the company who know the pre-marketing is happening (founder-CEO, CFO, GC, board members, and 2–3 named executives on a need-to-know basis). For each, note what specifically they know and what they do not.
- **Board discipline.** Name the board-visibility cadence (weekly during pre-marketing, per exercise-03's task 8) and the executive-session protocol for M&A discussions.
- **No-comment reflex.** Name who is authorised to respond to press or industry inquiries about M&A speculation and what the standing script is. The canonical script is "we don't comment on M&A speculation" — write it down, brief the CEO / CFO / CMO / IR contacts, and rehearse.
- **NDA template.** Confirm the NDA template you will use for any acquirer who receives the CIM. Standard-form, typically 2–3 years, includes a non-solicitation of employees clause, and (for public-adjacent contexts) a standstill clause. External counsel should have this in the drawer.
- **Employee-communications posture.** Name the internal-communications posture. Employees who are not on the deal-team clean list should not be aware of pre-marketing activity. What is the story if a discovery meeting shows up on someone's calendar or an unusually senior visitor arrives in the office?

Write these disciplines on one page. This is the fact-set every scenario references.

### 2. Scenario A — an unsolicited inbound arrives cold

**Setup.** An acquirer you had scored as relationship-depth 1 (aware, no active relationship) sends an unsolicited email through your general contact form (or through a mutual investor connection). The email is signed by their VP of Corporate Development and reads: *"We've been watching your company for the last 18 months and have developed a strong strategic thesis around what a combination could look like. We'd like to explore this at senior-executive level."* No specific price or transaction structure is proposed.

**Design the choreography.** Produce a 1–2 page playbook covering:

- **Immediate response (first 24 hours).** Who inside the company sees the email? What is the immediate acknowledgement to the sender? Do not commit to a meeting yet; acknowledge receipt and buy time.
- **Internal alignment (first 72 hours).** Who is convened — CFO, board chair, GC, banker (if engaged)? What is the specific alignment decision — do we engage, decline, or defer? How does the seriousness of the inbound get assessed (chapter 2's shape-classification signals are the starting point)?
- **Buyer-map re-scoring.** How does this inbound update this acquirer's scores on your map? Does it move them from second-tier to top-tier, or does the specific shape of the inbound tell you they are not actually who you thought they were?
- **First substantive meeting design.** If you engage, what is the frame of the first meeting (discovery meeting per chapter 6, or something more specific)? Who attends on your side? What information do you share, and what do you hold back? What information do you seek from them?
- **Broadening the process.** If the inbound is serious, do you keep it single-bidder or accelerate pre-marketing with your top-tier set to bring competitive tension? A single-bidder situation is systematically worse than a competitive one; unless the price and terms are compelling on their own, the right answer is often "yes, we'll continue exploring, and we'll also accelerate broader engagement."
- **The specific script for the CEO reply email.** Write the actual reply — 3–5 sentences, warm but non-committal, proposing a specific next step (a call within 2 weeks, a specific date).
- **The failure mode to watch out for.** For example: "the inbound is a fishing expedition from a corp-dev associate who is not empowered to lead a real process. Signal: they will not commit to a specific senior-executive meeting time; they will keep pushing for information without reciprocal specificity." Name the failure mode and what you do if you spot it.

### 3. Scenario B — a close competitor is acquired

**Setup.** A close competitor in your category is acquired by a strategic acquirer. The announcement (an 8-K, a press release, and immediate trade-press coverage) discloses a headline price at a specific revenue multiple that is 40% above where your board's most recent internal valuation was anchored. Multiple analysts publish deal-rationale notes within a week naming three other likely category-consolidation targets. You are named as one of the three.

**Design the choreography.** Produce a 1–2 page playbook covering:

- **Buyer-map rearrangement.** The acquirer of the competitor is now (probably) off the buyer map for a while — they have made their category bet. But every *other* plausible acquirer in the category is now more actively thinking about the category as an M&A target. Which specific acquirers on your top and second tier just moved to a higher urgency? Which moved to lower (because they had considered a different target and now feel late)?
- **Pricing-anchor recalibration.** The announced multiple changes your ability-to-pay analysis and the pricing-conversation anchor with every remaining acquirer. Update your target-price band accordingly. Note where the analyst reports would create anchor drift for buyers.
- **Timing decision.** The window during which acquirers are actively thinking about the category is finite (typically 3–9 months post-announcement). Do you accelerate pre-marketing to catch the window, or does that create a signal of desperation ("they're reacting to the competitor's exit")? What is your decision criteria?
- **Board conversation.** Draft a short (1-page) memo to your board covering: the deal announcement, the implications for your buyer map, the timing-window question, and a specific recommendation (accelerate / hold / observe).
- **Inbound preparation.** After a comparable transaction, inbounds spike. What is your standing response? Who owns the triage? How do you distinguish serious inbounds from fishing expeditions?
- **Selective outreach.** For 2–3 specific top-tier acquirers where the competitor's exit has raised their urgency, is there a specific pre-marketing touch that would be appropriate now? (A "we noticed the deal, wanted to compare notes on where the category is heading" market-intelligence conversation, per chapter 6's foundational pattern.) Draft the specific outreach for one of them.
- **The failure mode to watch out for.** For example: "we accelerate pre-marketing too visibly and the market interprets our activity as reactive rather than strategic, capping our price at the competitor's multiple rather than above it."

### 4. Scenario C — fundraise is mid-flight when a serious M&A signal arrives

**Setup.** You are in the second week of a Series D fundraising process, with a lead investor identified and a term sheet expected within 3 weeks. Simultaneously, an inbound arrives from a top-tier strategic acquirer with a specific, senior expression of interest ("we'd like to move quickly to explore an acquisition"). The strategic's implied valuation range (based on their questions and the sub-type profile from exercise-01) is comparable to or above the expected fundraise pre-money.

**Design the choreography.** Produce a 2-page playbook covering:

- **The dual-track tension.** Both paths are live. The fundraise investors will re-price down or walk if they learn of the M&A conversation; the M&A acquirer will slow their process if they learn of the fundraise. Both discoveries are likely. Design the choreography that manages the tension.
- **The board-and-lead-investor conversation.** This is a board-level decision. Draft the memo to the board (and, separately, the conversation script for the lead investor of the round). Both need to happen within days, not weeks. What do you tell each; what do you hold back?
- **The three decision paths.** Explicitly walk through the three options from chapter 6's Rule 1:
  - **Shift to sale.** Pause fundraise; notify investors that the process is on hold. What do you tell them? Which investors will respect the shift and which will feel misled? What is the fallback if the sale does not close?
  - **Continue the fundraise.** Park the M&A inbound with a soft "we appreciate the interest and would like to reconnect after we complete our current financing." What is the specific script? What is the risk that the acquirer interprets this as declining and moves on?
  - **Dual-track.** Explicit board approval, defined narrative to both channels, hard deadline for a decision point. What is the specific defined narrative to each channel? What is the hard deadline?
- **The decision criteria.** Name the 3–5 specific criteria that would push you to each path. Price relative to fundraise pre-money is one; strategic conviction of the acquirer is another; personal readiness of the founder-CEO is a third; alignment of the fundraise investors with a sale outcome (would they support a sale to a strategic that would give them a partial DPI vs. push for continued growth) is a fourth.
- **The specific 30-day plan.** Whichever path you choose, what does the next 30 days look like day-by-day for the founder-CEO and CFO? Which meetings happen, which are cancelled, what is the sequencing?
- **The failure mode to watch out for.** For example: "we attempt dual-track without board approval; the fundraise lead investor learns of the M&A conversation from a mutual contact and pulls the term sheet. Now we have neither channel and a damaged relationship with the lead investor."

### 5. Scenario D — pre-marketing has been running for 8+ months, no formal launch

**Setup.** Your pre-marketing programme has been running actively for 8 months. Discovery meetings have happened with 6 of the top-tier acquirers. The banker (if engaged) has warmed corp-dev at another 5. Two industry conferences have brought CEOs into your booth for "informal conversations." Yet the formal process has not launched — internal readiness has slipped (audit not fresh, projections not stable, one senior executive vacancy), a market-window question has emerged, and a specific major-customer situation has consumed founder-CEO bandwidth. You are now in the "shopping perception zone" per chapter 6's Rule 4.

**Design the choreography.** Produce a 1–2 page playbook covering:

- **Root-cause diagnosis.** What is the actual cause of the delay — internal readiness, board misalignment, market-window uncertainty, founder-personal-readiness gap? Be specific and honest.
- **The decision framework.** Chapter 6 names two options: launch the formal process within a defined near-term window (30–60 days), or fully pause and return to stage-0 standing. Neither is comfortable; both are better than continuing to drift. Name the criteria that would push you to each.
- **If launching: acceleration plan.** What are the specific 30–60 day workstreams to get the formal process out — audit, financials, data-room, banker (if not yet engaged) — and who owns each? Where does the pre-marketing-primed acquirer set fit in the first-round outreach? How do you manage the price-anchor damage from the shopping perception?
- **If pausing: reset plan.** How do you re-close the "shopped" perception? A specific "we've decided to focus on building the business" narrative to investors and (through the banker) to the acquirer set is the standard move. Draft the specific narrative. What signals reinforce the pause credibility — an executive hire, a specific strategic-partnership announcement, a strong operating quarter? How long does the reset take (6–12 months typically) before pre-marketing can credibly restart?
- **Communication with the board.** The board has been watching. Draft the board update that names the drift, the diagnosis, and the recommended path.
- **The failure mode to watch out for.** For example: "we choose neither — we neither launch nor pause. The shopping perception continues to consolidate; the pre-marketing conversations stay warm-but-not-productive; when the formal process finally launches 6 months later, the top-tier acquirers arrive at low anchor and know the process is stale."

### 6. Scenario E — the founder-CEO is genuinely uncertain whether to sell

**Setup.** The rolling buyer map is warm, an inbound is credible, and the board would support a sale exploration. But the founder-CEO is genuinely torn. Their spouse has a specific view (either strongly for or strongly against a sale); the founder has a specific personal-narrative discomfort with what "selling" would signal; and the founder's ambition for what the company could become independent is real and unresolved.

This is not a scenario about market conditions. It is a scenario about the founder.

**Design the choreography.** Produce a 1–2 page playbook covering:

- **The rule from chapter 6.** Do not run pre-marketing while genuinely uncertain. The founder's ambivalence bleeds into every conversation and undermines both paths (continuing independent and selling). Name this rule and enforce it.
- **The resolution machinery.** Use the endgame-optionality memo discipline from mod-101 chapter 6 to force the decision to a defensible position. What is the specific 4–6 week process to resolve the ambivalence — a memo, a specific set of conversations (spouse, board chair, an executive coach, a peer-founder who has been through the decision), a specific decision date?
- **The board conversation.** How does the founder-CEO signal to the board that they are working through the decision, without either (a) triggering board-side alarm about founder engagement or (b) committing to a specific outcome prematurely? Draft the specific message.
- **The interim posture.** During the 4–6 week resolution period, what is the pre-marketing posture — full stage-0 standing (no active pre-marketing signalling), the specific reasons any active pre-marketing conversations need to pause, and the specific handling of any inbounds that arrive during the period.
- **The post-resolution paths.** If the resolution is "sell," what is the specific pre-marketing plan for the next 12–24 weeks? If the resolution is "continue independent," what is the specific narrative to the board and to the buyer-map counterparts who were expecting some form of engagement? How do you preserve the option to reopen the conversation in 12–24 months?
- **The failure mode to watch out for.** For example: "the founder-CEO half-engages with pre-marketing while genuinely uncertain; the top-tier acquirers detect ambivalence in the discovery meetings and either lose interest or interpret the ambivalence as leverage; when the founder later resolves toward sale, the acquirers are cooler and the pricing anchor has shifted down."

### 7. Cross-scenario patterns

After drafting all five playbooks, produce a 1-page cross-scenario summary that names:

- **What is common across all five.** Board involvement, banker coordination, confidentiality discipline, buyer-map re-scoring after triggering events. These are the standing muscles that make each specific scenario response possible.
- **The most common failure mode across the five.** In all likelihood, it is some variant of "acting too fast in a specific tactical way (accepting a meeting, accelerating outreach, launching a formal process) before internal alignment (board, CFO, GC, banker if engaged, founder-CEO personal resolution) is complete." Name it specifically for your company and note the specific discipline that guards against it.
- **The 24-hour decision-latency target.** Every scenario should be actionable within 24–48 hours of the trigger. If any of your playbooks assumes longer decision latency, name the specific reason and the specific process improvement that would shorten it.
- **What you now know you need that you do not yet have.** From working the five scenarios, what specific artefacts, relationships, or process disciplines are missing that would materially improve your response to any of the five? Name them and note the owner and target date for installing each.

### 8. Rehearsal (optional but valuable)

For one of the five scenarios, run a 30-minute rehearsal with a board member, an investor, or an experienced peer founder. Present the trigger, the choreography, and the specific first-move; solicit their pushback. Note what you would change based on the rehearsal.

## Starter guidance

Four anti-patterns to watch out for:

- **Playbook perfectionism.** A defensible playbook that gets used beats an exhaustive playbook that stays in the drawer. Bias toward specificity ("the CEO responds within 24 hours with this specific script") over comprehensiveness ("here are 12 possible responses depending on the sub-scenario").
- **Board avoidance.** Every scenario materially involves the board. Playbooks that try to avoid the board conversation until the founder-CEO has "figured it out" fail — the board's input is part of the choreography, not a barrier to it.
- **Banker over-reliance.** If you have engaged a banker, do not delegate the entire scenario response to them. The banker owns execution machinery; the founder-CEO owns the founder-personal-readiness and CEO-to-CEO conversations that most of these scenarios turn on. Name the split clearly.
- **Under-writing the failure mode.** Every scenario has a specific way it can go wrong. Playbooks that skip the failure-mode analysis are drafts that will surprise you in execution. Force yourself to write the specific failure mode for each.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- The standing disciplines from task 1 are written down as a one-page fact-set.
- Playbooks for all five scenarios (A–E) are drafted, each 1–2 pages, covering the specific sub-questions listed for each scenario.
- Each playbook names the immediate response, the internal-alignment process, the specific script or memo for the key communication, and the failure mode to watch out for.
- The cross-scenario summary (task 7) names common patterns, common failure modes, decision-latency targets, and gaps to install.
- If task 8 is completed: a rehearsal note with specific playbook revisions.
- A reviewer could hand any of the five scenarios' triggers to your company and see a defensible response ready within 24–48 hours.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. Which of the five scenarios did you find hardest to design the choreography for, and why? The hardest one is usually the one whose real-world instance you are least prepared for.
2. In scenario C (fundraise-vs-sale), which of the three decision paths (shift to sale, continue fundraise, dual-track) is the modal choice for your specific situation, and what would need to be true for a different path to be right?
3. If scenario E (founder ambivalence) applied to you today, honestly, what would the answer be after working through the resolution process? Do you have the endgame-optionality memo from mod-101 chapter 6 to lean on, or would you have to build it in real time?
4. Which of the standing disciplines from task 1 is currently weakest at your company? What is the specific 30-day project to close the gap?
5. What is the one specific relationship-depth investment (a specific board member, a specific investor, a specific external advisor) that would most improve your response quality across all five scenarios?

## Stretch goals

- **Sixth scenario authoring.** Design and write the playbook for a scenario the module did not cover but that fits your specific situation — a specific commercial-relationship counterparty makes an M&A approach, a specific piece of internal news (litigation, executive departure, security incident) constrains pre-marketing options, a specific market-window signal (macro rate move, sector-multiple compression) shifts the fundamental attractiveness of the exit path.
- **Six-month post-mortem drill.** For scenario B (competitor acquired), design a specific 6-month post-mortem you would run after the choreography executes — what did the buyer-map say vs. what actually happened, what were the pricing anchors vs. reality, what would you have done differently. Even hypothetically, the discipline of designing the post-mortem before the event improves the choreography during the event.
- **Playbook tabletop.** Convene the deal-team clean list (or a rehearsal version of it) for a 90-minute tabletop exercise. Present two of the five scenarios as triggers; work through the response in real time. Note where the playbook needed clarification, where the deal team's alignment gaps surfaced, and where the discipline held.
- **Confidentiality-break rehearsal.** Design a specific scenario where confidentiality is at risk (a journalist calls, an employee suspects and asks the CEO directly, a competitor's corp-dev calls to congratulate you on "the deal"). Draft the specific response script for each and rehearse it. Confidentiality discipline is one of the standing muscles that most reliably fails under real pressure; the rehearsal is worth the time.
