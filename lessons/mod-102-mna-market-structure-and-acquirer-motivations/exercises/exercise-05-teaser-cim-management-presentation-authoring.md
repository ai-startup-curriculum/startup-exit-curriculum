# exercise-05: Teaser, CIM, and Management-Presentation Authoring

**Estimated effort:** 6 hours

## Objective

Author the three pre-marketing artefacts — the teaser, the CIM outline with the executive summary and two core sections fully drafted, and the management presentation deck — for your company, holding a single invariant narrative arc across all three. Then run the consistency check against the data-room material being built under mod-105 (or its placeholder) and produce the narrative-consistency matrix that a well-run sell-side maintains. By the end you should have a defensible first cut of the three-artefact set with the specific discipline that separates authored narrative from templated fill-in.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 5 — The Pre-Marketing Document Set](../05-pre-marketing-document-set.md)

Chapter 3 (buyer map) provides the top-tier acquirer set that shapes the acquirer-specific appendix personalisation. Chapter 6 (pre-marketing choreography) provides the context for how these documents get into the market. Chapter 1 (acquirer sub-type) informs the narrative-emphasis choices — a strategic-heavy top tier needs synergy-forward language; a financial-heavy top tier needs operating-lever-forward language.

## Prerequisites

- The company profile you have carried through the module (ARR, growth, unit economics, product, team, customer profile).
- Your completed buyer map from exercise-03 with the top-tier and second-tier acquirers named and sub-typed.
- The most recent three-statement historical financials and projections (from the finance function, or the version you have built for the exercise).
- Access to at least one real published CIM for reference. Public examples are hard to come by (CIMs are confidential by definition), but redacted CIMs occasionally surface in litigation (SEC EDGAR proxy filings sometimes reference the CIM used in a specific deal); the summary excerpts in academic case studies (Harvard Business School, Stanford GSB, Columbia Business School M&A cases) are useful proxies.
- Optional: a professional-services drafting environment (Google Docs, Notion, Microsoft Word — whatever your team uses). A slide tool (Google Slides, Keynote, PowerPoint, Pitch) for the management presentation.

## Tasks

### 1. Author the narrative arc

Before drafting any of the three documents, write down the narrative arc — the invariant 3–5 sentence story that every artefact will express at a different resolution. Use chapter 5's structure:

1. The market you are in and why it matters (1–2 sentences).
2. The problem you solve and why it is hard (1–2 sentences).
3. How you solve it and why you are uniquely positioned (2–3 sentences).
4. Why now (1–2 sentences).
5. What owning this company enables that owning nothing does not (1–2 sentences).

Draft two versions:

- **Version A: strategic-acquirer-emphasis.** The fifth sentence leans on synergy language — what a strategic acquirer can do with you.
- **Version B: financial-acquirer-emphasis.** The fifth sentence leans on operating-lever language — durable ARR, retention economics, add-on opportunity, margin expansion.

Both share sentences 1–4 verbatim. This dual arc is what lets you personalise emphasis without divergence (chapter 5's rule).

Have the founder-CEO and CFO (or your role-play stand-in) sign off on the arc in writing. The arc is the invariant; every subsequent choice references it.

### 2. Draft the teaser

Draft the 1–2 page teaser to the specification in chapter 5's teaser section:

- **Header line.** Anonymous project codename, size dimension, sector one-liner.
- **Business description.** 2–4 sentences at a level of specificity that a strategic in your category will recognise but that does not identify you to a casual reader.
- **Key metrics.** Revenue (range or exact), growth rate, gross margin band, EBITDA / operating profit band (if favourable), headcount, customer count (if favourable).
- **Investment highlights.** 4–6 bullets compressing the arc's third and fifth pillars.
- **Transaction context.** One line on the process.
- **Contact.** Placeholder for the banker's contact information (or, in a no-banker process, external counsel).

**Anonymity test:** show the drafted teaser to a colleague who does not know the deal is being framed. Ask them to guess the company. If they identify it correctly on first read, revise until the anonymity holds against a well-informed but non-insider reader. If it holds even against an insider, you have probably under-specified — err on the side of specificity-with-plausible-deniability rather than genericism.

Write down (½ page) the anonymity trade-offs you made — what you left out, what you kept in, and why.

### 3. Author the CIM outline in full, and draft the executive summary and two core sections in depth

**3.1 — Full outline.** Draft a section-by-section outline of the full CIM, targeting 40–55 pages of core content plus appendices. Use chapter 5's modal structure:

1. Executive summary (2–3 pages).
2. Investment highlights (2–3 pages).
3. Company overview (3–5 pages).
4. Market opportunity (3–5 pages).
5. Product and technology (4–6 pages).
6. Go-to-market (3–5 pages).
7. Customers (3–5 pages).
8. Team (2–3 pages).
9. Financial overview (5–8 pages plus appendices).
10. Growth strategy (3–5 pages).
11. Transaction context (1–2 pages).
12. Appendices (variable).

For each section, list 4–8 sub-headings that will structure that section's content. This is the CIM skeleton.

**3.2 — Executive summary (2–3 pages, full draft).** Draft the full executive summary. Every bullet or paragraph should be traceable back to the narrative arc (task 1) and to a later section of the CIM that supports it. Nothing in the executive summary should appear that is not backed by evidence elsewhere.

**3.3 — Two core sections (full drafts).** Pick two of the following four and draft each to full CIM depth (target: 3–5 pages each):

- Market opportunity (with TAM / SAM / SOM methodology footnoted and defended).
- Customers (with concentration analysis, retention metrics, marquee references).
- Financial overview (with historical financials, key-metrics dashboard, projections and underlying assumptions, and an honest discussion of cohort economics).
- Growth strategy (with product-expansion, market-expansion, M&A opportunities where relevant, and honest key-risks-and-mitigations).

The picks should reflect where your specific business has the most nuance to convey — pick the sections where the buyer's price conviction is most sensitive to how you frame the material.

**3.4 — Section stubs.** For every remaining section (from the outline in 3.1 that you did not fully draft), write a 1-paragraph stub that names the key content and the specific evidence base. This is what the banker's associates would fill in during the full drafting cycle.

### 4. Draft the management presentation deck

Produce a 40–80 slide deck (target: 50–60 slides) using the modal structure in chapter 5's management-presentation section. For each of the seven major sections (Introductions, Business overview, Product & technology, Go-to-market & customers, Financial overview, Growth strategy, Q&A), draft the slides at the following depth:

**Fully-drafted slides:**
- Cover slide.
- Agenda slide.
- Section-divider slides for each of the seven major sections.
- The 6–10 core slides in the Business overview and Growth strategy sections — these are where the founder-CEO's narrative lives, and they carry the arc.
- The 6–10 core slides in the Financial overview section — these are where the CFO's discipline shows, and they carry the numbers that the acquirer's investment committee will price against.

**Placeholder / structure slides:**
- The remaining slides in Product & technology, Go-to-market & customers can be placeholder slides that name the topic and the intended message but not the full detail. Note the source data (product-marketing deck, sales-ops dashboard, cohort model) where the full detail would come from.

Every slide should be traceable to the CIM section it corresponds to. The management presentation is the CIM's narrative delivered in-person; the two artefacts should not diverge.

**Speaker-notes discipline:** for each fully-drafted slide, write a 2–4 sentence speaker note capturing the specific point the presenting executive is trying to make. Slides without speaker notes are underwritten.

**Q&A preparation.** Draft the top 20 hardest questions you expect an acquirer to ask, with a 2–3 sentence answer script for each. Group by section (business overview, product, GTM, financials, growth, risks). The Q&A prep is often the highest-signal document in the pre-marketing set — it is where the founder-CEO / CFO's private honesty lives before it hits the acquirer's ears.

**Note on definitions.** Inconsistencies most often come from *definition drift* rather than value drift. ARR calculated one way in the teaser and another way in the financial overview will produce two different numbers even when the underlying business is the same. Define each metric once (in the CIM's financial-overview section, ideally) and reference the definition in the other artefacts.

### 6. Author acquirer-specific personalisation (top-tier only)

For each of your top 3–5 top-tier acquirers from exercise-03, draft:

- **A one-page CIM appendix** that speaks to their specific strategic thesis — a category-thesis paragraph in their language, a synergy-opportunity paragraph, and a specific-product-integration-or-platform-fit paragraph. This appendix is added to the CIM copy that goes to this specific acquirer; the core CIM is unchanged.
- **A 2–3 slide management-presentation addendum** that leans into the same acquirer-specific angle in the Growth strategy or Business overview section. Again, the core deck is unchanged; the addendum is inserted for this acquirer's specific presentation.

The rule: **one narrative arc, one CIM core, one management-presentation core, with acquirer-specific appendices and acquirer-specific talking points**. Personalise the emphasis, not the story.

Write a 1-paragraph note per top-tier acquirer explaining the specific angle you took and why.

### 7. Failure-mode audit

Walk through the six failure modes from chapter 5's failure-modes section and self-diagnose against your drafts:

- **Marketing-department CIM.** Read the CIM sections you drafted aloud. Does the voice sound like evidence-and-argument, or does it sound like a customer-facing brochure? Flag any paragraphs that lean toward brochure voice.
- **Aspirational-metric bloat.** Cross-check your projections against your historicals. If your projected next-year growth is 2x+ your trailing-twelve-month growth without a specific catalyst named and defensible, revise.
- **Consistency drift.** From the matrix in task 5, flag every inconsistency and resolve it in writing.
- **Risk-omission surprise.** List the top 5 risks a sophisticated buyer would surface in Q-of-E or commercial diligence. Confirm each appears in the CIM's growth-strategy or risk-factors section. Flag any that do not.
- **Over-personalised documents.** Cross-read the top-tier acquirer appendices — do the *core* CIM sections (executive summary, market opportunity, product & technology) differ across acquirers? They should not.
- **Banker-generic CIM.** Have a colleague who is not on the deal team read the executive summary and the two core sections. Does the voice sound like it was authored by the founder-CEO / CFO, or does it sound like a generic sector template? Flag paragraphs that read as banker-generic.

For each flagged issue, write down what you would revise and (if the time in this exercise permits) actually revise it.

## Starter guidance

Five anti-patterns to watch out for:

- **Drafting the teaser last.** The teaser feels like the easiest artefact and gets treated as a summary of the CIM. In practice the teaser is often what the acquirer's decision-maker reads first (sometimes only), and it is what convinces them to sign the NDA. Draft it early and with the same care as the CIM — sometimes a strong teaser is the difference between an acquirer engaging and passing.
- **Loading the CIM with projections.** The CIM's projections are the negotiating anchor for the acquirer's price conviction. Aggressive projections raise the anchor but also raise the diligence bar — the buyer's Q-of-E will surface the reality gap and cost you credibility. Bias toward defensible-and-slightly-conservative on the projections; the process narrative and the growth-strategy discussion can carry the ambition.
- **Skipping risk disclosure.** Sophisticated buyers assume any CIM that omits obvious risks is hiding them. Naming the risk (with a 1-paragraph mitigation) is stronger than omitting it and hoping the buyer will not notice.
- **Slide-heavy management presentation.** 40–80 slides feels like a lot; the temptation is to fill every slide with dense content. In practice, the founder-CEO and CFO's conviction, answers, and rapport are what actually convince the acquirer — the slides are scaffolding. Slides with 3 bullets and a chart beat slides with 12 bullets and 4 charts, every time.
- **Consistency-matrix skip.** The matrix in task 5 is unglamorous but is the single highest-leverage discipline for a professional-quality sell-side. Do not skip it.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- The narrative arc is written down in two versions (strategic-emphasis, financial-emphasis) with sentences 1–4 shared verbatim.
- The teaser is drafted in full, passes an anonymity test with a well-informed reader, and includes an anonymity-trade-off note.
- The CIM has a full section-by-section outline; the executive summary is fully drafted; two core sections are fully drafted at 3–5 pages each; remaining sections have 1-paragraph stubs.
- The management presentation is 40–80 slides with the core sections fully drafted, section-dividers in place, and 2–4 sentence speaker notes on each fully-drafted slide.
- The top-20 Q&A preparation is drafted with answer scripts.
- The narrative-consistency matrix is populated for at least the metrics listed above, with inconsistencies flagged and resolutions noted.
- Top 3–5 acquirer-specific appendices (CIM one-page) and management-presentation addendums (2–3 slides) are drafted with a rationale note.
- The failure-mode audit walks through all six failure modes with specific flagged issues and resolutions.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection:

1. Which section of the CIM was hardest to draft, and why? The difficulty is diagnostic — it usually reflects a genuine tension in the business that the buyer's diligence will surface. Name the tension and what you would do about it operationally.
2. Which of the top-tier acquirers' specific personalisation was easiest to write, and which was hardest? What does the easy vs. hard split tell you about which acquirers you have the strongest strategic-fit case for?
3. If you had to shorten the CIM by 30%, which sections would you cut and which would you keep at full length? Why?
4. Which of the top-20 Q&A questions do you least want to be asked, and what does that suggest about what specific operating work would move the answer to a stronger position?
5. Which metric in the consistency matrix was most vulnerable to definition drift across the artefacts? What discipline would you install (a definitions appendix, a metrics-authority person, a shared glossary) to prevent recurrence?

## Stretch goals

- **Peer-founder CIM review.** Circulate the drafted executive summary and one core section to 1–2 peer founders / CFOs who have run a sell-side. Ask specifically: (a) does the narrative arc hold, (b) is there anything in here you would not put in a CIM, (c) is there anything obvious that is missing? Revise based on the feedback.
- **Investment-committee simulation.** Role-play the acquirer's investment committee. Read the CIM as if you were the committee — what specific questions would you ask? Do the CIM sections and the Q&A prep answer them? Where they do not, revise.
- **Data-room reconciliation dry run.** For the two fully-drafted CIM sections, walk through the specific data-room folders that would support each claim. Confirm the data-room content exists (in draft form is fine) and is consistent with the CIM. Note any gaps.
- **Alternative narrative arc.** Draft a *third* arc — different from the two you drafted in task 1 — that emphasises a different strategic dimension (e.g., defensive-strategic angle for a specific acquirer who might buy defensively). Note when this arc would be right and when it would not. Do not use this arc unless the specific acquirer situation clearly warrants it; the discipline is in *knowing* the alternative arcs, not in shipping them.
- **Public-CIM excerpt study.** Find (via SEC EDGAR proxy filings that reference the CIM, or via academic case studies) an excerpt from a real published CIM in a similar-shape deal. Compare the voice, the depth, and the risk-disclosure discipline against your draft. Note where the published example is stronger and revise.
