# The Pre-Marketing Document Set — Teaser, CIM, Management Presentation

## Why this matters

A sell-side process is a sales process. The buyer is not shopping for a product — they are shopping for a *thesis* about how owning your company creates value for them, backed by evidence that the thesis is defensible. Three documents carry that thesis and evidence into the market, in a defined order, at defined thresholds of confidentiality:

1. **The teaser** — the 1–2 page non-confidential document that goes to buyers on first outreach. Its job is to get the acquirer to sign an NDA.
2. **The CIM (Confidential Information Memorandum)** — the 30–60 page confidential document that goes to buyers after they have signed the NDA. Its job is to move them from "curious" to "willing to send a written indication of interest."
3. **The management presentation** — the 40–80 slide deck used by the management team in-person (or over video) with buyers who have submitted qualifying indications. Its job is to build enough conviction for the buyer to commit real diligence resources and eventually submit a bid.

These three artefacts are not templates to fill in. They are the *authored narrative* of your company — the case you want the acquirer to take back to their CEO, their board, their investment committee. The quality of the narrative is often the single biggest lever on the final price beyond the fundamentals of the business itself. A great business with a bad narrative gets under-bid; a good business with a great narrative gets bid up; a bad business with a great narrative gets found out in diligence and either walks or reprices.

This chapter is about *authoring* the three, not about templating them — with specific attention to the discipline that keeps the narrative consistent across all three, and consistent with the data room being built in parallel under mod-105.

## The narrative arc

Before drafting any of the three documents, install the *narrative arc* — the 3–5 sentence story about your company that every document, every conversation, every management presentation will lean into.

The arc has a defined structure:

1. **The market you are in and why it matters.** One-to-two sentences on the category, its size, its growth, its structural dynamic.
2. **The problem you solve and why it is hard.** One-to-two sentences on the specific customer problem, why prior approaches fell short, what the market is missing.
3. **How you solve it and why you are uniquely positioned.** Two-to-three sentences on your approach, your differentiation, your moat, your traction.
4. **Why now is the right time.** One-to-two sentences on the market inflection, the technology inflection, or the competitive-window dynamic that makes the current moment the right one.
5. **What owning this company enables that owning nothing does not.** One-to-two sentences on the strategic thesis for a buyer — what they can do with you that they cannot do without you.

The arc is the invariant. The teaser is the arc at 200 words. The CIM is the arc at 15,000 words with supporting evidence. The management presentation is the arc delivered in-person over 90 minutes with the management team's conviction and answers-to-hard-questions. Each expresses the arc at a different resolution; none contradict it; all evolve as market feedback comes in during the process (chapter 6 depth).

The arc should be written down, agreed by the founder-CEO, CFO, board chair (if applicable), and lead banker before any of the three documents is drafted. Every subsequent drafting choice references back to it.

## The teaser

**Purpose:** to get the acquirer past the initial "should I take this seriously?" gate and to the point where they will sign an NDA to learn more. It is a *marketing* document, and it is *non-confidential* — assume it will be forwarded internally within the acquirer, shared with advisors, and possibly leaked. Draft accordingly.

**Length:** 1–2 pages, sometimes as short as a single page. Longer than 2 pages is a red flag that you are trying to close the deal in the teaser rather than open the conversation.

**Structure (roughly):**
- **Header line:** anonymous ("Project [codename]" — a defined banker-side codename that is not identifying), the size dimension (revenue range, growth-rate range, employee-count range), the sector one-liner.
- **Business description:** 2–4 sentences on what the company does, at a level of specificity that a strategic acquirer in the space will recognise but that does not identify the company to a casual reader.
- **Key metrics:** revenue (range or exact), growth rate, gross margin band, EBITDA / operating profit band if favourable, headcount, customer count if favourable.
- **Investment highlights:** 4–6 bullet points on why this is an interesting asset. These are the arc's third and fifth pillars (differentiation + strategic thesis for a buyer), compressed.
- **Transaction context:** one line on the process (typically "the company has retained [banker] to explore a sale of the company" or equivalent, with a timeline reference).
- **Contact:** the banker's contact information (never yours directly — the banker is the gatekeeper for NDA execution).

**What to include, what to leave out:**
- Include enough to make the acquirer want to sign the NDA. Financial highlights that are strong enough to matter, market-position language that resonates, differentiation that is specific.
- Leave out anything that would identify the company to a mildly-motivated reader (specific customer names, distinctive product names, distinctive geographic details).
- Leave out anything that would embarrass you if it were forwarded publicly (aspirational metrics that outstrip reality, category-defining claims that a competitor would immediately dispute).

**The anonymity trade-off:** the teaser is nominally anonymous but the anonymity is often thin — a well-informed reader in your category can usually figure out which company it is from the size, growth, and description. The anonymity is doing two jobs: (1) making it plausibly deniable that you are running a process (helpful if you want to preserve optionality and not have the market know until you are ready to signal), and (2) filtering out casual readers who would not sign an NDA. Neither job is done perfectly, and both jobs matter less than the pre-marketing choreography that decides who receives the teaser and when (chapter 6 depth).

**Failure modes:**
- **Over-specific teaser** that identifies the company on first read. Cure: have a colleague who does not know the deal read it and try to guess.
- **Under-specific teaser** that reads like every other teaser in the category. Cure: name the specific differentiation and the specific market thesis; if the differentiation cannot be named without identifying the company, note that tension and decide.
- **Aspirational-metric teaser** that leans on projected numbers rather than actual ones. Cure: use actuals for the current-period metrics; projections belong in the CIM with the supporting model.

## The CIM (Confidential Information Memorandum)

**Purpose:** to move an acquirer who has signed the NDA from "curious" to "willing to send a written indication of interest at a specific price range." This is the document where the buyer's investment committee (financial acquirer) or business-unit executive team + corp-dev (strategic acquirer) will develop their initial thesis and get their first internal alignment on price.

**Length:** typically 30–60 pages of core content, plus appendices. Longer is not better — a bloated CIM signals a company trying to hide something in the volume; a tight CIM signals a company confident in its story.

**Structure (a modal outline — the specific ordering varies):**

### 1. Executive summary (2–3 pages)

The narrative arc, at full resolution. This is what a busy acquirer executive reads first (and sometimes only). Every subsequent section supports something in the executive summary; nothing appears in the executive summary that is not backed by a later section.

### 2. Investment highlights (2–3 pages)

6–10 numbered highlights, each with a short paragraph. These are the "reasons to buy this company" in a format optimised for internal-summary-deck lifting. Assume the acquirer's corp-dev will copy-paste these bullets into their internal memo.

### 3. Company overview (3–5 pages)

- Founding story and mission (short — 1 paragraph).
- Business model (how you make money).
- Products and services (what you sell).
- Customer segments and channels (who buys and how they buy).
- Geographic footprint.
- Organisation chart at the executive-team level.

### 4. Market opportunity (3–5 pages)

- Market size (TAM / SAM / SOM with methodology footnoted).
- Market growth dynamics and drivers.
- Competitive landscape (with a market map that positions you honestly — a self-serving competitive map is a well-known negative signal to sophisticated buyers).
- Structural trends creating the opportunity now.

**Note on TAM methodology:** every sophisticated M&A buyer will discount the TAM number you present. The way to earn trust here is to show your work — cite the source, show the calculation, and adjust honestly for penetration realities. A defensible $10B TAM presented with methodology beats an aspirational $100B TAM presented as a headline.

### 5. Product and technology (4–6 pages)

- Product architecture and capabilities.
- Technology stack and defensible technology assets (patents, proprietary data, unique algorithmic approaches).
- Product roadmap (12–24 months, at a level of detail that gives the buyer conviction without giving away the crown jewels).
- Any technology-integration story if relevant to strategic buyers.

### 6. Go-to-market (3–5 pages)

- Sales motion (sales-led, product-led, hybrid).
- Sales team structure and productivity.
- Customer-acquisition channels and unit economics.
- Customer-success motion, retention, expansion metrics.

### 7. Customers (3–5 pages)

- Customer count, segmentation, concentration.
- Customer-retention metrics (NRR / GRR / churn).
- Marquee customer references (named where permission allows; anonymised with descriptors where not).
- Customer-testimonial excerpts (subject to permission).

**Customer concentration:** buyers scrutinise concentration carefully. Present the top-10 or top-20 customer concentration honestly; note the diversification trend if favourable; note the customer-relationship depth (contract length, expansion, relationship tenure) that mitigates concentration risk.

### 8. Team (2–3 pages)

- Executive team bios (founders, key executives, board).
- Total headcount and functional composition.
- Retention metrics.
- Compensation philosophy at a high level.

### 9. Financial overview (5–8 pages plus appendices)

- Three-statement financials for the last 3 years and current year-to-date. Historical financials are audited (or reviewed) where possible; auditor identity noted.
- Key metrics dashboard (ARR / revenue by segment, growth rates, cohort retention, gross margin, operating expenses by function, EBITDA / operating profit / cash burn).
- Financial projections for the next 3–5 years, with underlying assumptions clearly stated. Projections are the buyer's next-step conversation; be defensible but ambitious.
- Working capital, cash position, debt.
- Any one-time or non-recurring items called out.

**The bottom-up cohort model matters more than the top-down TAM chart.** Sophisticated buyers value businesses on cohort economics — the ARR-per-cohort curve, the retention curve, the CAC-payback curve. If your CIM's financial section leans on a top-down TAM chart and glosses the cohort economics, sophisticated buyers will read the omission as either "they do not know their numbers" or "the numbers do not support the thesis."

### 10. Growth strategy (3–5 pages)

- Product-expansion opportunities.
- Market-expansion opportunities (geography, segment, adjacent categories).
- M&A opportunities the target might pursue (relevant for buyout PE that will fund add-ons).
- Strategic-partnership opportunities.
- Key risks and mitigations (do not leave this out — sophisticated buyers appreciate honest risk disclosure and distrust CIMs that omit it).

### 11. Transaction context (1–2 pages)

- Process outline (timeline, next steps, key dates).
- Contact information for the banker team.
- Any specific transaction structure preferences the seller is signalling.

### Appendices (variable length)

- Detailed financial model.
- Customer-cohort analysis.
- Product-screenshots or technical deep-dives.
- Detailed market-research studies.
- Any specific supporting material for the highlights in section 2.

## The management presentation

**Purpose:** to give the buyer's decision-makers a direct sense of the management team, deepen the narrative, and answer the hard questions in-person (or over video). This is where the story lives in three dimensions — with the founder-CEO, CFO, and (where relevant) other executives making the case in real time. The management presentation is often the moment a buyer moves from "we should submit an indicative bid" to "we will submit a competitive final bid" — or the opposite.

**Length:** typically 40–80 slides delivered over 90 minutes to 3 hours. The slides are the scaffolding; the management team's conviction, answers, and rapport are what actually matter.

**Attendees on the buyer side:** for a strategic acquirer, the business-unit executive (SVP / EVP / GM), the corp-dev lead, sometimes the CFO or CEO of the acquirer, sometimes a product / engineering leader. For a financial acquirer, the deal team's senior partner, one or two investment professionals, sometimes an operating partner.

**Attendees on the seller side:** founder-CEO always. CFO always (or Head of Finance if pre-CFO). Sometimes the CTO, VP of Sales, or GM of the primary business unit for questions in their area. The banker attends but should not speak more than briefly except in the transaction-context section — the buyer is buying the *management team*, not the banker.

**Structure (a modal outline):**

### 1. Introductions (5 minutes)

Everyone in the room introduces themselves. Set the tone as substantive, not ceremonial.

### 2. Business overview and market position (15–20 minutes)

The narrative arc, delivered by the founder-CEO. Focus on why the market is what it is, what your company does that others do not, and why the moment is right. This is where the founder-CEO's specific conviction about the market shows.

### 3. Product and technology (15–20 minutes)

Delivered by the founder-CEO or (if the founder is not the technical lead) the CTO or head of product. Product demonstrations where feasible; architectural walkthroughs for platform-oriented acquirers.

### 4. Go-to-market and customers (15–20 minutes)

Delivered by the founder-CEO or the VP of Sales / Head of Revenue. Cover sales motion, customer acquisition, retention, and expansion. This is often where sophisticated buyers press hardest — expect questions on individual cohort behaviour, sales-team productivity, and customer concentration.

### 5. Financial overview (20–30 minutes)

Delivered by the CFO. Walk through the historical financials, the current run-rate, the projections, and the underlying assumptions. Buyer questions here are often the most technical and the most predictive of the eventual bid — a strong CFO performance in this section can move a bid up materially.

### 6. Growth strategy and value creation (15–20 minutes)

Delivered by the founder-CEO with CFO support. This is where the buyer starts thinking about *what they would do with the company* — the discussion often shifts into an implicit joint-planning conversation, which is a good signal.

### 7. Q&A and open discussion (30–60 minutes)

The most important section. The buyer will ask the specific questions their investment committee will require answers to. This is where the management team's ability to answer honestly and specifically — including "we do not know, here is how we would find out" — determines whether the buyer's internal conviction builds.

**Preparation:**
- Rehearse the presentation in full at least twice. The banker's associates typically drive rehearsal Q&A that mimics buyer objections; take those rehearsals seriously.
- Anticipate the 20 hardest questions and have crisp answers rehearsed. The banker's team will draft an FAQ; expand it with founder-CEO / CFO judgement.
- Coordinate the choreography — who takes which section, who handles which question, when the founder-CEO defers to the CFO and vice versa.

## Narrative consistency across the three artefacts

The single most damaging pattern in a sell-side is *narrative drift* between the three documents (and between the documents and the data room). Buyers read across all four; corp-dev associates specifically cross-check. Drift produces distrust; distrust produces price haircuts or process exits.

**Specific consistency disciplines:**

- **The narrative arc is invariant.** Every artefact expresses the same arc at a different resolution. If a bullet in the CIM contradicts a bullet in the management presentation, one of them is wrong.
- **Metrics are consistent.** ARR in the teaser matches ARR in the CIM matches ARR in the management presentation matches ARR in the data room's financial model. Definitions are the same across all four (what counts as ARR, how gross margin is calculated, what is included in operating expenses). If a metric is calculated differently in different artefacts, one is wrong.
- **Projections are consistent.** The projections in the CIM match the projections in the management presentation and are supported by the model in the data room. If the CIM shows 45% growth next year and the management presentation shows 50%, the discrepancy will surface in Q&A and cost you credibility.
- **Customer references and testimonials are consistent.** Named customers in the teaser are named customers in the CIM and are available for reference calls from the data room's customer-list section. Not naming a customer publicly but named privately in the data room is fine; naming a customer publicly and then not having them available for a reference call is a red flag.
- **Risk disclosure is consistent.** Risks named in the CIM are addressed in the management presentation. Risks that appear only in the data-room's risk-register but are omitted from the CIM are a diligence-time surprise that will produce reprice-or-walk pressure.

The banker's associates typically own a cross-reference matrix — the ARR, growth-rate, gross-margin, customer-count, and headcount numbers in each artefact are tracked in a shared workbook, with any drift flagged for resolution. This is unglamorous work; it is the difference between a professional-quality sell-side and a home-brewed one.

## Coordinating with the data room (mod-105)

The data room (mod-105 depth) is being built in parallel with the CIM. The two must be internally consistent.

- **Financial model in the CIM = financial model in the data room.** The buyer's Q-of-E will reconcile against this.
- **Customer list in the CIM = customer list in the data room.** Any redactions in the data-room customer list are for legitimate confidentiality reasons and are noted; the CIM does not name customers who are not in the data-room list.
- **Product architecture in the CIM = product architecture in the data-room's technology folder.** Deep-diligence questions will pull on the data-room material; the CIM should not oversell what the technology folder actually supports.
- **Risk factors in the CIM = risk register in the data room.** Litigation, IP, employment, and regulatory risks in the data room match the risk disclosure in the CIM.

mod-105 develops the data-room construction discipline in depth. For this chapter, the key coordination pattern is: the CIM author and the data-room lead are in a weekly (minimum) sync during the pre-marketing period, converging on a consistent narrative and consistent numbers. Drift between the two is the first thing sophisticated buyers detect and the last thing you want to explain in Q&A.

## Personalisation without divergence

For the top-tier acquirers (chapter 3's top 5–8), personalise the CIM and management presentation to their specific strategic thesis — an appendix in the CIM that speaks to their category priorities, a section in the management presentation that leans into how ownership benefits them specifically. This is legitimate and welcomed.

What is not welcomed: *different core narratives* to different acquirers. If acquirer A gets a CIM that positions the company as a data-integration platform and acquirer B gets one that positions it as an application-layer product, the two acquirers will eventually compare notes (they always do, especially when they end up in competitive rounds), and the credibility hit is severe.

The rule: **one narrative arc, one CIM core, one management-presentation core, with acquirer-specific appendices and acquirer-specific talking points**. Personalise the emphasis, not the story.

## Failure modes

Six failure modes recur in the pre-marketing document set:

- **Marketing-department CIM.** A CIM that reads like a customer-facing marketing brochure — heavy on brand voice, light on numbers. Sophisticated buyers detect this immediately and discount the entire document. Cure: CIM voice is *evidence-and-argument*, not marketing.
- **Aspirational-metric bloat.** Projections that outrun defensible reality by 2–3x. Cure: keep projections ambitious but defensible; the buyer's Q-of-E and diligence will surface the truth anyway, and the surprise is worse than the disappointment.
- **Consistency drift.** As above.
- **Risk-omission surprise.** Leaving out a material risk from the CIM (customer concentration, key-person dependency, litigation exposure, technology-debt) in the hope the buyer will not find it. They always find it. Disclosed risks are negotiable; hidden risks are exit-or-reprice events.
- **Over-personalised documents.** As above.
- **Banker-generic CIM.** A CIM drafted by the banker's associates using their template without deep founder / CFO engagement, producing a document that reads like every other CIM in the sector. Cure: the founder-CEO and CFO must be *authors* of the narrative arc and the core sections, with the banker's team providing the scaffolding and the polish. A CIM the founder-CEO cannot defend in Q&A is a bad CIM.

## Summary

The teaser, CIM, and management presentation carry the sell-side narrative into the market at three different resolutions and confidentiality thresholds. All three are expressions of a single invariant narrative arc that must be authored and agreed before drafting begins. Metrics, projections, customer references, and risk disclosure are consistent across all three and consistent with the data room being built under mod-105. Personalisation for top-tier acquirers is welcomed at the appendix / talking-point level; different core narratives to different acquirers is not. The banker's team provides scaffolding and polish; the founder-CEO and CFO are the authors of the narrative and the defenders of it in Q&A. A great narrative on a good business gets bid up; drift and bloat get discounted. Chapter 6 turns to the pre-marketing choreography that decides who receives these documents, when, and against what timeline — the discipline that surrounds the artefacts and turns them into a live competitive process.
