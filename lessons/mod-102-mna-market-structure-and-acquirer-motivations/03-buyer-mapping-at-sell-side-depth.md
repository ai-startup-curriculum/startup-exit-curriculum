# Buyer Mapping at Sell-Side Depth

## Why this matters

mod-101 chapter 3 installed the rolling buyer map as an ongoing-company discipline — a warm-relationship investment kept alive for years so acquisition remains a live endgame option. That version of the map is right for the board's quarterly review of *endgame optionality*. It is not, on its own, ready to drive an actual sell-side process.

The gap between the endgame-framing map and a sell-side-execution map is not the shape (both use the same six axes) but the *depth* at which each score is defensible. On a sell-side, every acquirer on the shortlist is a real allocation of CFO / CEO / banker time and a real seat in a management-presentation calendar. Every score has to be *defensible against interrogation* — by your board, by your banker, by the acquirer's corp-dev when they eventually see the process behind the process. A map with lazy scoring at endgame-framing depth is fine; the same map at sell-side depth is malpractice.

This chapter pushes the map from the frame depth to the execution depth. It uses the same six-dimension scoring, but each dimension gets a sub-diligence — the specific artefacts and evidence trail that back the score. It also introduces the *ranked-tier* discipline (top-tier / second-tier / long-tail) that turns a flat map into a runnable process, and it names the failure modes that separate a working sell-side buyer map from a slide deck.

## The six dimensions, revisited at sell-side depth

The six dimensions from mod-101 are the right scaffolding. At sell-side depth, each dimension gets an evidence base.

### 1. Relationship depth (0–4)

At endgame framing, this axis measures how warm the relationship is; at sell-side depth, it also measures whether that warmth is *actionable* — can you get the meeting you need in the window you have?

**The 0–4 scale, sell-side-execution version:**

- **0 — Cold.** No prior contact. Requires a cold intro from a banker, investor, or advisor, and there is real risk that the target does not respond in the process window.
- **1 — Aware.** Prior contact exists, but there is no specific relationship owner on your side, no recent conversation, and no clear counterpart on their side. The intro is warmable but not warm.
- **2 — Warm.** A specific counterpart on their side (corp-dev lead, BU exec, or CEO) has taken a real meeting with you in the last 24 months. You can get a first meeting scheduled within 2–4 weeks of an outreach.
- **3 — Engaged.** Recurring cadence — periodic strategic conversations, product-integration discussions, technical collaboration, or a joint-customer motion. A meeting request produces a same-week or next-week meeting. Both sides recognise the other as a known quantity.
- **4 — Deep.** Multi-year, multi-layer relationship. Executive-to-executive contact is regular. A well-framed sell-side conversation gets a same-week response and a proximate decision-forward meeting.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**
- Named counterpart on their side with title and last-contact date within the last 12 months.
- Named owner on your side who can produce the meeting.
- History of at least two substantive conversations in the last 24 months (for scores 2+).
- Evidence of ongoing engagement (product collaboration, joint customers, executive touch cadence) for scores 3+.

**Sell-side implication:** relationship-depth ≥2 is the practical minimum for a top-tier shortlist entry, because a cold-outreach acquirer in a process with a 4–8 week window will (typically) not clear their internal alignment fast enough to be a real bidder. This does not mean cold acquirers are out of the map; it means they are candidates for pre-marketing groundwork (see chapter 6) that raises them to 2 before the formal process opens.

### 2. Strategic fit (1–5)

At endgame framing, this axis is the plausibility of a strategic thesis. At sell-side depth, it is a *documented* thesis anchored in public evidence.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**
- Cite the specific evidence: a specific earnings-call transcript excerpt (with date), an investor-day slide (with URL), a public M&A action in the category (with 8-K link), a public product announcement.
- Name which of the four strategic sub-types from chapter 1 this fit represents (adjacency, platform, defensive, talent-consolidating).
- Name the specific *strategic problem this acquisition would solve for them* — one sentence, in their language, from their point of view.
- Note any counter-evidence — recent divestitures in the category, executive statements de-emphasising the space, capital-return priorities that suggest M&A is de-prioritised.

**Sell-side implication:** the strategic-fit note is what your banker will use in the initial-approach conversation. A vague fit note ("they might be interested in our space") is a script that does not close the meeting. A specific fit note ("their CFO at the Q3 2025 earnings call named category X as their #2 M&A priority, and they have completed two deals in it in the last 18 months") is one that does. Sub-diligence at sell-side depth is what makes the note specific.

### 3. Ability to pay (1–5)

Financial capacity is the ceiling on any conversation. At sell-side depth, this axis is *modelled*, not asserted.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**

For **strategic acquirers (public):**
- Latest cash and short-term investments (10-Q balance sheet, most recent quarter).
- Undrawn revolver capacity if disclosed.
- Debt-capacity headroom relative to their stated leverage targets.
- Stock-currency value at latest price and 30-day VWAP (relevant for stock consideration).
- Recent M&A commitments (announced-but-not-closed deals that consume capacity).

For **strategic acquirers (private):**
- Public estimates of their private-market valuation and cash on hand.
- Recent capital-raise history and stated deployment priorities.
- Note that private-target ability-to-pay is genuinely harder to estimate; be honest about the uncertainty and lean on banker network / press coverage.

For **financial acquirers:**
- Fund size, vintage, and stated deployment pace.
- Estimated remaining dry powder (fund size minus deployed capital across the vintage's portfolio).
- Typical check size for the fund (from prior deals).
- Time remaining in the fund's active investment period (typically 5 years of a 10-year vehicle).
- Any recent large deployments that would reduce their appetite for another check.

**Sell-side implication:** a strategic with $500M in cash and a $1B undrawn revolver can support a $1B–$1.5B all-cash deal; the same acquirer will be at their comfort limit around $500M all-cash. A financial acquirer whose fund is 90% deployed and in year 8 is a walking mirage on the buyer map — they may show interest in early conversations but will not close because their fund is out of gas. Modelling ability-to-pay is what separates a working buyer shortlist from a wishlist.

### 4. Integration appetite (1–5)

At sell-side depth this axis is about *what will happen to your product, brand, and team post-close* — because that shapes both what price the acquirer can justify (autonomous-run buyers often pay higher headlines) and what retention terms and employee outcomes look like.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**
- Look at the acquirer's last 5–10 deals: which products still exist? Which brands still exist? Which teams stayed intact? What was the modal integration timeline?
- Where possible, talk to founders and executives who have gone through the acquirer's integration. LinkedIn is a first-cut search; personal-network intros are the substance.
- Note the acquirer's stated integration philosophy (published if they are large-cap; folkloric if not).

**Sell-side implication:** integration appetite has a direct effect on both *price* and *retention*. Autonomous-run buyers (rare but real — Alphabet in some categories, Berkshire Hathaway as a broader-market pattern) can pay higher because they underwrite less synergy realisation risk; aggressive-integrate buyers (Salesforce historically as a modal reference; many platform-extending strategics) pay more disciplined multiples but move faster to realise synergy. Retention terms are correspondingly shaped: at an autonomous-run buyer the target's leadership may stay in place for years; at an aggressive-integrate buyer, the 12–24 month retention window is often followed by absorption or departure.

### 5. Process behaviour

At endgame framing, this axis is folklore. At sell-side depth, it is *categorised folklore with a cited source for each claim*.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**
- Talk to bankers who have transacted opposite this acquirer in the last 2–3 years. What was the pace? How many rounds of diligence? Did they raise on final round or hold price? What was their reputation for closing what they signed?
- Talk to founders who sold to this acquirer, or came close and did not. What was their experience? Where in the process did surprises happen?
- Look at public reporting on past deals — announcement-to-close windows are on 8-K filings; regulatory-review timing is public; deal-termination and re-negotiation events are usually reported.
- Note the corp-dev leadership on the buyer side — how long have they been in place, who are they, what is their track record.

**Categorisation:**
- **Speed:** fast (weeks from LOI to signing) / medium (2–3 months) / slow (4+ months).
- **Diligence style:** light (leans on public information plus targeted diligence) / standard / heavy (extensive Q-of-E, deep commercial diligence, exhaustive systems review).
- **Certainty of close:** high (rarely walks after LOI) / medium / low (has walked or repriced late in past deals).
- **Behaviour under competitive pressure:** raises to compete / holds price and hopes for best-of-breed / drops out.
- **Post-signing behaviour:** honours signed deals with clean execution / renegotiates on diligence findings / pursues aggressive earn-out interpretation post-close.

**Sell-side implication:** process behaviour shapes almost every tactical decision — how much to invest in early-round outreach, whether to include this acquirer in a final-round tender or run them in parallel, how much runway to build into the timeline. Ignoring process-behaviour intelligence produces process choices that surprise you six weeks in.

### 6. Cultural fit (1–5)

At endgame framing, cultural fit is a scorable proxy. At sell-side depth, it is a specific *diligence workstream* on the target-acquirer's post-close track record.

**Sub-diligence to back the score:**
- Interview 3–5 employees who have moved from the target-acquirer to elsewhere in the last 2–3 years (LinkedIn search is the first-cut source; personal intros through investors, bankers, and industry network do the actual conversations). Ask specifically: what was the culture like day-to-day? How were technical decisions made? How were people managed? What surprised you about the acquirer's environment?
- Interview 2–3 founders or executives who joined via acquisition and stayed >2 years. Ask: what was the acquirer like as an employer of an acquired team? Where did the acquirer meet expectations, and where did they not?
- Interview 1–2 founders who joined via acquisition and departed <2 years. Ask: what went wrong? What would you have wanted to know in negotiation?
- Cross-check against Glassdoor patterns (as a signal, not as truth) and against the acquirer's public retention track record on past deals.

**Sell-side implication:** cultural fit determines whether the acquired team stays past the retention cliff and whether the deal produces the value the acquirer paid for. A low-cultural-fit deal is one where the retention pool pays out but the team leaves the day it vests; the value the acquirer thought they were buying evaporates on schedule. For the *seller*, low cultural fit predicts a bad experience for the team post-close — the founder-CEO's fiduciary duty to the team's outcome (not just their equity outcome) is materially informed by this axis.

## Ranked tiers — turning a map into a runnable process

At sell-side depth, a flat buyer map is not enough. You need a tiered shortlist that maps to different process treatments.

### Top tier (5–10 entries)

The acquirers you want in the room in the first-round bidding. Selection criteria:
- Strategic fit ≥4.
- Ability to pay ≥4 at the price band you are targeting.
- Relationship depth ≥2 (or with a credible plan to reach 2 within the pre-marketing window).
- Integration appetite compatible with your team's post-close preferences.
- Cultural fit ≥3.
- Process behaviour compatible with your timing (a slow acquirer in a fast process is a no-go regardless of other axes).

Each top-tier entry gets:
- A named owner who is the primary relationship-holder on your side (typically the CEO or CFO for peer-CEO acquirers, the SVP-Corp-Dev for corp-dev-owned relationships).
- A specific pre-marketing plan for the 12–24 weeks before the formal process opens (chapter 6 depth).
- A dedicated slot in the management-presentation calendar.
- A specific narrative angle in the CIM and management-presentation that emphasises the strategic thesis for *this* acquirer (personalisation without narrative divergence — chapter 5 depth).

### Second tier (10–20 entries)

Acquirers who are plausible bidders but whose fit, capacity, relationship depth, or process behaviour makes them less certain. They enter the process on a slightly delayed schedule (typically after the top tier's first round), or they enter as parallel-track bidders whose bid the process is designed to include but not depend on.

Each second-tier entry gets a lighter version of the top-tier treatment: a named owner, a lighter pre-marketing plan, a shared management-presentation slot (batched with other second-tier acquirers if useful), and the standard CIM.

### Long tail (20+ entries, optional)

Acquirers included primarily to preserve competitive pressure — their presence in the process is what keeps top-tier and second-tier acquirers disciplined on price. Long-tail bidders often do not make it to a final round, but their presence in the first round signals a genuine competitive process and can materially move the top-tier bids.

Long-tail entries get the CIM and a first-round-only outreach; they are not staffed with a dedicated owner and do not get pre-marketing investment.

**Ranking discipline:** the tiering is done *before* the process opens. Once the process is running, moving an acquirer between tiers requires explicit CFO / board-level approval, because tier changes have downstream operational consequences (who gets prioritised on scheduling, who gets which piece of the sell-side team's time).

## The board-visible cadence

At sell-side depth, the map moves from a quarterly board-review item to a *live operating artefact*. Some patterns:

- **Weekly during pre-marketing (roughly 8–16 weeks before formal process launch).** The map is a standing item in the CEO's weekly one-on-one with the CFO and (if applicable) SVP-Corp-Dev / lead banker. Updates: which relationships have advanced, which pre-marketing conversations have happened, which scoring changes have occurred.
- **Bi-weekly during the formal process.** Full sell-side team review — CEO, CFO, banker team lead, external counsel. Updates: which acquirers are progressing to next round, which have dropped, which price signals have surfaced.
- **Board update at each stage gate.** Pre-marketing launch, first-round bid deadline, second-round bid deadline, best-and-final, signing. The board sees the map at each gate with change-since-last-update flagged.

**Fiduciary-duty note:** in a formal sell-side, the board's process (from a Delaware fiduciary-duty standpoint) is materially informed by whether the buyer map was comprehensive, whether the outreach was appropriate, and whether the process created enough competitive tension to satisfy Revlon-standard scrutiny (mod-109 depth). The board's regular sight of the buyer map is not just an operating hygiene item; it is part of the process that produces the fiduciary defensibility of the eventual deal.

## Coordinating the map with the banker

If you have engaged a sell-side banker (chapter 4), the buyer-map ownership becomes a shared responsibility. Some patterns to install early:

- **The banker maintains a parallel buyer map based on their own database and process history.** Their map and yours should converge; the differences are the conversation. The banker will often surface financial-acquirer entries you would not have found, and you will often surface strategic entries the banker missed because they lack industry-specific depth.
- **The banker owns the outreach to acquirers where their existing relationship is stronger than yours.** For most tier-1 and tier-2 boutique bankers, this covers a meaningful fraction of the financial-acquirer set and some subset of the strategics.
- **The founder-CEO / CFO owns the outreach to acquirers where the strategic-thesis narrative requires industry-specific conviction.** The banker can open the door; the founder-CEO has to be the one delivering the strategic pitch.
- **The buyer map becomes the shared reference document for the process-design conversation with the banker.** How many acquirers in the first round? Which get parallel management-presentation slots? Which get sequential? Which get skipped in the first round and included only if the initial round underperforms? All of these are decisions taken against the buyer map.

## Failure modes at sell-side depth

Five specific failure modes recur at sell-side execution depth, above and beyond the endgame-framing failures from mod-101:

- **The wishlist buyer map.** A map full of top-cap acquirers scored optimistically on strategic fit, without evidence sub-diligences to back the scores. Every acquirer looks like a plausible bidder on paper; when the process opens, most of them do not respond, do not schedule management presentations, or drop out after a superficial first meeting. Cause: no evidence sub-diligences. Remedy: force yourself to write the specific-earnings-call-excerpt-with-date citation for every strategic-fit score of 4 or 5; if you cannot, lower the score.
- **The dry-powder mirage.** A financial-acquirer section full of funds whose vintage or deployment status makes them realistically incapable of a check at the size you need. Cause: no fund-vintage / dry-powder modelling. Remedy: for every financial acquirer in the top or second tier, model the check size against fund size, vintage, remaining investment period, and current deployment pace; retire the mirages.
- **The relationship-depth inflation.** Scoring an acquirer as depth-3 based on one polite dinner two years ago and a friendly email from their corp-dev. Cause: wishful thinking under pressure. Remedy: enforce the "named counterpart with contact within the last 12 months" evidence test for scores ≥2.
- **The banker-map hand-off.** Delegating the buyer map to the banker entirely once they are engaged, and losing the industry-specific strategic-fit depth that only the founder-CEO / CFO can carry. Cause: relief at being able to hand something over. Remedy: keep the buyer map as *your* artefact, with the banker's map as an input; converge but do not merge.
- **The cultural-fit skip.** Skipping the cultural-fit dimension because it feels soft or hard to score. Cause: quantitative bias. Remedy: enforce the ex-employee-interview sub-diligence for every top-tier entry; the interviews are hard to schedule but they surface information that changes deal design.

## Summary

The buyer map from mod-101 is the right scaffolding. At sell-side depth, each of the six dimensions gets an evidence sub-diligence that makes the score defensible against interrogation, and the map is organised into ranked tiers (top / second / long-tail) that map to distinct process treatments. Ownership is shared with the banker (if one is engaged) but not delegated. Board cadence moves from quarterly to weekly during pre-marketing and bi-weekly during the formal process. The failure modes at sell-side depth — wishlist scoring, dry-powder mirages, relationship-depth inflation, banker hand-off, cultural-fit skip — are all cured by enforcing the evidence sub-diligences and refusing to score above the evidence. A well-maintained sell-side buyer map is one of the two artefacts (with the CIM) that most determine the eventual deal's quality; the discipline to keep it defensible is the CFO / SVP-Corp-Dev job for the six months before the formal process opens.
