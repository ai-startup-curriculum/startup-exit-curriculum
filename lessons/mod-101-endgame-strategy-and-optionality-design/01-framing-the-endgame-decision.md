# Framing the Endgame Decision — The Five Candidate Outcomes

## Why this matters

Ask a founder at Series A what "success" looks like and you will get one of three answers: (1) "we're going to be a big independent company," (2) "we'll IPO," or (3) a shrug and a change of subject. Ask the same founder at their moment of forced choice — a term sheet on the table, a cash-out runway, an inbound from a strategic — and you will see one of three failure modes: they can only name the option that showed up, they have no vocabulary for comparing it to the ones that did not, and they have no operating history of preserving the alternatives.

Endgame framing is the discipline of naming the full set of futures a company could realise *before* one of them arrives with a deadline attached. It is not a five-year plan. It is a short list of candidate outcomes with the trade-offs made explicit, so that when the moment arrives you are choosing among options you have already reasoned about, not reacting to the one someone else picked for you.

## The five candidate outcomes

The endgame is not "sell or IPO." It is a family of at least five outcomes, and any specific company at any specific stage has some subset of them live. The five, in the order a founder is most likely to underweight them:

### 1. Acquisition

A third party buys the company. Consideration is cash, stock, or a mix; the acquirer is either **strategic** (a company for which owning yours produces synergy — adjacent product, defensive, talent, distribution) or **financial** (a fund whose return model is buying you, operating for a hold period, then selling or IPO'ing you). Cash-certainty rises, control drops to zero at close (or to a defined earn-out window), employees get a retention package designed by the acquirer, and the founder's post-close role is a negotiated term rather than a given.

The acquisition frame is not one thing. Within it sit at least four sub-shapes — aqui-hire (buyer wants the team, product may be shelved), tuck-in (buyer folds you into an existing product line), platform (you become the buyer's chassis for a new business area), and transformative-strategic (you are a company-defining bet for the buyer). mod-102 develops the full taxonomy; this chapter names it so the endgame frame does not collapse into "we could get bought."

### 2. IPO

The company registers securities with the SEC and lists on a public exchange — traditional bookbuilt IPO on NYSE or Nasdaq, direct listing, or a de-SPAC combination. Control is preserved to the extent your dual-class or governance choices preserve it, capital access opens, employees can eventually sell their equity, and the founder's job changes shape — new stakeholder set (public investors, sell-side analysts, proxy advisors), new disclosure discipline (10-Q / 10-K / 8-K cadence, Reg FD), new liability surface (Section 11 / 12 / 15, Section 10(b) / Rule 10b-5).

IPO is neither the "final boss" of startup outcomes nor a slam-dunk. It is a specific financing and liquidity mechanism with its own governance graduation cost, its own market-window dependency, and its own post-close constraints on how the company can be operated. mod-107 and mod-108 develop the readiness and execution mechanics; here the point is to install it as one option among several rather than as *the* success archetype.

### 3. Secondary liquidity

Existing shareholders — founders, early employees, early investors — sell some of their shares to new investors without the company being acquired or going public. Founder secondaries in a growth round, employee tender offers, forward-contract structured secondaries, and secondary-market-platform sales (Nasdaq Private Market, Forge, EquityZen, Carta X) are all shapes of this outcome. The *company* remains independent; specific *shareholders* get partial liquidity.

This is not a "mini-exit." It is a legitimate endgame path in its own right, especially for founders whose personal financial situation is a live constraint on how boldly they can operate the company. It also interacts with every other endgame: a well-designed secondary reduces founder pressure to take a bad acquisition; a badly-choreographed one telegraphs founder capitulation to the market. mod-106 develops the mechanics.

### 4. Stay independent

The company remains privately held indefinitely, financing itself from operating cash flow, ongoing private capital, venture debt, or some combination. No liquidity event happens — or it happens on a timeline the founder controls rather than one an acquirer or the public market imposes. Employees hold illiquid equity longer; investors' MOIC is realised only if a later event happens; the founder trades near-term liquidity for continued control and time.

"Stay independent" is often mis-framed as "no endgame" or "we haven't decided yet." It is neither. It is a decision — one that costs employee liquidity and investor DPI, and one that has to be defended to the same board that is often expecting one of the other three. Companies that stay independent successfully do so because the founder-CEO has explicitly chosen it and built a board that will support it, not because they drifted into it.

### 5. Responsible shutdown

The company winds down. This is not the same as "failure" — a company that has raised venture capital and cannot produce a venture-scale return has a duty to return remaining capital and dispose of its assets responsibly rather than continue burning until zero. The mechanisms — Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors, Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 11, Section 363 sale-for-parts — carry different economic, legal, and reputational consequences. WARN Act obligations, D&O tail, officer personal-liability watches, and IP-disposition decisions all attach. mod-113 develops the mechanics.

Naming shutdown as one of the endgame outcomes is not defeatism. It is the acknowledgment that the responsible way to run a venture-backed company includes the possibility of a responsible close, and the framing you install today shapes whether that outcome — if it arrives — is executed well or executed poorly.

## The rhetorical trap: "success" as a single thing

Founders internalise a mono-culture of success from the ambient press: TechCrunch covers acquisitions and IPOs, tech-Twitter celebrates unicorn markups, and the LP-facing narrative of most VCs privileges the fund-returner outcomes. This produces two failure modes:

1. **The single-outcome frame** — the founder can only reason about the endgame their peer group celebrates (typically IPO), and treats every other outcome as a form of failure. When an acquisition offer arrives, they cannot evaluate it on its merits because it does not fit the frame.
2. **The no-outcome frame** — the founder refuses to name any endgame ("we're just building"), which sounds virtuous but is operationally the same as choosing whatever endgame happens to arrive first. The board vacuum this creates is where activist investor letters and forced-sale pressure grow.

The remedy in both cases is not to pick an outcome earlier — that is its own trap (see the next section). The remedy is to name the *set* of live outcomes for this specific company, keep the set updated, and reason about the operating loop as pointing the company somewhere within that set rather than at a single dot.

## When to install the frame

The earliest stage a founder can afford to. That is deliberately vague, because the answer depends on stage, capital structure, and founder personal situation, but three tests help:

- **The board-conversation test.** If your next board meeting has an item titled "long-range plan" or "5-year targets," the frame belongs on that agenda. If your board has been asking about "exit thinking" and you have been deferring, the frame is already overdue.
- **The financing-terms test.** If you are about to sign a term sheet that carries a covenant, warrant, ratchet, or drag-along coverage rule that constrains a future endgame path, you cannot evaluate that term without the frame in place. mod-101 exists in part so you never sign a term whose endgame consequences you have not modelled. (Chapter 5 goes deep on this.)
- **The buyer-inbound test.** If a strategic acquirer has ever taken a "let's grab coffee" meeting with you, the frame is already active — you just have not written it down. The inbound was a market signal. Write it down before the next one arrives.

For most venture-backed companies, this puts the frame at Series A at the latest and Seed at the ideal. Before then, the frame is still worth having but is more about *ruling out* pre-closing decisions than about ranking outcomes.

## What framing is *not*

Three clarifications, because founders sometimes push back:

- **Framing is not deciding.** Naming the set of live outcomes is different from choosing one. The board conversation you want is "here are the five outcomes, here is what would trigger each, here is how we would preserve each" — not "here is the one we picked." That comes later, if at all.
- **Framing is not committing to a timeline.** Endgame frames are stable across years; the timing question ("act now vs. wait") is a separate call driven by market-window signals (chapter 4) and by the operating machine's readiness.
- **Framing is not a substitute for building.** Every endgame — including responsible shutdown — is better executed from a stronger operating position. The frame *orients* the operating loop; it does not replace it.

## What the frame produces

A framed founder-CEO carries three things into every subsequent decision:

1. **A short list of candidate outcomes** — three to five (all five if the company is at growth stage with real optionality, fewer if the capital structure or stage has closed some off) — held on a single page.
2. **A rank-order with justification** — a live "here's how we'd order these today, and here's why the ranking would flip under X or Y conditions."
3. **An operating-loop overlay** — the specific things the operating machine is doing this quarter that keep the top two or three options alive rather than pre-closing them.

The rest of this module builds those three artefacts. Chapter 2 makes the trade-offs among the outcomes explicit so the ranking has structure. Chapters 3 and 4 install the two intelligence inputs (rolling buyer map, market-window read) that keep the ranking honest as the world changes. ## Summary

The endgame decision is not one thing; it is a choice among at least five — acquisition, IPO, secondary liquidity, stay independent, responsible shutdown. Naming that set explicitly, at the earliest stage a founder can afford to, replaces the two common failure modes (single-outcome tunnel vision, no-outcome drift) with a live, board-visible frame that turns subsequent operating decisions into deliberate points-of-the-compass rather than accidental drift. The rest of this module is about keeping that frame useful — trade-offs, intelligence inputs, upstream operating discipline, and the artefact you write down.
