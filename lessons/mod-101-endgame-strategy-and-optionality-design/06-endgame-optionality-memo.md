# Authoring the Endgame-Optionality Memo

## Why this matters

Chapters 1 through 5 install the frame, the intelligence disciplines, and the operating audit. All of it is only as useful as the artefact that captures it and puts it in front of the people who make decisions. That artefact is the **endgame-optionality memo** — a short, board-facing document that names the candidate endgame paths for a specific company, the trigger conditions that would move the company toward each, and the operating changes needed to preserve or open each path.

A memo that lives is the difference between a founder-CEO who reasons about the endgame quarterly and one who thinks about it once, in a hallway conversation, three years before the moment it matters. The memo is not a slide deck for an investor. It is an internal artefact — the operating charter of the endgame decision — that the founder-CEO owns and the board reviews.

## What the memo is (and what it is not)

The endgame-optionality memo:

- Names the 3–5 candidate endgame outcomes that are live for this specific company today.
- Ranks them explicitly using the six-axis frame from chapter 2.
- Notes the trigger conditions that would flip the ranking.
- Records the current market-window read from chapter 4.
- Summarises the current buyer-map state from chapter 3.
- Names the operating audit findings from chapter 5.
- States the operating changes the company will make this quarter to preserve or open the top-ranked paths.

It is *not*:

- A commitment to a specific outcome. The memo names a ranking; it does not pick an outcome. Picking happens when a specific transaction is on the table.
- A pitch document. It is written for an audience (founder-CEO, board, sometimes CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev) that shares the vocabulary of this module. It is not for investors, not for employees, and not for external audiences.
- A one-time exercise. The memo is *versioned*. First cut typically at Series A or before; refreshed materially at every priced round and at least annually; touched in a light refresh every quarter as part of board-pack preparation.

## The audience and the writing register

The memo's primary audience is the board (executive session), with the founder-CEO as the drafter and the CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev as review partners. The writing register that fits is:

- **Executive-brief tone.** Short paragraphs, bulleted where bullets serve, no filler.
- **First-person plural** for the company's decisions ("we would preserve...").
- **Specific numbers.** ARR, gross margin, cash on hand, cumulative preference stack, top-10 customer concentration — the memo names the current numbers rather than referring to them.
- **Explicit uncertainty markers.** "If X occurs, we would move Y" rather than "Y is likely." Ranking, not prediction.
- **Named sources for external readings.** Market-window signals link to the sources (Meritech, Bessemer, etc.) so future readers can update.

The memo is short. First cut typically 4–8 pages plus one appendix table (the ranking matrix). Subsequent refreshes are shorter — a version log and updates to the sections that changed.

## Structure

A working structure. Adapt to the specific company, but do not skip sections without noting *why*.

### 1. Executive summary (½ page)

Three to five sentences. Names the top-ranked and second-ranked endgame paths, the single most important operating change the company will make this quarter, and the single most consequential trigger that would flip the ranking.

### 2. Company profile (½–1 page)

The frozen snapshot the rest of the memo reasons against.

- Stage (SEED / SERIES-A / GROWTH / MATURE).
- ARR (or MRR × 12), YoY growth rate, net dollar retention, gross margin, cash on hand, runway, burn.
- Cumulative capital raised, current 409A common per-share, last-round preferred per-share.
- Top-10 customer concentration percentage and any single-customer concentration >10% called out by name (redact if sensitive).
- Preference stack: cumulative liquidation preference by tranche, participation / non-participation, cap.
- Team size, key-person notes.

This section is the *shared factual base* for everything downstream. If the numbers are stale, the memo is stale.

### 3. Candidate endgame outcomes (1–2 pages)

For each of the 3–5 candidate outcomes:

- **Outcome name** (e.g., "Strategic acquisition, cash-heavy, mid-tier").
- **One-paragraph description** — what this outcome would look like for *this specific* company.
- **Six-axis score summary** — control / capital / time-to-outcome / employee outcomes / personal-brand / investor-return distribution, with a single-line note per axis where the assessment is not obvious.
- **Precondition list** — the specific conditions under which this outcome becomes achievable. E.g., "IPO becomes live when ARR passes $200M with rule-of-40 above 40 sustained for four quarters."

### 4. Ranking and trigger conditions (½–1 page)

The ranking, with reasoning:

- **1st / 2nd / 3rd / 4th / 5th ranked** with a two-sentence justification each.
- **What would flip 1 → 2** — the specific condition (market-window shift, preference-stack event, competitive-landscape change) that would demote the top outcome to second place.
- **What would flip 2 → 1** — the reverse condition that would promote the current #2.
- **What would remove an outcome from the ranking entirely** — e.g., "responsible shutdown drops out when runway is >24 months at current burn."

This section is where the memo shows its analytic depth. A memo that ranks without stating the flip conditions is a snapshot; a memo that states the flip conditions is a decision tool.

### 5. Market-window read (½ page + inline links)

The current quarter's market-window read (chapter 4's dashboard) in condensed form:

- IPO window: direction call, one-sentence justification.
- M&A market: direction call, one-sentence justification.
- Sector-multiple direction.
- Strategic-buyer appetite: one line about the top-tier buyer segment.
- Any changes since last memo version.

### 6. Buyer-map summary (½ page + appendix)

Current buyer-map state (chapter 3):

- Total entries by category (strategic, financial, market-structure).
- Top-tier by relationship depth (list 5–10 by name, with relationship-depth level).
- Key movements since last version — new entrants, new deep relationships, entries retired.
- Full buyer map is in the appendix or linked separately.

### 7. Operating-decision audit findings (1–2 pages)

Chapter 5's four-surface audit output:

- **Financing** — outstanding facilities, change-of-control terms, covenants, warrant coverage; findings.
- **Cap table** — preference-stack overhang analysis (waterfall at $X / $2X / $3X), ROFR / co-sale / drag-along status, protective provisions summary; findings.
- **Board** — independent-director count and transaction experience, transaction-committee-readiness assessment, board size / cadence; findings.
- **Contracts** — change-of-control clause survey (percent of top-10 customer contract value at risk), MFN inventory, reference-customer relationship risks; findings.

Each surface ends with **findings** — specific items the company should address (renegotiate at renewal, restructure at next round, recruit for board seat, etc.) — with an owner and a target date.

### 8. Operating changes for the coming quarter (½ page)

The commitments. Three to seven items maximum. Each item:

- What we are doing.
- Which endgame path it preserves or opens.
- Owner.
- Target date.

Example:

- Renegotiate change-of-control clause in [Customer X] contract at renewal in Q3. Preserves cash-heavy strategic acquisition path. Owner: VP Sales / GC. Target: 2026-09-30.
- Recruit a second independent director with M&A transaction experience. Preserves transaction-committee readiness. Owner: CEO / Board Chair. Target: 2026-12-31.

### 9. Appendix — six-axis ranking matrix

A single table that condenses the six-axis scoring across the outcomes. This is the artefact that gets referenced by later versions of the memo and by the board when the discussion happens.

## Practitioner canon

The memo's core disciplines are drawn from a small set of practitioner writing that is worth reading directly. This module does not require you to read all of it, but the memo is materially better when the author has spent time in it.

- **Elad Gil — *High Growth Handbook*.** Board chapters (composition, working with your board through hard decisions), the M&A chapter (thinking about acquisition as a founder-CEO), and the general "hire ahead of the curve" pattern all inform how the memo reasons about board readiness and buyer-map maintenance. <https://growth.eladgil.com/>
- **Reid Hoffman — *Blitzscaling* and *Masters of Scale* essays.** The *stages of blitzscaling* framing and the "what does success look like at each stage" pattern feed directly into how the memo reasons about the current stage and the endgame frame's stability across stages. <https://www.mastersofscale.com/> and *Blitzscaling* book.
- **Ben Horowitz — *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, "Should You Sell Your Company?" chapter.** The specific decision-frame for evaluating an acquisition offer — including the *how much of this decision is personal vs. how much is fiduciary* question — is the closest single-chapter treatment of the endgame-decision frame in the practitioner canon. <https://www.benhorowitz.com/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things>
- **Peter Thiel — *Zero to One*, Chapter 14 ("The Founder's Paradox").** The founder's role in the endgame decision, and the way founder-personal-brand considerations sit inside the fiduciary decision, is treated more sharply here than in most sources. <https://zerotoonebook.com/>
- **Brad Feld — *Venture Deals* (with Jason Mendelson) and *Startup Boards* (with Matt Blumberg / Mahendra Ramsinghani).** The mechanics of term sheets, board work, and the founder-board relationship inform the operating-decision audit and the board-composition section of the memo. <https://feld.com/books/>
- **Fred Wilson — AVC blog (ongoing).** Especially the M&A and IPO essays; use as periodic reading to keep the frame honest against practitioner reality. <https://avc.com/>
- **First Round Review — endgame and board essays** as a supplemental source. <https://review.firstround.com/>

The specific *voice* of the memo should be your own. The canon is for the analytic frames, not for the writing style — a memo that reads as if it were assembled from block quotes is worse than a memo that reasons through the frames in the author's own words.

## Worked excerpts

Two short worked excerpts illustrate the register. Adapt to your specific company; do not copy verbatim.

### Excerpt A — Executive summary of an endgame memo (illustrative)

> **Company:** Acme Data (Series C, GROWTH stage). $58M ARR, 92% YoY growth, 128% NRR, 78% gross margin, 21 months runway at current burn.
>
> **Top-ranked endgame path (next 18–24 months):** Strategic acquisition by a data-warehouse or observability platform, in the $600M–$1.2B range, cash-and-stock consideration. Second-ranked: continued independent operation with a $150M primary + secondary raise inside the next 12 months. Third-ranked: dual-track IPO / strategic sale from mod-107 readiness starting in Q1 2027.
>
> **Most important operating change this quarter:** Recruit a second independent director with prior corporate-development / M&A experience — currently zero independent directors on a 7-person board.
>
> **Single most consequential trigger:** A sustained closing of the Bessemer Cloud Index compression window (>15% multiple recovery, sustained 90 days) would flip the ranking to IPO-first.

### Excerpt B — A precondition list for one candidate outcome (illustrative)

> **Outcome — IPO on Nasdaq Global Select Market, traditional bookbuilt, $300–500M primary.**
>
> **Preconditions:**
> - ARR ≥ $200M at time of confidential DRS submission (target Q3 2027).
> - Rule of 40 ≥ 40 sustained four consecutive quarters (currently 38, on track).
> - PCAOB-registered auditor engagement in place by Q2 2026 (currently Big-4 review; needs upgrade to full audit; timing risk noted).
> - SOX ICFR design and testing programme running for 12+ months before submission.
> - Sector-index (Bessemer Cloud Index) trading above compression threshold; recent-cohort proof positive (last 3 sector IPOs above issue price at 6-month mark).
>
> **Current gap-to-preconditions:** ARR gap 3.5x; rule-of-40 gap ~2 points; auditor upgrade decision pending Q4 board; ICFR programme not yet started.

## Common failure modes

Four ways the memo can go wrong.

- **The single-outcome memo.** Ranks acquisition #1 without seriously assessing the other four outcomes; produces a self-fulfilling narrowing of optionality. The remedy is to force yourself to write a full precondition list for each of the five outcomes, even ones you consider unlikely.
- **The unranked memo.** Names the outcomes but refuses to rank them ("all options are open"). This is worse than a wrong ranking — it produces no decision content. Rank explicitly, with reasoning; you can always reorder next quarter.
- **The static memo.** Written once, referenced never. Version-log it. Every board pack should reference the current memo version and note the last update.
- **The memo that hides the audit.** Rich on framing, empty on operating-decision audit. If the memo cannot say "here are the pre-closed options and here is what we are doing about them," it is not doing the job. The audit findings are the deliverable, not the frame.

## The board conversation

The memo exists to enable a specific board conversation. A working choreography:

- **Annual full review** — a 60- to 90-minute board executive session anchored on the current memo. Purpose: align on the ranking and the operating commitments.
- **Quarterly refresh** — a 15–30 minute agenda item in the board meeting anchored on the version diff since last quarter. Purpose: surface changes and validate that the operating commitments are being executed.
- **Event-driven update** — any material inbound (acquisition interest, market-window shift, adjacent-category M&A) triggers a version update within 30 days, circulated to the board with a specific ask for feedback.

The board's role is not to *decide* the endgame; the board's role is to *sanity-check the reasoning* and to *approve the operating commitments*. The founder-CEO's role is to *own the memo* — draft it, defend it, update it. When the memo is high-quality and current, board conversations about the endgame become fast and productive; when it is stale or absent, those conversations become slow, defensive, and often driven by whoever last received an inbound.

## Summary

The endgame-optionality memo is the artefact that makes the frame from chapters 1–5 usable. Structure: executive summary, company profile, candidate outcomes with six-axis scores, ranking with trigger conditions, market-window read, buyer-map summary, operating audit findings, operating commitments, appendix ranking matrix. Written by the founder-CEO, reviewed by CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev, presented to the board annually with quarterly refreshes and event-driven updates. Grounded in the practitioner canon (Gil, Hoffman, Horowitz, Thiel, Feld) but voiced in the author's own words. The memo is short, versioned, and — critically — *actually written down* and *actually kept current*.
