# exercise-05: Endgame-Optionality Memo Authoring

**Estimated effort:** 3 hours

## Objective

Author the endgame-optionality memo for the same company you carried through exercises 01–04. The memo pulls the ranking (exercise-01), buyer map (exercise-02), market-window dashboard (exercise-03), and operating audit (exercise-04) into a single board-facing artefact using the structure in chapter 6. By the end you should have a versioned memo you would be willing to bring to your next board meeting.

This is the capstone deliverable of the module. The paired lab (published separately) then asks you to publish the memo for review; the project (`project-103-endgame-decision-portfolio`) uses it as one input into a broader portfolio.

## Background

This exercise covers material from:

- [Chapter 6 — Authoring the Endgame-Optionality Memo](../06-endgame-optionality-memo.md)

All prior chapters are load-bearing inputs. Do not attempt this exercise without having completed exercises 01–04; the memo is a synthesis, not a re-derivation.

## Prerequisites

- Completed exercises 01, 02, 03, and 04.
- Read chapter 6 for the memo structure and the practitioner canon.
- At least one hour of focused writing time. This memo does not flow well in fragmented sessions.

## Tasks

### 1. Write the memo end-to-end

Follow the chapter 6 structure:

- **Executive summary** (½ page).
- **Company profile** (½–1 page) — reuse and update from exercise-01.
- **Candidate endgame outcomes** (1–2 pages) — reuse and update from exercise-01, one paragraph and one axis-summary per outcome.
- **Ranking and trigger conditions** (½–1 page) — reuse and update from exercise-01, integrating the market-window signal from exercise-03.
- **Market-window read** (½ page + inline links) — condense exercise-03 into a paragraph per section, with links.
- **Buyer-map summary** (½ page + appendix) — top entries from exercise-02, key movements, appendix or link to full map.
- **Operating-decision audit findings** (1–2 pages) — condense exercise-04's four-surface findings.
- **Operating changes for the coming quarter** (½ page) — the 5–12-item punch-list from exercise-04, refined to 3–7 items that this quarter's operating loop will actually deliver.
- **Appendix — six-axis ranking matrix.**

Total length: 4–8 pages for the memo body, plus appendix.

### 2. Write in the executive-brief register

Deliberately match the writing register from chapter 6:

- Short paragraphs, bullets where bullets serve.
- First-person plural for company decisions.
- Specific numbers, not qualitative hand-waves.
- Explicit uncertainty markers ("if X occurs, we would move Y") rather than predictions.
- Named sources for external readings with inline links.

Read the memo aloud once when you think you are done. Any sentence that sounds like fluff, filler, or hedging should be cut or made specific.

### 3. Set the version metadata

At the top of the memo, capture:

- **Version.** v0.1 for a first cut; v1.0 when you would defend it in front of the board; subsequent quarters as v1.1, v1.2, etc.
- **Date.** The date of this version.
- **Owner.** The founder-CEO by default.
- **Reviewers.** CFO / GC / SVP-Corp-Dev names and dates of last review.
- **Next scheduled refresh.** Quarterly is the default cadence.

### 4. Version-log the changes

At the end of the memo, add a version-log section:

- v0.1 — first draft; open items: [list].
- v1.0 — [what changed from v0.1, what was resolved].
- Subsequent versions in the same format.

For your first cut, the version log lists what you know is incomplete or uncertain in the current draft. This becomes the punch-list for the next revision.

### 5. Peer- or advisor-review pass (recommended)

Have one person outside your immediate role — a board member, an outside advisor, a peer founder-CEO, or a corporate-development leader you trust — read the memo and try to break it. Note:

- Which specific claims did they push back on?
- Which sections did they find weakest?
- What questions would they ask if this memo were on the board-pack table?

Incorporate their feedback into v1.0.

### 6. Plan the board conversation

Draft (½ page, at the bottom of the memo or as a separate note) how you would present the memo in a board executive session:

- Framing sentence — the one thing you want the board to leave the meeting agreeing on.
- Two or three specific questions on which you want board input (typically around ranking flip conditions or the operating punch-list items that require board support, e.g., independent-director recruitment).
- The specific *ask* — approval of the operating commitments, agreement on the ranking, sanction for a specific action.

## Starter guidance

Four anti-patterns that break memos:

- **The pitch-deck disguise.** A memo written as if it were a pitch deck for investors — glossy, marketing-oriented, hedged — is not a decision tool. Write for the board, not for the outside world.
- **The framework-tour.** A memo that walks the reader through the frame from chapter 1 to chapter 5 as if introducing it for the first time misses the point. The frame is the *scaffold*; the memo's content is the specific ranking, the specific readings, and the specific commitments for *this* company. Assume the reader (the board) shares the vocabulary; get to the substance.
- **The unranked memo.** Naming outcomes without ranking is not "keeping options open" — it is refusing to do the analytic work. Rank explicitly.
- **The static memo.** A memo that is written once and never updated is worse than no memo — it produces a false sense of currency. Commit to the refresh cadence and the version log at the start, and treat v0.1 as the *first* version of many, not the final answer.

## Acceptance criteria

You can demonstrate that:

- The memo covers all nine sections from the chapter-6 structure (exec summary, profile, candidate outcomes, ranking + triggers, market-window, buyer map, audit findings, operating commitments, appendix).
- Total length is 4–8 pages plus appendix.
- The register matches an executive-brief tone: short paragraphs, specific numbers, first-person plural, explicit uncertainty.
- The ranking is explicit (numbered 1–5 or 1–3 for the truly-live outcomes), each with two-to-three-sentence justification.
- Every external reading in the market-window section links to a primary source with a date.
- The operating-commitments section has 3–7 items, each with a specific action, an owner, and a target date.
- Version, date, owner, reviewers, and next-refresh cadence are captured at the top.
- The version log at the end names what is open in this version.
- A board member reading it could ask a challenging question and see the evidence you used to answer.

## Reflection

Add a short reflection (½ page):

1. Which section of the memo was hardest to write, and what did that tell you about a gap in the underlying analysis?
2. Which trigger condition, if it arrived tomorrow, would you feel least prepared for — and what would you add to next quarter's punch-list as a result?
3. If you were reviewing this memo as a director, what one section would you ask the founder-CEO to strengthen before circulation?

## Stretch goals

- **Two-version comparison.** Write both v0.1 (raw synthesis) and v1.0 (post-review) and diff them. The diff often surfaces what the review actually improved.
- **Present the memo.** Set up a 30-minute presentation of the memo to a peer group (founders, mentors, or an advisory board). Note the questions asked; those become inputs to the next refresh.
- **Adapt for a different endgame frame.** Rewrite the memo for the same company under a *different* top-ranked endgame path — e.g., you originally ranked acquisition #1; rewrite as if IPO is #1. What operating commitments change? This exercise sharpens the sense of which decisions are actually endgame-contingent vs. which are stage-general.
- **Board-pack integration.** Add the memo (or a two-page summary) as a standing item in your next board pack. Observe whether the board conversation changes.
